Who It Is

Awonawilona is the supreme creator deity of the Zuni people — a dual-gendered, bisexual being who existed alone in the infinite void before creation. The Zuni name is often translated as 'All-Container' or 'Maker and Container of All.' According to Zuni cosmology recorded by anthropologist Frank Hamilton Cushing in the late 19th century, Awonawilona existed in the great dark void and, through the power of thought and desire, transformed itself into the sun, bringing light to the cosmos. From the sun were born Earth Mother (Awitelin Tsta) and Sky Father (Uchtsiti), who in turn produced all life. Awonawilona's dual-gendered nature is fundamental to Zuni metaphysics — creation requires both male and female principles, but they need not be separate beings; they can coexist in a single divine person. This sophisticated conception of divinity predates and parallels similar ideas in world religion, including the Hindu Ardhanarishvara (Shiva as half-male, half-female) and certain Gnostic conceptions of the primal androgyne. Awonawilona is so transcendent that the Zuni do not depict him/her in art, do not build temples specifically for him/her, and address him/her only indirectly through the intermediary gods and kachinas. The name itself is considered sacred and is spoken with reverence. Awonawilona represents the Zuni recognition that the source of existence must contain all possibilities — including all genders — within itself.

The Approach

Before there were worlds, waters, or even light, Zuni storytellers say that only one presence existed—Awonawilona, the ‘All Container,’ a being so encompassing that everything that would ever be was already held within. From that solitary awareness came a thought that pushed outward into the dark, thickening into mist, then water, then a blazing sun whose light turned green scum on a primordial sea into Earth and Sky themselves. In this cosmology, creation is not a distant act by a human-shaped god but a slow unfolding of light and water from a consciousness that becomes the sun, births the world, and then steps back, letting twin war gods and womb-like worlds deliver humanity into daylight. Awonawilona is less a character than a horizon—an origin that never quite leaves the story, even when the myths move on to mountains, masks, and the crowded caves where people first learned to feel their way toward the light.

Content advisory. This dossier engages with sacred narratives from the Zuni tradition as mediated through colonial-era ethnography and modern scholarship. Readers should be aware that these accounts may reflect outsider perspectives and that Zuni communities maintain their own protocols for sharing and protecting religious knowledge. The myths themselves do not center on graphic violence or sexual exploitation, but the historical context includes religious persecution and cultural appropriation.

Etymology

Attested English glosses render the Zuni name as “All Container” or “Maker and Container of All,” emphasizing a cosmological role as the encompassing source of existence rather than a narrowly anthropomorphic deity. The form Áwonawílona in Cushing’s transcription reflects late-19th-century conventions for Zuni phonology; detailed internal Zuni etymological analysis is not provided in the primary sources and remains uncertain.

Cultural Ownership

Awonawilona belongs to the religious and cosmological tradition of the Zuni (A:shiwi) people of western New Mexico, a Pueblo community with a strong emphasis on oral transmission and restricted ritual knowledge. Zuni religious authorities maintain control over detailed ceremonial narratives; much of what is available in print comes from outsider ethnographers of the late 19th and early 20th centuries and later synthetic mythological surveys.

Disputed Identifications

Scholarly and popular literature sometimes loosely equates Awonawilona with generic ‘creator gods’ or solar deities in other Indigenous North American traditions, and occasionally aligns Awonawilona with pan-Pueblo figures such as Taiowa (Hopi) or with dual-gender creator deities like Mawu-Lisa (Fon/Ewe) in comparative mythology. These are analogical comparisons, not Zuni identifications. Within Zuni myth itself, there is a tension between Awonawilona as an abstract, encompassing principle and Awonawilona as the Sun Father after self-transformation; some sources treat ‘Sun Father’ as a distinct deity created by Awonawilona, while Cushing’s text presents Awonawilona as becoming the Sun Father. The deity’s gender is explicitly fluid or unspecified in several modern summaries, contrasting with Cushing’s repeated use of ‘All-father’ and masculine pronouns, which reflects his own linguistic and cultural framing rather than a clear Zuni doctrinal statement.

Description

Appearance

Attested descriptions of Awonawilona in English-language sources emphasize the deity’s abstract, cosmological nature rather than a fixed anthropomorphic form. In Cushing’s narrative, Áwonawílona initially exists as a formless, all-encompassing presence in a void of darkness and desolation. Through ‘innate knowledge’ and thought, this being evolves mists and steams and then ‘made himself in person and form of the Sun,’ becoming the Sun Father. This implies a transformation from an unfigured principle into a luminous, solar body. Later summaries describe Awonawilona as either male or female, or as beyond gender, and do not specify detailed bodily features, clothing, or ornaments. When Awonawilona is equated with the Sun Father, the deity is implicitly associated with the visible sun disk and its radiance, but the mythic texts focus more on cosmological actions than on visual iconography. Thus, Awonawilona’s appearance is best characterized as attested in two modes: (1) an unfigured, encompassing presence; and (2) a solar embodiment as the Sun Father, without further physical detail.

Symbols & Iconography

Awonawilona’s primary symbol is the sun itself, particularly in the form of the Sun Father into whom the deity transforms. The creation narrative links Awonawilona’s solar embodiment with the brightening of space, the thickening of mist-clouds, and the evolution of the world-holding sea. The deity is also conceptually associated with water in all its configurations—mists, steams, clouds, seas—since Awonawilona creates and becomes these forms at the beginning of the world. Later Zuni ritual practice, as described by Stevenson and others, features masks and kachina figures that embody ancestral and divine powers, including solar and sky beings, but specific ritual iconography directly labeled as Awonawilona is not clearly documented in the cited sources. Symbolically, Awonawilona stands at the intersection of light and water, sun and sea, and is linked to the layered structure of the universe and the four-fold womb of the world. These associations are attested; detailed visual motifs (colors, specific mask designs, etc.) remain largely undocumented in accessible sources and should be treated as unknown.

Behaviour & Personality

Awonawilona is portrayed as a self-knowing, self-transforming, and generative principle. In Cushing’s account, the deity ‘conceived within himself and thought outward in space,’ suggesting introspective cognition and deliberate creation. Awonawilona’s behavior is characterized by orderly, purposeful cosmogenesis: evolving mists, becoming the Sun, generating waters, and forming Earth Mother and Sky Father from green scum on the sea. In Benedict’s emergence tale, Awonawilona is the Sun and creator who takes pity on the people crowded in darkness and acts through his sons to bring them into the daylight world, indicating compassion and concern for proper ritual relations (offerings and prayer sticks). Modern summaries emphasize Awonawilona as the ‘essence of life’ and ‘Supreme life-giving power,’ suggesting a personality that is less about individual temperament and more about fundamental vitality and balance. There is no attested depiction of capriciousness or moral alignment in the sense of good/evil; Awonawilona operates above human moral categories, focused on sustaining the cosmos and enabling life.

Domains & Habitats

Awonawilona’s domain is the totality of the cosmos. Before creation, the deity alone has being in a vast, dark, desolate space. Through creation, Awonawilona becomes associated with the Sun, the world-holding sea, and the layered universe. Cushing’s narrative describes the evolution of the sea, the formation of Earth Mother and Sky Father, and the conception of terrestrial life in the four-fold womb of the world. The universe is structured into multiple layers or worlds, including underworlds and the Daylight World, with mountains, rivers, and habitats for animals, birds, and trees. Awonawilona’s habitat is not a localized heaven or mountain but the entire cosmic structure, especially the solar sphere and the generative waters. Modern summaries note that the universe consists of nine layers, with the first as earth and the remaining eight as homes for various beings; Awonawilona presides over this layered cosmos as its origin and sustaining power.

Version Differences

Several important version differences are attested:

1. **Pre-existence of fog and steam**: Some modern summaries state that Awonawilona creates and becomes all configurations of water, including fog and steam, while other versions say fog and steam already existed before Awonawilona. This reflects divergent narrative emphases on absolute creation versus ordering of pre-existing elements.

2. **Relationship to the Sun Father**: In Cushing’s account, Áwonawílona ‘made himself in person and form of the Sun whom we hold to be our father,’ effectively identifying Awonawilona with the Sun Father. In other retellings, Awonawilona ‘chose to embody the sun and created the deity the Sun-father,’ implying a distinction between the abstract creator and a subsequent solar deity. Benedict’s emergence tale treats Awonawilona as the Sun and creator, aligning more with Cushing’s identification.

3. **Gender**: Cushing’s language (‘All-father Father’) and masculine pronouns suggest a male framing, while modern reference works explicitly note that Awonawilona’s gender is not specified in myth and that the deity may be referred to as either he or she. This indicates a shift from ethnographer-imposed gender to recognition of gender fluidity or transcendence.

4. **Structure of the universe**: Cushing’s text emphasizes the four-fold womb of the world and multiple cave-worlds (Underworld, Moss World, Mud World, Wing World) leading to the Daylight World. Later summaries mention nine layers of the universe, with earth as the first and eight additional layers as homes for animals, birds, and trees. These may represent different Zuni narrative strands or interpretive syntheses.

5. **Role in human emergence**: In Cushing’s narrative, the Twin War Gods (Twain Deliverers) are born from the sea and deliver men and creatures from the underworlds, acting under the broader cosmological framework established by Awonawilona. In Benedict’s version and modern summaries, Awonawilona more directly sends the twin gods of war to bring people into the light and to establish ritual relations (offerings and prayers). The degree of direct involvement varies by version.

Powers & Abilities

Cosmic creation and containment

Source Version Cushing’s Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths; Gray et al., Mythology of All Races; Oxford Reference entry.

Description

Awonawilona is explicitly described as the ‘Maker and Container of All’ and ‘All Container,’ indicating the power to originate, encompass, and sustain the entire cosmos. This includes the ability to conceive creation within the self, to think outward into space, and to evolve mists, steams, waters, and the Sun. The deity’s containment is not merely spatial but ontological: all beings and worlds arise within and are held by Awonawilona.

Limitations

No explicit limitations are stated in the myths; however, Awonawilona delegates specific functions to other beings (Earth Mother, Sky Father, Twin War Gods), suggesting a distributed cosmological order rather than direct micromanagement of all events.

Contradictions

Some comparative mythological treatments may downplay the ‘container’ aspect and treat Awonawilona as a more conventional high god, but primary sources emphasize encompassing containment as central.

Self-transformation into the Sun Father

Source Version Cushing’s ‘The genesis of the worlds, or the beginning of newness’; Benedict’s emergence tale (Awonawilona as Sun and creator).

Description

Awonawilona conceives mists and steams and then ‘made himself in person and form of the Sun,’ becoming the Sun Father. This power of self-transformation establishes the deity as the source of light and solar vitality. Through this transformation, Awonawilona brightens the spaces, thickens mist-clouds, and initiates the evolution of the world-holding sea.

Limitations

The transformation is presented as a singular, foundational act rather than a repeated or reversible ability; the myths do not describe Awonawilona shifting back and forth between forms in later narrative episodes.

Contradictions

Later summaries sometimes treat the Sun Father as a distinct deity created by Awonawilona rather than as Awonawilona’s own transformed state, creating a tension between identity and distinction.

Generation of waters and world-holding sea

Source Version Cushing’s creation narrative; modern summaries of Awonawilona’s role in forming all configurations of water.

Description

By thinking outward and becoming the Sun, Awonawilona causes mist-clouds to thicken and fall, evolving ‘water in water’ and the world-holding sea. The deity’s power encompasses the creation and configuration of all forms of water—mists, steams, clouds, seas—which serve as the medium for further creation.

Limitations

Some versions suggest that fog and steam pre-exist Awonawilona, which would limit the deity’s role to ordering and transforming rather than absolute creation of these elements.

Contradictions

The pre-existence versus creation of fog and steam is explicitly noted as a version difference in modern reference works.

Formation of Earth Mother and Sky Father from green scum

Source Version Cushing’s ‘The genesis of the worlds’; summarized in Gray et al. and modern encyclopedic entries.

Description

From the Sun Father’s substance of flesh drawn from his person, seed-stuff of twain worlds is formed and impregnates the great waters. In the heat of his light, the waters grow green and scums rise upon them, waxing wide and weighty until they become Earth Mother (Áwitelin Tsíta, ‘Four-fold Containing Mother-earth’) and Sky Father (Ápoyan Tä'chu, ‘All-covering Father-sky’). Awonawilona, as Sun Father, thus possesses the power to generate major cosmic beings from the interaction of light and water.

Limitations

Once Earth Mother and Sky Father are formed, they act with considerable autonomy, conceiving terrestrial life and organizing the world; Awonawilona’s direct creative role shifts to a more foundational, background function.

Contradictions

No major contradictions in the basic outline, though later retellings may simplify or omit the detailed imagery of green scum and seed-stuff.

Conception of terrestrial life in the four-fold womb of the world

Source Version Cushing’s ‘The genesis of men and the creatures’; Gray et al. synthesis.

Description

From the lying together of Earth Mother and Sky Father upon the great world-waters, terrestrial life—men and creatures—is conceived in the four-fold womb of the world. While Earth Mother and Sky Father are the immediate parents, Awonawilona’s prior actions (becoming the Sun, forming waters, generating these beings) make the deity the ultimate source of terrestrial life.

Limitations

The myths attribute gestation and birth to Earth Mother and Sky Father; Awonawilona’s role is more remote at this stage, emphasizing cosmic hierarchy.

Contradictions

None significant; later summaries may attribute creation of humans more directly to Awonawilona, compressing the multi-step process.

Sending or empowering the Twin War Gods (Twain Deliverers)

Source Version Cushing’s Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths; Benedict’s The Emergence; modern reference summaries.

Description

In Cushing’s narrative, the Twin Children of the Sun (Ko'wituma and Wats'usi) are endowed with sacred knowledge, caps, bows, arrows, and shields to have dominion over men and creatures and to deliver them from the underworlds. In Benedict’s emergence tale and modern summaries, Awonawilona (as Sun and creator) sends the twin gods of war (Ahayuta) to bring people from the womb-like darkness into the light and to establish ritual relations (offerings and prayer sticks). Awonawilona thus exercises power through delegated agents, shaping human emergence and social order.

Limitations

The twins act with their own agency and sacred equipment; Awonawilona’s involvement is primarily commissioning and empowering rather than direct physical intervention.

Contradictions

Different versions name the twins differently (Ko'wituma and Wats'usi vs. Ahayuta) and vary in how explicitly Awonawilona is said to send them, reflecting diverse narrative traditions.

Essence of life and vegetative growth

Source Version Modern encyclopedic entries and secondary mythological surveys drawing on Cushing and later ethnography.

Description

Modern summaries note that the combination of light, clouds, and air created by Awonawilona allows for vegetation and makes Awonawilona the ‘essence of life.’ This power is less a discrete miracle than a continuous function: Awonawilona’s solar and atmospheric actions sustain plant growth and, by extension, all living beings.

Limitations

The myths do not describe specific interventions in agriculture or plant species; the association is cosmological rather than agrarian in detail.

Contradictions

None major; some sources may emphasize Earth Mother’s role in vegetation more strongly, creating a nuanced division of responsibilities.

Stories & Myths

The genesis of the worlds, or the beginning of newness

Source Frank Hamilton Cushing, Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths, Thirteenth Annual Report of the Bureau of Ethnology, 1896.Culture Zuni (A:shiwi)Period Recorded 1879–1881; published 1896 (reflecting late-19th-century Zuni oral tradition as mediated by an outsider ethnographer).

Summary

Before the beginning of new-making, Áwonawílona alone has being in a vast darkness and desolation. Conceiving within himself and thinking outward in space, he evolves mists and steams potent of growth. By innate knowledge, he makes himself in person and form of the Sun, the Sun Father, whose appearance brightens the spaces. The mist-clouds thicken and fall, evolving water and the world-holding sea. From the Sun Father’s substance, seed-stuff of twain worlds impregnates the waters; in the heat of his light, the waters grow green and scums rise, becoming Earth Mother (Áwitelin Tsíta) and Sky Father (Ápoyan Tä'chu). From their lying together upon the waters, terrestrial life is conceived in the four-fold womb of the world. This story establishes Awonawilona as the sole pre-existent being, the self-transforming Sun, and the ultimate source of Earth, Sky, and life.

Participants

Awonawilona / Áwonawílona

Sun Father (Yatoka, identified with Awonawilona in this version)

Earth Mother (Áwitelin Tsíta)

Sky Father (Ápoyan Tä'chu)

Terrestrial beings (men and creatures, collectively)

Consequences

The cosmos is created, with light, waters, Earth Mother, Sky Father, and the four-fold womb of the world. This sets the stage for the genesis of men and creatures, the birth of the Twin Deliverers, and the emergence of humans into the Daylight World. Awonawilona’s status as ultimate creator and container of all is established, shaping Zuni cosmology and ritual organization.

Variants

Later summaries emphasize nine layers of the universe rather than the four-fold womb and multiple cave-worlds. Some versions treat the Sun Father as a distinct deity created by Awonawilona rather than as Awonawilona’s transformed state. There are also variations regarding whether fog and steam pre-exist or are created by Awonawilona.

The genesis of men and the creatures

Source Frank Hamilton Cushing, Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths, 1896.Culture ZuniPeriod Recorded 1879–1881; published 1896.

Summary

From the lying together of Earth Mother and Sky Father upon the great world-waters, terrestrial life—men and creatures—is conceived in the four-fold womb of the world. Earth Mother, foreboding for her first-born, withholds birth and seeks counsel with Sky Father on how their children will know places by the light of the Sun Father. Through their changeable, mask-like manifestations, they envision terraced mountains and world-countries that will differentiate space. Eventually, the foremost of men and creatures are brought forth from the earth, and the Twain Deliverers (Twin War Gods) are born from the sea. These twins, endowed with sacred knowledge and weapons, deliver men and creatures through successive cave-worlds (Underworld, Moss World, Mud World, Wing World) into the Daylight World, where they learn the ways of life and seek the Sun Father.

Participants

Awonawilona / Sun Father (background, ultimate source)

Earth Mother (Áwitelin Tsíta)

Sky Father (Ápoyan Tä'chu)

Twin War Gods / Twain Deliverers (Ko'wituma and Wats'usi)

Men and creatures (collective humanity and animal life)

Consequences

Humans and creatures emerge into the Daylight World, acquire knowledge, clothing, and social organization, and establish relations with the Sun Father and other divine beings. The layered structure of the universe and the role of the Twin War Gods in mediating emergence become central to Zuni ritual and mythic identity.

Variants

Names and attributes of the twins vary (Ko'wituma and Wats'usi vs. Ahayuta). Some versions emphasize Awonawilona’s direct commissioning of the twins to bring people into the light and to secure offerings and prayers. The number and names of cave-worlds and the details of human transformation (from scaly, webbed beings to fully human) differ among tellings.

The Emergence (people brought from the fourth world to the daylight world)

Source Ruth Benedict, The Emergence and Other Kachina Tales.Culture Zuni (as recorded by Benedict)Period Early 20th century (fieldwork and publication).

Summary

People dwell crowded together in total darkness in the fourth world, deep in the earth. The daylight world above has hills and streams but no people to live there or to present prayer sticks to Awonawilona, the Sun and creator. Awonawilona takes pity on the people, and his two sons are stirred to lead them to the daylight world. The sons, with human features, locate the opening to the fourth world in the southwest and pass through progressively dimming first, second, and third worlds before reaching the overcrowded fourth world. The people, blinded by darkness, identify the brothers by touch and call them bow priests. The brothers lead the people upward, eventually bringing them into the daylight world, where they can live properly and offer prayers and ritual objects to Awonawilona.

Participants

Awonawilona (Sun and creator)

Two sons of Awonawilona (twin brothers, bow priests, war gods)

People of the fourth world (proto-humans)

Spirits and beings of the intermediate worlds (implicit)

Consequences

Humans are relocated from a cramped, dark existence to the daylight world, establishing a proper ritual relationship with Awonawilona through offerings and prayer sticks. The story reinforces Awonawilona’s compassion and the importance of ritual reciprocity.

Variants

Modern summaries identify the twin sons as Ahayuta, the twin gods of war. Some versions emphasize that Awonawilona sends the twins specifically to bring forth people who will give him offerings and prayers, highlighting the ritual dimension. Details of the intermediate worlds and the people’s physical condition vary.

Poshaiyankya and the four-fold womb of Awitelin Tsita

Source Modern summaries based on Cushing and later ethnographic retellings.Culture Zuni (as mediated through English-language scholarship)Period Late 19th–20th century.

Summary

In one version of the Zuni creation story, the creature Poshaiyankya escapes from the four-fold womb of Awitelin Tsita (Earth Mother) and convinces the Sun Father, Awonawilona, to send the Ahayuta, the twin gods of war, to bring the rest of the womb dwellers into the light. The twins act as deliverers, guiding beings from the enclosed womb-world into the open daylight world. In other versions, Awonawilona himself sends the twin gods of war to bring forth people who will give him offerings and prayers, emphasizing the deity’s desire for ritual reciprocity.

Ahayuta (twin gods of war)

Womb dwellers (proto-humans and creatures)

Consequences

The escape of Poshaiyankya and the subsequent mission of the twin gods of war result in the emergence of beings from the womb-world into the daylight world, reinforcing the motif of deliverance and the establishment of ritual relations with Awonawilona.

Variants

Some versions foreground Poshaiyankya’s role as instigator; others omit this figure and focus on Awonawilona’s direct commissioning of the twins. The naming and characterization of the twins vary, and the emphasis on offerings and prayers differs among tellings.

Colonial ethnography and extraction of sacred narratives

Source Cushing, Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths; eHRAF World Cultures abstract noting the basis of Zuni cultural elements in these myths and the context of Spanish-Zuni history and missions.

Description

The primary textual sources for Awonawilona—especially Cushing’s Outlines of Zuñi Creation Myths—were produced by non-Zuni ethnographers during a period of intense colonial pressure and missionary activity. Cushing lived among the Zuni and recorded extensive mythic material, but his work involved translating and publishing narratives that, in Zuni tradition, are embedded in ritual secrecy and community control. This raises ethical concerns about the extraction and public dissemination of sacred knowledge without full community consent, and about the framing of Zuni cosmology through Euro-American categories (e.g., ‘All-father,’ ‘mythology’).

Scholarly Context

Anthropological scholarship has increasingly critiqued early ethnography for its role in colonial knowledge production. The eHRAF abstract notes that Cushing’s document includes an outline of Spanish-Zuni history and missions, highlighting acculturation and culture contact. Modern Zuni leaders have restricted access to certain ceremonies (e.g., Shalako) and religious knowledge, reflecting a response to earlier exposure and misrepresentation.

Religious persecution and suppression of Zuni practices

Source General discussions of Zuni religion and history in Zuni mythology overviews and ethnographic works (e.g., Zuni mythology entries, Bonvillain’s The Zuni).

Description

Zuni religion has historically faced persecution and suppression from non-Native authorities, including missionaries and government agents. The integration of Roman Catholicism into Zuni religious life occurred under conditions of pressure and coercion. While Awonawilona as a mythic figure is not directly depicted as suffering or inflicting violence, the religious system in which Awonawilona is central has been subject to external attempts to eradicate or replace Indigenous beliefs.

Scholarly Context

Scholars of Native American religions have documented patterns of missionary campaigns, boarding schools, and legal restrictions that targeted Indigenous ceremonies and cosmologies. Zuni responses include selective openness and closure of rituals, emphasizing cultural survival and control over sacred narratives.

Distortion and simplification of Awonawilona in popular mythological surveys

Source Mythology of All Races; Oxford Reference; various online encyclopedic entries.

Description

Popular mythological handbooks and internet summaries often reduce Awonawilona to a generic ‘creator god’ or ‘solar god,’ sometimes imposing gender binaries or moral alignments (e.g., ‘neutral’ or ‘good’) that do not reflect Zuni categories. These simplifications can obscure the complex cosmological role of Awonawilona as an encompassing container, self-transforming Sun, and source of layered worlds, and may contribute to a sanitized or homogenized view of Indigenous cosmologies.

Scholarly Context

Critical Indigenous studies and religious studies scholars have highlighted the risks of decontextualizing Native cosmologies in global mythological compendia. The emphasis on cross-cultural comparison can overshadow specific historical and ritual contexts, leading to misrepresentation.

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity of Awonawilona and Sun Father

Position A

Awonawilona becomes the Sun Father, making them effectively the same being in different modes.

Position A — evidence

Cushing’s narrative explicitly states that Áwonawílona ‘made himself in person and form of the Sun whom we hold to be our father,’ indicating self-transformation rather than creation of another being.

Position B

Awonawilona creates and embodies the sun but also creates a distinct deity, the Sun Father.

Position B — evidence

Modern summaries and some reference works describe Awonawilona as choosing to embody the sun and creating the deity Sun Father, implying a creator–created relationship.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally acknowledge that Cushing’s account identifies Awonawilona with the Sun Father, but later retellings sometimes separate them for clarity or to align with broader patterns of high god plus solar deity. There is no single consensus; careful work distinguishes between Cushing’s identification and later interpretive distinctions.

Gender of Awonawilona

Position A

Awonawilona is male (‘All-father Father’) and referred to with masculine pronouns.

Position A — evidence

Cushing repeatedly uses ‘All-father Father’ and masculine pronouns in his English rendering, reflecting his own linguistic and cultural framing.

Position B

Awonawilona’s gender is unspecified or fluid; the deity may be referred to as he or she and transcends binary gender.

Position B — evidence

Modern reference works explicitly note that Awonawilona’s gender is not specified in myth and that the deity may be referred to as either he or she, emphasizing gender ambiguity or transcendence.

Scholarly Consensus

Contemporary scholarship tends to treat Awonawilona as beyond fixed gender categories, recognizing that Cushing’s masculine language reflects his perspective rather than definitive Zuni doctrine. The deity is often described as dual-gendered or gender-transcendent in comparative mythology.

Pre-existence of fog and steam

Position A

Awonawilona creates and becomes all configurations of water, including fog and steam.

Position A — evidence

Modern summaries based on Cushing and other sources state that Awonawilona created and became all configurations of water, leading to fog, clouds, large bodies of water, and air.

Position B

Fog and steam already exist before Awonawilona; the deity orders or transforms them rather than creating them.

Position B — evidence

Other versions, noted in reference works, explicitly state that fog and steam were already existent before Awonawilona.

Scholarly Consensus

This contradiction is acknowledged as a version difference rather than a doctrinal dispute. Scholars generally present both possibilities, noting that oral traditions often contain multiple strands with varying emphases on absolute creation versus ordering of pre-existing elements.

Structure of the universe (four-fold womb vs. nine layers)

Position A

The universe is structured around the four-fold womb of the world and multiple cave-worlds leading to the Daylight World.

Position A — evidence

Cushing’s detailed narrative describes the four-fold womb of the world and successive cave-worlds (Underworld, Moss World, Mud World, Wing World) through which men and creatures ascend.

Position B

The universe consists of nine layers, with earth as the first and eight additional layers as homes for animals, birds, and trees.

Position B — evidence

Modern summaries and some reference works mention nine layers of the universe, with the first as earth and the remaining eight as habitats for various beings.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars recognize that Zuni cosmology includes multiple models and that different narratives emphasize different structures. The four-fold womb and cave-worlds are well-attested in Cushing; the nine-layer model appears in later syntheses. Rather than harmonizing them, careful work treats them as coexisting or context-specific cosmological schemas.

Degree of Awonawilona’s direct involvement in human emergence

Position A

Awonawilona acts primarily through delegated agents (Twin War Gods), with Earth Mother and Sky Father handling gestation and birth.

Position A — evidence

Cushing’s narrative focuses on Earth Mother, Sky Father, and the Twin War Gods as the immediate actors in human emergence, with Awonawilona’s role more foundational.

Position B

Awonawilona directly sends the twin gods of war to bring people into the light and to secure offerings and prayers, emphasizing a more personal involvement.

Position B — evidence

Benedict’s emergence tale and modern summaries explicitly state that Awonawilona sends his two sons or the twin gods of war to lead people to the daylight world and to establish ritual relations.

Scholarly Consensus

Scholars generally accept that both perspectives exist in the tradition: one emphasizing cosmological delegation and another highlighting Awonawilona’s compassionate decision to send helpers. The difference is seen as a matter of narrative focus rather than a fundamental doctrinal conflict.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Several significant gaps remain in this dossier:

1. **Direct Zuni-authored accounts**: The primary narratives about Awonawilona available in English are mediated through non-Zuni ethnographers (Cushing, Benedict, Stevenson) and later reference works. Contemporary Zuni-authored theological or ritual texts, if they exist, are not widely accessible and may be intentionally restricted. This limits the ability to present an internal Zuni perspective on Awonawilona.

2. **Language and etymology**: Detailed linguistic analysis of the name Áwonawílona within Zuni (phonology, morphology, semantic range) is not provided in the cited sources. The gloss ‘All Container’ is attested, but deeper etymological breakdown remains uncertain without specialized Zuni linguistic studies.

3. **Ritual iconography and practice**: While Stevenson and others describe Zuni masks and ancestral gods, specific ritual representations of Awonawilona (if any) are not clearly documented in accessible sources. Information on how Awonawilona is invoked, represented, or embodied in contemporary Zuni ceremonies is limited, partly due to community privacy and restrictions.

4. **Internal debates and contemporary reinterpretations**: The contradiction register here relies on differences among published outsider accounts and reference works. Internal Zuni debates or contemporary reinterpretations of Awonawilona’s role, gender, and cosmological function are not documented in the sources consulted.

5. **Reception in modern media**: Beyond scholarly and reference works, there is little clear evidence of Awonawilona appearing as a character in mainstream films, games, or popular fiction. Any such appearances would require careful verification to avoid conflating homonyms or creative inventions with Zuni tradition.

6. **Chronological layering of myths**: The precise historical development of different versions (Cushing’s four-fold womb and cave-worlds vs. nine-layer universe models; Poshaiyankya variants) is not fully traceable with the available sources. Oral tradition’s fluidity makes it difficult to assign strict chronological order to these variants.
