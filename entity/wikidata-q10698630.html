Who It Is

Imagine a god so vast and remote that he is the sky itself — not the blue dome above, but the entire, star-strewn cosmos, the source of all power, the silent author of everything. This is Tirawa, the supreme being of the Pawnee people. He is not a god who walks among mortals or answers every prayer. He is the ultimate architect, the one who set the Sun and Moon in their courses, who gave the Morning Star his terrible power, and who handed down the sacred ceremonies that keep the world in balance. To understand Tirawa is to understand a cosmos where power is delegated, where the stars are not distant lights but living beings, and where the fate of humanity is tied to the precise performance of ancient rituals. He is the 'Father Above,' a figure of awe, distance, and ultimate authority — a cosmic CEO who created the universe and then trusted his children to run it.

Tirawa is a remote, benevolent, but largely disengaged creator figure. He set the cosmos in motion, delegated authority to the celestial bodies and the lesser spirits, and established the sacred ceremonies for the Pawnee to maintain harmony with the cosmos. He is not a personal, interfering deity in daily affairs, but rather the ultimate source of divine power and order. He is a figure of veneration and awe, not of intimate personal relationship.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Atius Tirawa' is from the Pawnee (Skiri/Pawnee) language. 'Tirawa' is often translated as 'The Sky' or 'The Heavens', and 'Atius' is often translated as 'Father' or 'The One Above'. Combined, the name is typically rendered as 'Father Above' or 'The Sky Father'.

Pronunciation — AH-tee-us TEE-rah-wah

Aliases — Tirawa, Atius Tirawa, Tirawa Atius, The One Above, The Great Spirit, The Creator

Ownership — Pawnee (Skiri and South Band) people, Native American, Great Plains, present-day Nebraska and Kansas

Powers
Creation of the Cosmos

Tirawa is the ultimate creator. He created the heavens, the earth, and all that is in them. In some accounts, he tasked the Sun and the Moon with the actual creation of the earth and its inhabitants, while he remained in the highest heaven.

Limit: He is often seen as a 'deus otiosus' — a high god who, after creation, withdrew from direct involvement in the world.

Delegation of Authority

Tirawa is the source of all power and authority in the universe. He delegated power to the celestial bodies (Sun, Moon, Morning Star, Evening Star), the natural forces (Thunder, Wind, Rain), and the animal spirits. These delegated powers are the active forces in the world.

Limit: His delegation is absolute; he does not typically intervene personally.

Giver of Sacred Ceremonies

Tirawa is the ultimate source of the sacred ceremonies, which were revealed to the Pawnee through his celestial intermediaries. The Hako ceremony, a great peace and fertility ritual, was given to the Pawnee by Tirawa's children, the Morning Star and the Evening Star, to ensure the well-being of the people and the continuity of life.

Limit: The ceremonies are the only means by which the Pawnee can interact with the divine order.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tirawa is not typically described in anthropomorphic terms in the surviving records. He is more often conceived as an abstract, all-pervading presence associated with the sky, the heavens, and the cosmos. He is the 'power above' that manifests through the celestial bodies, particularly the Sun (Shakuru) and the Moon (Pah), and through the forces of nature like thunder, wind, and rain. He has no fixed iconography.

Symbols — The sky itself, the stars (particularly the morning star and the evening star, which are his children or manifestations in some accounts), the Sun and the Moon, the four directions, the sacred pole used in the Hako ceremony. The number four (for the four directions) and the number seven (for the seven stars of the Pleiades, or the seven Pawnee villages) are also sacred and associated with his cosmic order.

Domains — The sky, the cosmos, the heavens, the upper world, the source of all power, creation, cosmic order, the ultimate authority. He is the supreme being of the Pawnee pantheon.

Accounts I
The Creation of the WorldPawnee (Skiri) tradition, recorded by George A. Dorsey.

Pre-reservation, oral tradition.

In the beginning, there was only Tirawa above. He created the Sun (Shakuru) and the Moon (Pah) and gave them power. He then created the Morning Star (Opirikats) and the Evening Star (Wiruk). He tasked the Sun and Moon with creating the earth and its inhabitants. The Sun and Moon, along with the other stars, worked together to create the land, the waters, the plants, the animals, and finally, the first humans. Tirawa gave the people the sacred bundles and the ceremonies to maintain their connection to him and the celestial order. The creation of the world and the establishment of the sacred order that governs Pawnee life.

Accounts II
The Origin of the Hako CeremonyPawnee tradition, recorded by James R. Murie and Alice C. Fletcher.

Pre-reservation, oral tradition.

The Hako ceremony, a great ritual of peace, kinship, and fertility, was given to the Pawnee by Tirawa's children. In some accounts, the Morning Star and the Evening Star, as children of Tirawa, brought the ceremony to the people. The ceremony involves a sacred pole, which represents the cosmos and the connection between the people and the celestial powers. It is a prayer for the well-being of children, the fertility of the earth, and the unity of the people. Establishment of the Hako as a central ritual for social harmony and cosmic renewal.

The Morning Star SacrificePawnee tradition, recorded by George A. Dorsey and other ethnographers.

Pre-reservation, oral tradition.

A complex and controversial ritual. The Morning Star, one of Tirawa's children, was believed to have demanded a human sacrifice in exchange for his power and the continuation of the cosmic cycle. A captive (usually a young woman) was sacrificed to the Morning Star in a highly structured ceremony. This practice was suppressed in the 19th century. The story of the Morning Star's demand is interpreted in various ways: as a literal command, as a symbolic cosmic necessity, or as a later corruption of the tradition. A highly controversial and violent ritual, which has been the subject of much scholarly and popular debate. It highlights the dark, demanding aspects of the Pawnee cosmos, even in relation to the supreme creator's children.

Tirawa is the same as the generic 'Great Spirit' of all Plains Indians. — While Tirawa is a supreme being, he is specific to the Pawnee. Other Plains nations have their own distinct creator figures with different names, attributes, and myths. The generic 'Great Spirit' is a pan-Indian concept that was popularized in the 19th century and does not reflect the specific theology of any single tribe.

Tirawa is a personal, interventionist god who answers prayers directly. — Tirawa is a remote, transcendent deity. He is the ultimate source of power, but he delegates authority to the celestial bodies and lesser spirits. The Pawnee interacted with these intermediaries through ceremonies, rather than expecting direct, personal intervention from Tirawa.

The Pawnee religion was 'primitive' or 'simple'. — The Pawnee had a highly complex cosmology, with a sophisticated understanding of the stars, a detailed pantheon, and a rich ceremonial life. The Hako ceremony, for example, is one of the most complex and beautiful rituals ever recorded in North America.
