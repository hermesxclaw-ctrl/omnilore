Form

Tirawa is not typically described in anthropomorphic terms in the surviving records. He is more often conceived as an abstract, all-pervading presence associated with the sky, the heavens, and the cosmos. He is the 'power above' that manifests through the celestial bodies, particularly the Sun (Shakuru) and the Moon (Pah), and through the forces of nature like thunder, wind, and rain. He has no fixed iconography.

Powers

Creation of the Cosmos Tirawa is the ultimate creator. He created the heavens, the earth, and all that is in them. In some accounts, he tasked the Sun and the Moon with the actual creation of the earth and its inhabitants, while he remained in the highest heaven. Pawnee (Skiri) creation narrative, recorded by Dorsey. He is often seen as a 'deus otiosus' — a high god who, after creation, withdrew from direct involvement in the world. Some narratives imply he directly created the world, while others say he delegated it to his children, the Sun and Moon.

Delegation of Authority Tirawa is the source of all power and authority in the universe. He delegated power to the celestial bodies (Sun, Moon, Morning Star, Evening Star), the natural forces (Thunder, Wind, Rain), and the animal spirits. These delegated powers are the active forces in the world. Pawnee (Skiri) cosmology, recorded by Murie and Dorsey. His delegation is absolute; he does not typically intervene personally. None recorded.

Giver of Sacred Ceremonies Tirawa is the ultimate source of the sacred ceremonies, which were revealed to the Pawnee through his celestial intermediaries. The Hako ceremony, a great peace and fertility ritual, was given to the Pawnee by Tirawa's children, the Morning Star and the Evening Star, to ensure the well-being of the people and the continuity of life. Pawnee Hako ceremony, recorded by Murie and Fletcher. The ceremonies are the only means by which the Pawnee can interact with the divine order. None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

Tirawa is a remote, benevolent, but largely disengaged creator figure. He set the cosmos in motion, delegated authority to the celestial bodies and the lesser spirits, and established the sacred ceremonies for the Pawnee to maintain harmony with the cosmos. He is not a personal, interfering deity in daily affairs, but rather the ultimate source of divine power and order. He is a figure of veneration and awe, not of intimate personal relationship.

Accounts

In the beginning, there was only Tirawa above. He created the Sun (Shakuru) and the Moon (Pah) and gave them power. He then created the Morning Star (Opirikats) and the Evening Star (Wiruk). He tasked the Sun and Moon with creating the earth and its inhabitants. The Sun and Moon, along with the other stars, worked together to create the land, the waters, the plants, the animals, and finally, the first humans. Tirawa gave the people the sacred bundles and the ceremonies to maintain their connection to him and the celestial order.

The Hako ceremony, a great ritual of peace, kinship, and fertility, was given to the Pawnee by Tirawa's children. In some accounts, the Morning Star and the Evening Star, as children of Tirawa, brought the ceremony to the people. The ceremony involves a sacred pole, which represents the cosmos and the connection between the people and the celestial powers. It is a prayer for the well-being of children, the fertility of the earth, and the unity of the people.

A complex and controversial ritual. The Morning Star, one of Tirawa's children, was believed to have demanded a human sacrifice in exchange for his power and the continuation of the cosmic cycle. A captive (usually a young woman) was sacrificed to the Morning Star in a highly structured ceremony. This practice was suppressed in the 19th century. The story of the Morning Star's demand is interpreted in various ways: as a literal command, as a symbolic cosmic necessity, or as a later corruption of the tradition.

Modern Interpretations

Ethnography Pawnee Hero Stories and Folk-Tales George Bird Grinnell 1889 direct One of the first major English-language collections of Pawnee stories, including references to Tirawa. Ethnography The Pawnee: Mythology (Part I) George A. Dorsey 1906 direct A comprehensive collection of Pawnee myths, including the creation narrative and the stories of the celestial deities. Scholarship Ceremonies of the Pawnee James R. Murie (edited by Douglas R. Parks) 1981 direct A detailed account of Pawnee ceremonies, including the Hako, with extensive notes on the role of Tirawa. Scholarship The Hako: A Pawnee Ceremony Alice C. Fletcher 1904 direct A key study of the Hako ceremony, which is the primary ritual connection between the people and Tirawa. Literature The Way to Rainy Mountain N. Scott Momaday 1969 inspired While focused on the Kiowa, Momaday's work is a powerful example of how Native American oral traditions and cosmology are presented in modern literature, a context in which Tirawa is often discussed. Film Dances with Wolves Kevin Costner 1990 inspired The film's depiction of Plains Indian spirituality, while not specifically about the Pawnee, has shaped popular perceptions of the 'Great Spirit' concept, which is often conflated with Tirawa. Internet Wikipedia and other online encyclopedias Various Ongoing reference Tirawa is a subject of online articles, but these are often brief and rely on the same few ethnographic sources.

Scholarly Debates
Role in Creation
Tirawa is the direct creator of the world. ⇌ Tirawa delegated creation to the Sun and Moon, remaining a distant, ultimate source of power.
Evidence for A: Some versions of the creation narrative say he made the earth.
Evidence for B: Other versions, particularly from the Skiri, say he tasked the Sun and Moon with creation.
Relationship with Nesaru
Nesaru is another name for Tirawa. ⇌ Nesaru is a separate, subordinate spirit or intermediary.
Evidence for A: Some early sources use the names interchangeably.
Evidence for B: Other sources describe Nesaru as a distinct being who acts as an agent of Tirawa.

What We Don't Know

1) The primary sources are all from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, recorded by non-Pawnee ethnographers. There is a need for contemporary Pawnee voices and perspectives on Tirawa. 2) The exact nature of the Morning Star sacrifice and its connection to Tirawa remains a sensitive and unresolved area of study. 3) The relationship between Tirawa and Nesaru is unclear and requires further analysis of the original Pawnee-language sources. 4) Very little is known about the pre-contact, pre-reservation theology of the Pawnee, as the ethnographic record was made after a period of immense disruption. 5) The specific rituals and prayers addressed directly to Tirawa (as opposed to his intermediaries) are not well documented.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Tirawa as the cosmic equivalent of a founder-CEO who has retired to a mountain retreat. He created the company (the universe), set the mission statement (the sacred order), and appointed a board of directors (the Sun, Moon, and stars) to run the day-to-day operations. He is the ultimate authority, the source of all power and legitimacy, but he doesn't micromanage. The people (the employees) don't pray to the CEO directly for a raise; they perform their duties (the ceremonies) and appeal to their immediate supervisors (the celestial deities). The CEO's will is absolute, but it is enacted through a complex hierarchy. Tirawa is the silent, all-powerful presence behind everything, the ultimate 'why' of the universe, even if he rarely appears in the 'how'.
