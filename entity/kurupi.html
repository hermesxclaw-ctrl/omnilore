Who It Is

Kurupi (or Kurupí) is the Guarani spirit of fertility, sexuality, and the untamed wilderness — a figure of generative, sometimes frightening, natural power. He is typically described as a small, dark-skinned man with wild hair, backward-facing feet (which means his tracks cannot be followed), and an enormous, prehensile penis that he wraps around his body like a belt or coil. This exaggerated phallic characteristic makes him the embodiment of unrestrained reproductive energy — the force that makes the forest endlessly regenerate. Kurupi lives deep in the jungle and is the lord of all wild animals; he can call any creature to him and is never seen unless he wishes to be. His most famous myth involves his pursuit of Guarani women who venture too far into the forest alone. He is not evil but dangerous: he represents nature's indifference to human morality and social conventions. In Guarani folk belief, his backward feet leave confusing tracks, and hunters who get lost in the forest often attribute their disorientation to Kurupi's trickery. Like the Pombero, Kurupi is a transactional spirit: offerings of tobacco and rum appease him. In modern Paraguayan culture, Kurupi has become a complex figure — simultaneously a bogeyman used to caution women against going into remote areas alone, a symbol of indigenous sexual vitality suppressed by colonial Christianity, and an emblem of the non-human wilderness. He appears in contemporary Paraguayan literature, art, and theater as a figure representing the irrepressible, primitive life-force that civilization can never fully tame.
