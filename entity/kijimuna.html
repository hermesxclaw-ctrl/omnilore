Etymology

The name 'kijimuna' is derived from the Okinawan language. 'Ki' means tree, and 'jimuna' is related to 'child' or 'spirit'. Thus, it translates to 'child of the tree' or 'tree ghost'. The alternative name 'bunagaya' means 'large-headed'.

Cultural Ownership

Ryukyuan (Okinawan) culture, specifically the Ryukyu Islands of Japan.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources equate kijimuna with the Japanese yōkai known as 'majimun' or 'magimun', but they are distinct entities. Kijimuna are specifically tree spirits, while majimun is a broader term for supernatural beings in Okinawan folklore.

Archive Dossier — Approach

In the moonlit banyan groves of Okinawa, a small, red-haired creature perches on a branch, watching with mischievous eyes. The kijimuna, a spirit of the sacred gajumaru tree, is both a playful trickster and a loyal friend—until trust is broken. Its tales are woven into the very fabric of Ryukyuan folklore, offering a glimpse into a world where the boundary between the human and the spirit is as thin as a leaf.

I live in the banyan tree with my red hair and my laughter. I hide your shoes, I steal your fish, but I will also guide you home if you ask nicely. Just don't bring an octopus near my tree!

Who It Is

Kijimuna (alternatively kijimun or bunagaya) are playful, red-haired forest sprites in Ryukyuan folklore, among the most beloved and well-known creatures of Okinawan mythology. They are described as small, child-like beings roughly 3-5 years old in appearance, with bright red or orange hair, round faces, ruddy skin, and a tendency to wear leaves or nothing at all. Kijimuna live in banyan trees (gajumaru), which are considered sacred in Ryukyuan culture. They are generally mischievous but not malevolent — they love practical jokes: hiding tools, switching objects, tickling sleeping people, pulling the hair of passersby. However, they can become dangerous if angered or disrespected. Cutting down a banyan tree without first asking the kijimuna's permission is a grave offense that invites misfortune. Kijimuna are also associated with fishing: they are said to help fishermen find the best spots, but only if the fisherman shares his catch. Kijimuna are terrified of octopuses and squid, and they cannot cross water (rivers or the sea) since they cannot swim. A common folk belief is that kijimuna can be seen only by children and by those with 'pure eyes.' The kijimuna legend is closely related to the Chinese/xianbei tradition of tree spirits and is similar to the Japanese kodama and the European brownie. In modern Okinawan culture, kijimuna have become mascots for local products and are featured in anime, manga, and tourism campaigns. They represent the playful, mischievous soul of the Ryukyuan natural world.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Kijimuna are described as resembling a three or four-year-old child, with wild red hair covering their bodies. They have large heads, and their size is child-like. They are often depicted as small, impish creatures.

Symbols & Iconography

Associated with the gajumaru (banyan) tree, which is considered sacred. In art, they are often shown with red hair and a mischievous grin.

Behaviour & Personality

Kijimuna are mischievous and playful, delighting in pranks on humans. They are known to lie on a person's chest, causing a feeling of paralysis (kanashibari). They are also skilled fishermen, but they only eat one eye of each fish they catch. They can be friendly and form friendships with humans, but these relationships often sour due to the kijimuna's sensitivity to certain behaviors, such as flatulence.

Domains & Habitats

They inhabit the banyan trees (gajumaru) of Okinawa, particularly on the island of Okinawa itself. They are wood spirits, closely tied to the forests and trees.

Version Differences

While the core description is consistent, variations exist in the details of their appearance and specific pranks. Some stories emphasize their fishing habits, others their tree-dwelling nature. The name 'bunagaya' is used in some regions, highlighting their large heads.
