Form

Albina's appearance is not described in any surviving primary source. As a divine figure, she would likely have been depicted in Roman style—perhaps robed, crowned, or holding attributes of dawn or the sea. In the medieval legend, she is described as a beautiful princess, the eldest of thirty sisters, though no physical details beyond her beauty and royal bearing are given. The name's association with whiteness suggests iconographic links to pale robes, white flowers, or chalk-white cliffs.

Powers

Eponymous naming Albina's primary power is linguistic: she is said to have given her name to the island of Albion (Britain). This is a foundational act—naming as creation, claiming as conquest. Medieval (Des Grantz Geanz, Geoffrey of Monmouth) This is a legendary, not historical, etymology. The name 'Albion' likely predates any Albina figure. Some sources derive 'Albion' from 'albus' (white cliffs) without reference to Albina.

Mother of giants Albina and her sisters mated with incubi (demonic spirits) and gave birth to a race of giants who inhabited Britain before the arrival of Brutus and the Trojans. Medieval (Des Grantz Geanz) This is a genealogical myth, not a personal power. It establishes Albina as a matriarchal founder figure. Geoffrey of Monmouth's account differs in details; some versions omit the demonic paternity.

Survival and leadership Albina leads her 29 sisters through exile, shipwreck, and wilderness survival, demonstrating resourcefulness and authority. Medieval Human-level capability; no supernatural enhancement. None.

Accounts

Albina, eldest of the thirty daughters of the Greek king Diocletian, is married to a cruel husband. She convinces her sisters that they are all mistreated by their husbands and proposes a solution: they will murder their husbands in their beds. The sisters agree and carry out the murders. The crime is discovered, and the sisters are set adrift in a ship without oars or sails. They eventually land on an uninhabited island (Britain), which Albina names 'Albion' after herself. There, they mate with incubi and give birth to giants.

Geoffrey briefly recounts that Britain was once called Albion, named after Albina, a princess who was exiled there. He does not provide the full murder narrative, which was likely known from oral tradition or a lost earlier source.

Content Notes

Albina and her sisters murder their husbands in their beds. This is the central act of the legend and is portrayed as both a crime (in the medieval moral framework) and an act of justified rebellion (from Albina's perspective). The narrative does not condemn Albina outright—it presents her as a victim of cruelty who takes violent revenge.

The story has been read as a medieval meditation on female agency, marriage, and the limits of patriarchal authority. Some scholars see it as a warning against female rebellion; others as a proto-feminist narrative of resistance.

Des Grantz Geanz

Albina and her sisters are set adrift at sea without oars or sails—a death sentence that becomes a miraculous survival. Their exile is both punishment and liberation.

The exile motif is common in medieval literature and echoes the biblical story of Cain, as well as classical myths of exile and foundation.

Des Grantz Geanz

Albina and her sisters mate with incubi, demonic spirits that seduce women, often in their sleep. The resulting pregnancies produce giants. In some versions, the encounters are framed as non-consensual (the women are asleep); in others, they are willing.

The incubus motif appears throughout medieval demonology and folklore. The giants of British legend are thus given a demonic lineage, positioning them as enemies of the Christianized Trojan founders.

Scholarly Debates
Historicity of Albina
Albina was a real historical figure, a Greek princess who founded Britain (medieval chroniclers and early modern antiquarians). ⇌ Albina is a medieval literary invention, likely a back-formation from the place name 'Albion' (modern scholarship).
Evidence for A: Chronicles from Geoffrey of Monmouth to Holinshed treat her as historical.

Albina as a Roman deity
Albina was a genuine Roman goddess, possibly of dawn or of the sea (some antiquarian and popular claims). ⇌ There is no evidence for a Roman goddess named Albina; the name appears only as a common female cognomen (family name) in inscriptions.
Evidence for A: The name 'Albina' appears in Roman inscriptions and literary sources.
Evidence for B: Inscriptions use 'Albina' as a personal name, not a divine epithet. No temple, cult, or myth is attested.
Etymology of 'Albion'
The island of Britain was named 'Albion' after Albina (medieval legend). ⇌ The name 'Albion' derives from the Indo-European root *albho- (white), referring to the white cliffs of Dover (modern linguistics).
Evidence for A: Des Grantz Geanz and Geoffrey of Monmouth.

What We Don't Know

No primary evidence for Albina as a Roman deity exists; all information about her as a divine figure is speculative. The medieval legend is well-attested but has not been traced to a definitive pre-12th-century source. The relationship between the Roman name 'Albina' and the medieval legend is unclear. No visual representations of Albina are known to exist. The full text of Des Grantz Geanz is available only in critical editions; no modern English translation is widely accessible.

A way to hold it in your mind

Albina is the 'dark matter' of British mythology: she's invisible in the classical record, yet her gravitational pull shapes the entire medieval origin story of Britain. She's like the hidden backstory in a video game—the lore that explains why the world is the way it is, even if most players never encounter it. Think of her as the anti-Arthur: where Arthur unites, Albina divides; where Arthur is the once and future king, Albina is the once and never-queen, a mother of monsters rather than a mother of nations.
