Who It Is

Imagine a princess who murders her husband, is cast adrift on the open sea, and ends up founding a nation of giants by mating with demons. That's Albina—the forgotten mother of Britain, a figure who lurks at the very beginning of the island's legendary history, yet is almost entirely unknown today. She is the dark mirror of Brutus of Troy: not a noble founder, but a defiant exile whose legacy is a race of monsters. And her name, whispered across two millennia, might just be the ghost behind the white cliffs of Dover.

In the medieval tradition, Albina is characterized by pride, defiance, and a capacity for violent revenge. She is the instigator of the plot to murder her husband, motivated by his cruelty and her own ambition. She is also a survivor—resourceful, determined, and unrepentant. As a divine figure, her personality is largely unknowable; the Roman Albina may have been a gentle dawn or boundary deity, but no myths survive to confirm this.

Powers
Eponymous naming

Albina's primary power is linguistic: she is said to have given her name to the island of Albion (Britain). This is a foundational act—naming as creation, claiming as conquest.

Limit: This is a legendary, not historical, etymology. The name 'Albion' likely predates any Albina figure.

Mother of giants

Albina and her sisters mated with incubi (demonic spirits) and gave birth to a race of giants who inhabited Britain before the arrival of Brutus and the Trojans.

Limit: This is a genealogical myth, not a personal power. It establishes Albina as a matriarchal founder figure.

Survival and leadership

Albina leads her 29 sisters through exile, shipwreck, and wilderness survival, demonstrating resourcefulness and authority.

Limit: Human-level capability; no supernatural enhancement.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Albina's appearance is not described in any surviving primary source. As a divine figure, she would likely have been depicted in Roman style—perhaps robed, crowned, or holding attributes of dawn or the sea. In the medieval legend, she is described as a beautiful princess, the eldest of thirty sisters, though no physical details beyond her beauty and royal bearing are given. The name's association with whiteness suggests iconographic links to pale

Symbols — No verified symbols or iconography exist for Albina as a deity. In the medieval tradition, her symbols are implicitly: the ship (her vessel of exile), the island (Britain), and the giant offspring (her legacy). The name itself—whiteness—serves as her primary symbolic attribute, linking her to dawn, chalk, and the boundary between known and unknown worlds.

Domains — Albina's domains are ambiguous: (1) as a Roman deity, she may have been associated with dawn, whiteness, or the western sea; (2) as a medieval figure, she is tied to the island of Britain (Albion), which she names and populates with giants. Her habitat is thus liminal—between sea and land, between divine and mortal, between the classical world and the medieval British imagination.

Accounts I
The Conspiracy of the Thirty SistersDes Grantz Geanz (c. 1300-1330)

High Medieval

Albina, eldest of the thirty daughters of the Greek king Diocletian, is married to a cruel husband. She convinces her sisters that they are all mistreated by their husbands and proposes a solution: they will murder their husbands in their beds. The sisters agree and carry out the murders. The crime is discovered, and the sisters are set adrift in a ship without oars or sails. They eventually land on an uninhabited island (Britain), which Albina names 'Albion' after herself. There, they mate with incubi and give birth to giants. The giants populate Britain and terrorize it until the arrival of Brutus and the Trojans, who defeat them. Albina's legacy is thus both foundational (naming the land) and monstrous (populating it with enemies).

Accounts II
The Naming of AlbionGeoffrey of Monmouth, Historia Regum Britanniae

12th century

Geoffrey briefly recounts that Britain was once called Albion, named after Albina, a princess who was exiled there. He does not provide the full murder narrative, which was likely known from oral tradition or a lost earlier source. Establishes a native, pre-Trojan origin for Britain, giving the island a deeper history and a female founder.

Etymology of 'Albion'The island of Britain was named 'Albion' after Albina (medieval legend).The name 'Albion' derives from the Indo-European root *albho- (white), referring to the white cliffs of Dover (modern linguistics).

Albina was a Roman goddess of dawn. — There is no evidence for this. 'Albina' in Roman sources is a personal name (cognomen), not a deity. The association with dawn is a modern inference from the name's meaning (white).

Albina was the first queen of Britain. — In the medieval legend, Albina is a princess, not a queen. She is exiled and never rules; she becomes the mother of giants, not a monarch. The title 'first queen' is a modern simplification.

The name 'Albion' comes from Albina. — The name 'Albion' is attested in classical sources (e.g., Pliny, Ptolemy) and derives from the Indo-European root *albho- (white), likely referring to the white cliffs of Dover. The Albina story is a medieval folk etymology.

Albina was a real historical figure. — No classical or early medieval source mentions her. She appears only in 12th-century and later texts, and is universally regarded by scholars as a literary invention.
