The Approach

Cannibalism, the consumption of one's own kind, is a stark and often misunderstood phenomenon that spans the animal kingdom and human history. Far more than a taboo or a horror trope, it is a complex ecological strategy with profound evolutionary, cultural, and medical implications.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses cannibalism in both animal and human contexts, including violent and ritualistic practices, disease transmission, and disturbing historical events. Reader discretion is advised.

Etymology

The term 'cannibal' originates from the Arawakan word 'caniba' or 'cariba', used by the Spanish to describe the Carib people of the Caribbean, whom they believed practiced cannibalism. It entered European languages via Columbus's accounts.

Cultural Ownership

The concept of cannibalism is global, with documented practices and taboos across many cultures. It is not owned by any single culture, but the term itself has colonial origins.

Disputed Identifications

Historically, accusations of cannibalism have been used as propaganda to justify colonization and conquest, leading to disputes over the veracity of many claims. Some groups have been labeled cannibals without definitive evidence.

Description

Appearance

No single appearance; refers to an individual (animal or human) that engages in cannibalism. In humans, no physical traits distinguish cannibals from others.

Symbols & Iconography

In popular culture, cannibalism is symbolized by devouring, often associated with monstrosity or extreme survival. In some cultures, ritual cannibalism has symbolic meanings related to respect, power, or spiritual beliefs.

Behaviour & Personality

In animals, cannibalistic behavior is often opportunistic, driven by hunger, stress, or population density. In humans, motivations range from survival (e.g., in famines or shipwrecks) to ritual, warfare, or pathological urges.

Domains & Habitats

Cannibalism occurs across diverse habitats, from aquatic to terrestrial, and in human societies across all continents. It is more common in nutritionally poor environments.

Version Differences

There is no single 'version' of a cannibal; the term applies to any individual of a species that consumes conspecifics. In humans, cultural practices vary: endocannibalism (consuming kin) vs. exocannibalism (consuming enemies).

Powers & Abilities

Nutritional acquisition

Source Version Animal behavior studies

Description

Cannibalism provides a high-quality food source, especially when other resources are scarce, supplying proteins and fats.

Limitations

May lead to injury during prey capture, and consumption of harmful compounds or pathogens.

Contradictions

Some studies show cannibalistic individuals have higher fitness, but population-level survival may decrease.

Population regulation

Source Version Ecological studies, e.g., in beetles and fish

Description

Cannibalism can reduce competition for resources, space, and mates, thereby regulating population density.

Limitations

May inadvertently consume kin, reducing inclusive fitness.

Contradictions

Benefits may be offset by increased disease transmission risk.

Disease transmission vector

Source Version Medical and epidemiological research

Description

Cannibalism can transmit pathogens and parasites, such as prions (kuru, BSE), nematodes, and viruses.

Limitations

Disease spread via cannibalism is inefficient compared to other transmission routes.

Contradictions

Cannibalism may reduce parasite loads by consuming infected individuals, but also increases exposure.

Stories & Myths

Kuru and the Fore people

Source Medical anthropology studies, e.g., by Carleton GajdusekCulture Fore people of Papua New GuineaPeriod Mid-20th century

Summary

The Fore practiced endocannibalism in funeral rituals, consuming deceased relatives, including brains. This led to the spread of kuru, a prion disease, particularly among women and children.

Participants

Fore people

Deceased relatives

Consequences

Epidemic of kuru with high mortality; practice ceased in the 1960s, leading to decline in disease.

Variants

Some accounts suggest that the practice was not universal among the Fore, and it may have been introduced relatively recently.

Donner Party

Source Historical accounts, diariesCulture American pioneersPeriod 1846-1847

Summary

A group of American pioneers trapped in the Sierra Nevada mountains resorted to cannibalism to survive after their wagons became snowbound.

Participants

Members of the Donner Party

Consequences

Of the 87 members, 48 survived; cannibalism occurred among the dead.

Variants

Some survivors denied participation, and accounts vary on who was consumed.

Cannibalism in the animal kingdom

Source Scientific studiesCulture Various speciesPeriod Ongoing

Summary

Many species, including amphibians, fish, insects, and mammals, exhibit cannibalism under certain conditions, such as overcrowding or food scarcity.

Participants

Various animal species

Consequences

Population regulation, nutritional benefits, but also risks of injury and disease.

Variants

Sexual cannibalism, size-structured cannibalism, and intrauterine cannibalism are distinct forms.

Human cannibalism in warfare and ritual

Source Historical records, anthropological studies

Description

Throughout history, human cannibalism has occurred in contexts of warfare, ritual, and survival. Examples include Aztec sacrificial practices (disputed), Pacific Islander rituals, and colonial-era accusations.

Scholarly Context

The extent and nature of many historical cases are debated, with some scholars questioning the reliability of colonial accounts.

Kuru epidemic

Source Medical and anthropological research

Description

The Fore people's practice of consuming deceased relatives led to a prion disease epidemic, causing severe neurological degeneration and death.

Scholarly Context

This is one of the most well-documented cases of disease transmission through cannibalism.

Cannibalism in famines and disasters

Source Historical accounts

Description

In extreme situations, such as sieges, shipwrecks, and famines, humans have resorted to cannibalism to survive. Examples include the Donner Party and the Siege of Leningrad.

Scholarly Context

These cases highlight the desperation of survival situations, but are often sensationalized.

Contradictions & Disputes

Benefits vs. costs of cannibalism

Position A

Cannibalism provides nutritional benefits and increases individual fitness.

Position A — evidence

Studies showing faster growth and higher fitness in cannibalistic tadpoles.

Position B

Cannibalism increases risk of injury, disease, and reduces population survival.

Position B — evidence

Studies showing increased pathogen transmission and population decline.

Scholarly Consensus

Both are true; the balance depends on ecological context.

Extent of human cannibalism in prehistory

Position A

Cannibalism was widespread in prehistoric human populations.

Position A — evidence

Archaeological finds of cut marks on human bones.

Position B

Cannibalism was rare and often misidentified.

Position B — evidence

Some marks may be from defleshing rituals, not consumption.

Scholarly Consensus

Debated; some cases are clear, but prevalence is uncertain.
