Who It Is

Imagine a pain so specific it feels like a narrow wind has blown straight into one side of your skull and is trying to push it apart — and imagine that, on the frontier of the Roman Empire, a soldier's family treated that pain not with a quiet tonic but by burying a strip of silver beside their dead with a sentence scratched onto it, commanding the very wind out of a living head. That wind had a name. She was Antaura, the Contrary Wind, the migraine-daemon — and she is one of the rare ancient entities who survives to us as a single, named, bannable cause of a single, named ailment.

Antaura is a hostile, invasive female spirit who rises from the sea and seeks to settle 'in the heads of people' to produce migraine — the half-sided headache the Greek physicians called hēmikrania. She is not a personal, articulate enemy in the surviving text but an elemental malevolent force, an 'evil wind', that must be named, confronted and ordered away by a higher power. Her effect is inseparable from the ancient belief that certain regional winds (Scirocco, Föhn) physically cause splitting head-pain.

Powers
Migraine Infliction

Antaura's defining power: she enters the human head and produces migraine — the severe, often one-sided (hemicranial) headache the ancient world knew as hēmikrania. The half-sided headache, pain striking head and eyes, inflammation, tears and dizziness become the catalogue of her effects in the later Christianised formulae, where the daemon is asked precisely where she carries this affliction.

Limit: She can be named, confronted and ordered to depart by a superior power (the Ephesian Artemis; later Christ or Saint Gregorius). Her power is conditional and removable through amulet magic, spoken command and banishing formula.

Affliction-by-Wind (the 'Contrary Wind')

Antaura is identified with an adverse or contrary wind (the 'aura' of her name, glossed as a 'böser Gegenwind'). She is the ancient personification of the kind of wind — like the Scirocco or the Föhn — long blamed for triggering splitting head-pain. She travels invisibly through the air, rises from the sea, and can be 'blown into' and out of a person.

Limit: As air she has no fixed body and no lasting foothold; a strong command, an amulet's protection, or a banishing formula suffices to drive her out.

Descent-Driven Persistence (inherited from Abyzou)

Through her genealogical root in Abyzou — the infertile Near-Eastern female daemon who, motivated by envy of fertile mothers, attacks childbirth and newborns, and who 'cannot sleep before she has killed a child each night' — Antaura shares a restless, compulsive, ungratifiable malevolence. In Antaura's case the compulsive urge is redirected upward: she 'wanted to settle in the heads of people and cause migraine' until forbidden.

Limit: The compulsion can be checked by naming, binding and banishing; in the Testament-type scene the power that binds her is a holy authority (Solomon/Artemis), and a written name on papyrus or an amulet makes her flee 'to the other world'.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No secure physical depiction of Antaura survives as a distinct, named figure; she is primarily a spoken/named force on inscribed amulets. She is conceptualised as an invisible wind or breath (aura/pneuma) that enters the head, and is said to come up out of the sea. Symbols — No fixed independent iconography; her elemental symbol is the adverse wind (the 'aura' of the name), and her amuletic context associates her with the protective imagery of the binding and banishing demon (the horseman/Solomon/Artemis figure who restrains her). In Christianised amulets the iconography shifts to the trampling rider and the gorgoneion/womb symbol inherited from the Abyzou tradition.

Domains — Sea (she emerges from the sea); the air; the human head (where she lodges and inflicts migraine). Her proper place, after banishment, is 'elsewhere' — in the Christianised formula, the uninhabited mountains 'where no dog barks and no cock crows'.

Accounts I
Antaura rises from the sea and is forbidden (the Carnuntum amulet)Greek lamella from a stone sarcophagus at Carnuntum (SEG 47, no. 1183), c. 2nd–3rd century CE

c. 2nd–3rd century CE

At the Roman frontier town of Carnuntum — garrison of the Legio XIV Gemina and a hub of the Danube amber trade — a stone sarcophagus held a small inscribed metal leaf (lamella) intended to ward off migraine. The text describes the female daemon Antaura, the 'Contrary Wind', coming up out of the sea with the intention of settling in a person's head and bringing hemlkrania. She is confronted by a goddess — most plausibly the Ephesian Artemis, who here plays the role that the male figures Solomon, Arlaph and Sisinnios take in the parallel Jewish and Christian texts — who forbids her and (by the standard reconstruction) banishes her elsewhere. The tablet is damaged and the text breaks off mid-sentence; the 'forbidden and banished' ending is recovered by analogy with the wide family of anti-disease amulets. The amulet was a therapeutic object: naming and commanding the daemon was believed to drive the migraine out of the sufferer. From Artemis to Christ: the medieval transformation of the migraine-daemonMedieval Christian amulet formulae; Byzantine/Orthodox charm tradition (documented in the German encyclopaedic literature and the Abyzou amulet corpus)

Medieval

The ancient scene of a goddess forbidding the migraine-daemon was repurposed for a Christian world. Jesus Christ — or, in the Eastern church, the Archangel Gregorius — takes the place of Artemis as the one who confronts Antaura. Christ/the archangel asks her where she carries the symptoms: the half-sided headache, the pain that strikes head and eyes, the inflammation, the tears, the dizziness. He warns that, should she not desist, she will be banished to the desolate mountain where no dog barks and no cock crows. In these retellings the Greek Antaura is recast as 'Aura', a 'Windsbraut' (storm-bride), or flattened into the neuter disease-name 'Migräne'. The therapeutic logic remains transfer-magic (Schadzauber): the ailment is addressed in the imperative and commanded to pass into beasts or inert matter rather than the suffering person. The tradition carried Antaura's underlying idea — a nameable, wind-borne daemon behind migraine — into a millennium of Christian charm- and amulet-magic, where it coexisted with (and often outlasted) the humoral medicine of the time.

Antaura in the great sea-daemon family (Barb's 'Mermaid and the Devil's Grandmother')A.A. Barb, 'Antaura. The Mermaid and the Devil's Grandmother: A Lecture', Journal of the Warburg and Courtauld Institutes 29 (1966) 1–23; the Abyzou tradition (Testament of Solomon)

Antiquity to the early Middle Ages

In the foundational scholarly study, A.A. Barb situated Antaura within the archaic family of female sea-daemones descended, in essence, from the Mesopotamian primeval sea (Abzu/Apsû, whence the Greek 'abyss'). This is the same kin-group as Abyzou — the infertile, envious child-strangling daemon identified with Lilith in Jewish tradition and with Gylou/Alabasandria in the Christian East — and, at a further remove, the seductive but deadly sea-monstresses of Greek myth (the Gorgons, sirens, harpies). Antaura is the migraine-specialist member of the line: a sea-born, wind-borne female who preys not on childbirth but on the head. The lecture's title puns on her two faces: 'the Mermaid' (the marine origin) and 'the Devil's Grandmother' (the near-relative of the abyss-born Devil). Barb's genealogical reading anchors Antaura in a coherent, ancient and wide Mediterranean phenomenon of female disease-daemones — a context the sparse single amulet alone would not reveal.

Antaura is a major 'goddess' of wind or the sea — Antaura is a minor, malevolent disease-daemon — specifically of migraine — known from a single inscription (with a reconstructed family context). She has no cult, no independent mythology, and no worship; she is a named force in protective magic, not a deity.

Antaura is the same as the classical goddess Aura — The classical 'Aura' (personification of the breeze, a follower of Dionysus) is a different figure. Antaura was only retyped 'into' Aura the Windsbraut in medieval Christian retellings of the migraine formula. Treating them as the same deity before the medieval transformation is anachronistic.

The Leto/Eileithyia reading of the Carnuntum invocation is settled — The confronting power is most likely the Ephesian Artemis; the Leto/Eileithyia reading is an older, fragment-dependent alternative. Presenting Artemis-only as universally agreed overstates the (real) scholarly consensus.

Antaura has documented film/game/anime appearances — Antaura herself has no known major modern adaptation. The horror receptions (The Possession, The Offering, Undertone, Sleepy Hollow) concern her genealogical kin Abyzou, and are routinely misattributed to Antaura in online listicles. This is flagged as an honest gap rather than asserted.
