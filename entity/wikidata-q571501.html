Form

No secure physical depiction of Antaura survives as a distinct, named figure; she is primarily a spoken/named force on inscribed amulets. She is conceptualised as an invisible wind or breath (aura/pneuma) that enters the head, and is said to come up out of the sea. A related visual tradition survives for her genealogical kin Abyzou (shown on amulets with fish- or serpent-like attributes, greenish gleaming face, dishevelled serpentine hair in the Testament of Solomon, or bound and whipped/rammed by a horseman). A cameo pendant in the Kunsthistorisches Museum Wien is catalogued as depicting the disease-daemon Antaura, indicating she does have at least one known small glyptic image.

Powers

Migraine Infliction Antaura's defining power: she enters the human head and produces migraine — the severe, often one-sided (hemicranial) headache the ancient world knew as hēmikrania. The half-sided headache, pain striking head and eyes, inflammation, tears and dizziness become the catalogue of her effects in the later Christianised formulae, where the daemon is asked precisely where she carries this affliction. Greco-Roman amulet tradition (Carnuntum lamella, 2nd–3rd c. CE); medieval Christian amulet formulae She can be named, confronted and ordered to depart by a superior power (the Ephesian Artemis; later Christ or Saint Gregorius). Her power is conditional and removable through amulet magic, spoken command and banishing formula. The extent of her effect (pure hemicrania/migraine vs. the fuller headache + eye + dizziness + inflammation catalogue) reflects a Christian-era expansion; the short ancient text only preserves the migraine core.

Affliction-by-Wind (the 'Contrary Wind') Antaura is identified with an adverse or contrary wind (the 'aura' of her name, glossed as a 'böser Gegenwind'). She is the ancient personification of the kind of wind — like the Scirocco or the Föhn — long blamed for triggering splitting head-pain. She travels invisibly through the air, rises from the sea, and can be 'blown into' and out of a person. Greco-Roman aetiology of wind and migraine (name meaning); German encyclopaedic tradition As air she has no fixed body and no lasting foothold; a strong command, an amulet's protection, or a banishing formula suffices to drive her out. Scholars debate whether she is fundamentally a wind-entity or a sea-daemon who merely travels on the wind; the sources preserve both (she comes out of the sea AND is equated with a wind).

Descent-Driven Persistence (inherited from Abyzou) Through her genealogical root in Abyzou — the infertile Near-Eastern female daemon who, motivated by envy of fertile mothers, attacks childbirth and newborns, and who 'cannot sleep before she has killed a child each night' — Antaura shares a restless, compulsive, ungratifiable malevolence. In Antaura's case the compulsive urge is redirected upward: she 'wanted to settle in the heads of people and cause migraine' until forbidden. Testament of Solomon / Abyzou tradition transmitted to the Greco-Roman and Byzantine amulet world (Barb 1966) The compulsion can be checked by naming, binding and banishing; in the Testament-type scene the power that binds her is a holy authority (Solomon/Artemis), and a written name on papyrus or an amulet makes her flee 'to the other world'. The exact descent line (Abyzou → Antaura) is a scholarly reconstruction (chiefly A.A. Barb) rather than stated in any one ancient text; not every scholar accepts the genealogy as direct.

Accounts

At the Roman frontier town of Carnuntum — garrison of the Legio XIV Gemina and a hub of the Danube amber trade — a stone sarcophagus held a small inscribed metal leaf (lamella) intended to ward off migraine. The text describes the female daemon Antaura, the 'Contrary Wind', coming up out of the sea with the intention of settling in a person's head and bringing hemlkrania. She is confronted by a goddess — most plausibly the Ephesian Artemis, who here plays the role that the male figures Solomon, Arlaph and Sisinnios take in the parallel Jewish and Christian texts — who forbids her and (by the standard reconstruction) banishes her elsewhere. The tablet is damaged and the text breaks off mid-sentence; the 'forbidden and banished' ending is recovered by analogy with the wide family of anti-disease amulets.

The ancient scene of a goddess forbidding the migraine-daemon was repurposed for a Christian world. Jesus Christ — or, in the Eastern church, the Archangel Gregorius — takes the place of Artemis as the one who confronts Antaura. Christ/the archangel asks her where she carries the symptoms: the half-sided headache, the pain that strikes head and eyes, the inflammation, the tears, the dizziness. He warns that, should she not desist, she will be banished to the desolate mountain where no dog barks and no cock crows. In these retellings the Greek Antaura is recast as 'Aura', a 'Windsbraut' (storm-bride), or flattened into the neuter disease-name 'Migräne'. The therapeutic logic remains transfer-magic (Schadzauber): the ailment is addressed in the imperative and commanded to pass into beasts or inert matter rather than the suffering person.

In the foundational scholarly study, A.A. Barb situated Antaura within the archaic family of female sea-daemones descended, in essence, from the Mesopotamian primeval sea (Abzu/Apsû, whence the Greek 'abyss'). This is the same kin-group as Abyzou — the infertile, envious child-strangling daemon identified with Lilith in Jewish tradition and with Gylou/Alabasandria in the Christian East — and, at a further remove, the seductive but deadly sea-monstresses of Greek myth (the Gorgons, sirens, harpies). Antaura is the migraine-specialist member of the line: a sea-born, wind-borne female who preys not on childbirth but on the head. The lecture's title puns on her two faces: 'the Mermaid' (the marine origin) and 'the Devil's Grandmother' (the near-relative of the abyss-born Devil).

Content Notes

The core of Antaura's material is the ancient and medieval conception of migraine as an invasive, nameable spirit that must be commanded out of the body. This is not morally disturbing in a conventional sense, but it reflects a worldview in which illness is a personal, hostile agency against which the sufferer must be defended by magic — a framing modern readers may find alien or unsettling.

Magico-healing formulae that personify and address the ailment ('Flee, Fever!') are well documented; the practice sits at the border of folk medicine, religion and exorcism.

Carnuntum lamella; Greco-Roman and medieval amulet magic

Through her reconstructed family connection to Abyzou, Antaura is tied to one of the most brutally dark registers of ancient folklore: the female daemon who, infertile and envious, strangles newborns and causes miscarriages, and who 'cannot sleep until she has killed a child each night'. The Testament of Solomon has Solomon bind and hang the demon by her own hair before the Temple. This genealogy, though not directly attested for Antaura in any one text, connects her to a tradition saturated with infant death.

Such daemones reflect real ancient anxieties around the extremely high rates of infant and maternal mortality; the folk response was apotropaic amulets and invocations.

Testament of Solomon; Abyzou tradition (Barb 1966)

Antaura lives entirely in the genre of protective/exorcistic magic — the binding, naming and banishing of a named spirit, and the later transfer-magic (Schadzauber) that commands illness to pass into animals or dead nature 'where no dog barks and no cock crows'. This is a culturally mainstream practice for its time, but it is coercive and counter-medical by modern standards, and coexisted with (rather than replacing) rational medicine.

Amulets were the most common protection in the Greco-Roman world against curses, the evil eye and disease-daemones; Pliny the Elder himself conceded 'no one is not afraid of spells'.

Medieval amulet formulae; German encyclopaedic account of Antaura

Antaura's story shows how a pagan, female, wind-borne disease-daemon was progressively 'cleaned up' and absorbed into Christian charm-magic (recast as Aura the Windsbraut or just 'Migräne'), and how an originally richly-textured figure is often reduced in popular and online sources to a single-line 'obscure demon' entry. The name's true range — from the sea-born Abyzou kin to the Ephesian Artemis confrontation — is routinely lost.

A one-verse entry cannot carry the weight of the amulet tradition this figure represents; the reduction is a normal hazard of online demonography.

What We Don't Know

The single most important source, the Carnuntum lamella (SEG 47 no. 1183), is fragmentary and its text breaks off mid-sentence; the standard restoration is by analogy and cannot be treated as verbatim. The dispute over whether the confronting deity is the Ephesian Artemis or Leto/Eileithyia could not be conclusively settled from the secondary sources available, and rests on the damaged epithet. The metal of the lamella is reported inconsistently (gold vs silver) across the scholarly and encyclopaedic literature and was not resolved in this tick — this needs the primary publication or museum record to confirm. I could not access Barb's original 1966 lecture in full text (paywalled/printed), so its detailed argument is reconstructed from the Abyzou article's citations and the German encyclopaedic account rather than read directly — a genuine gap to close with the primary article. The full Christianised charm formulae (Greek and Slavic) were not accessed in the original; only their German/English paraphrases. Whether a dedicated scholarly edition beyond Barb exists could not be confirmed. These are honest limitations, not assertions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a pain so specific it feels like a narrow wind has blown straight into one side of your skull and is trying to push it apart — and imagine that, on the frontier of the Roman Empire, a soldier's family treated that pain not with a quiet tonic but by burying a strip of silver beside their dead with a sentence scratched onto it, commanding the very wind out of a living head. That wind had a name. She was Antaura, the Contrary Wind, the migraine-daemon — and she is one of the rare ancient entities who survives to us as a single, named, bannable cause of a single, named ailment.
