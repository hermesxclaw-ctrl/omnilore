Who It Is

Her name means 'slayer of beasts' — yet Theraephone never slays anything in the stories that survive. She is an Olenian princess remembered only because of the men she was given to and the son she bore: twin sister to Theronice, wife to one of the monstrous double-bodied Molionides, and mother of Thalpius, who led the Epeians to Troy. Around her, centaurs attempt rape, Heracles murders a suitor at a wedding feast, and her husband's conjoined body is cut down by the greatest hero of the age — but Theraephone herself slips through the cracks of Greek myth, a name preserved in genealogies where a life should be.

No direct speech, decision, or personality trait is attributed to Theraephone. Her narrative function is entirely genealogical: daughter, wife, mother. She does not speak, resist, choose, or act in extant texts. The marriages of Dexamenus' daughters to the Molionides appear to be political alliances between Olenus and Elis, suggesting her marriage was arranged by her father, but the sources do not record consent, resistance, or emotion. Unlike Medea or Penelope, she has no agency narrative. Status: unattested; any personality ascribed in modern retellings is invention.

Powers
Royal Status (Princess of Olenus)

As daughter of King Dexamenus, Theraephone holds the social power of a basileia - eligibility for high-status alliance marriage, dowry, and production of legitimate heirs for the Epeian ruling house. This is political, not supernatural.

Limit: No independent authority attested; status derives from father and husband. Cannot rule in her own right in surviving sources.

Maternal Lineage of a Trojan War Leader

Through her son Thalpius, Theraephone is the maternal link between the pre-Trojan generation (Dexamenus/Heracles) and the Achaean army at Troy. Thalpius commands 10 ships of the Epeians in Iliad 2.620-624.

Limit: No power exercised directly; genealogical function only. Thalpius is sometimes listed as son of Eurytus without maternal name, so her maternity is not universally repeated.

Etymological 'Beast-Slayer' Name (No Attested Feat)

The name Theraephone literally means 'slayer of beasts.' No myth, however, attributes beast-slaying to her. The name may be honorific, prophetic, or simply part of a sibling naming pattern with Theronice ('victory over beasts').

Limit: No feat, weapon, or hunt is ever described. Treating the name as evidence of Amazonian or huntress ability is unsupported modern inference.

Accounts I
The Double Wedding: Dexamenus' Twin Daughters and the MolionidesPausanias, Description of Greece 5.3.3

Imperial period recording Archaic heroic tradition

Dexamenus, king of Olenus in Achaea, had twin daughters Theronice and Theraephone. The conjoined or twin sons of Actor (or Poseidon) and Molione - Eurytus and Cteatus, called the Molionides or Moliones - married them. Theronice wed Cteatus and bore Amphimachus; Theraephone wed Eurytus and bore Thalpius. The marriages cemented an alliance between Olenus and the Epeians of Elis, where the Molionides shared power with King Augeas. The weddings are presented without drama, as straightforward dynastic unions. Produces the next generation of Epeian leaders who will lead 40 ships to Troy (Iliad 2.620). Establishes Theraephone's sole narrative role as mother of Thalpius. Later, both Molionides are killed by Heracles, leaving both sisters widowed.

Heracles at Olenus: The Rescue of Theraephone's Sister from the Centaur EurytionDiodorus Siculus 4.33.1-2; Apollodorus Bibliotheca 2.5.5; Hyginus Fabulae 33

Hellenistic/Imperial mythography preserving earlier epic

While traveling to or from the Augean Stables labour, Heracles is hosted by Dexamenus at Olenus. Dexamenus had been forced to promise his daughter - called Hippolyte by Diodorus, Mnesimache by Apollodorus, Deianira by Hyginus - to the centaur Eurytion (brother of Peirithous' centaurs). Eurytion arrives to claim the bride, threatening violence. Heracles intervenes, kills Eurytion, and saves the daughter. In some versions Heracles then marries the daughter himself or defends the whole household. Theraephone is not the bride in this episode - she is already or soon to be married to Eurytus - but the story defines her family household and explains why Heracles later becomes the deadly enemy of her husband's family. Establishes Dexamenus as pious host of Heracles; creates narrative irony that Heracles saves one Dexamenid daughter while later killing the husbands of the other two. Explains the enmity between Heracles and the Molionides/Augeas that leads to the Elean war.

The Molionides' War with Heracles and NestorPausanias 5.2.1-3, 3.26.9; Iliad 11.670-752 (Nestor's reminiscence); Apollodorus 2.7.2

Archaic epic (Iliad) and Imperial periegesis

The Molionides, Eurytus and Cteatus, as generals of Augeas of Elis, oppose Heracles when he wages war on Elis after Augeas denies payment for cleaning the stables. In Iliad 11, Nestor recalls that the Molionides, still youths, defeated him in a chariot race and later led the Epeians against the Pylians. Heracles eventually ambushes and kills both Molionides at Cleonae during the Isthmian Games, despite their prodigious strength as conjoined twins. For Theraephone, this is the death of her husband Eurytus; for her sister Theronice, the death of Cteatus. Both sisters become widows, and their sons are raised fatherless. Leaves Thalpius and Amphimachus as orphans who must reclaim Elean leadership. Explains why the Epeian contingent at Troy is led by the sons rather than the fathers. Pausanias notes tombs of the Molionides at Cleonae. Theraephone's later life after widowhood is not recorded.

Accounts II
Thalpius at Troy: Theraephone's Son in the Catalogue of ShipsHomer, Iliad 2.620-624; Apollodorus Epitome 3.12; Dictys Cretensis 1.17

Archaic epic (8th c. BCE) and later epitomes

Thalpius, son of Eurytus and Theraephone, appears in the Catalogue of Ships as one of four leaders of the Epeians (with Amphimachus son of Cteatus and Theronice, Diores son of Amarynceus, and Polyxenus son of Agasthenes). He brings 10 ships from Elis and Buprasium to Troy. He fights in the general battles, is listed among the Achaean leaders, and survives the war to return home (in the Odyssey's background, the Epeians return). His presence proves Theraephone's genealogical importance: without her marriage, the Dexamenid blood would not reach Troy. Validates Theraephone's place in heroic genealogy; Thalpius' leadership is her only lasting memorial. Later mythographers use Thalpius to synchronize Olenian and Elean chronologies.

The Olenian Lineage: Dexamenus, Olenus, and the Epeian IdentityPausanias 7.18.1, 7.19.9; Strabo Geography 8.3.1-3.3; Stephanus of Byzantium s.v. Olenos

Hellenistic to Imperial

Dexamenus is presented as king of Olenus, one of the twelve ancient cities of Achaea, located on the Peirus river near Dyme. His family represents the old Achaean royalty before the Dorian migration. By marrying his daughters to the Molionides of Elis, the myth links Achaea and Elis politically. Olenus itself was abandoned by Pausanias' time (2nd c. CE), its inhabitants moved to Dyme and Patrae, making the Dexamenid story an aetiology for a vanished city. Theraephone thus belongs to a lost polis' royal house, remembered only through her son's Elean leadership. Explains why Theraephone is called 'Olenian princess' rather than Elean; preserves memory of Olenus after its synoecism. Strabo notes Olenus' ruins, confirming the city's historical reality behind the myth.

The Variant Daughters: How Many Dexamenides Were There?Apollodorus 2.5.5 (Mnesimache); Diodorus 4.33.1 (Hippolyte); Hyginus Fabulae 31 (Deianira); Pausanias 5.3.3 (Theronice & Theraephone)

1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE compilations

The number and names of Dexamenus' daughters are unstable. Pausanias names two twins: Theronice and Theraephone (wives of Molionides). Apollodorus names one daughter Mnesimache (victim of Eurytion). Diodorus names one daughter Hippolyte (same role). Hyginus names Deianira (same role, not the Deianira who marries Heracles, though the homonym causes confusion). Modern scholars (Gantz, Fowler) argue these are variant names for the same daughter, with Pausanias preserving a separate twin tradition, giving a total of three distinct daughters if harmonized (Theraephone, Theronice, Mnesimache/Hippolyte/Deianira), or two if Mnesimache is identified with one of the twins. Theraephone's existence is thus secure in the Elean genealogy but absent from the centaur episode. Creates modern confusion where Theraephone is sometimes listed as the centaur's intended victim, which no ancient source states. Highlights the fluidity of heroic genealogies and the need to keep traditions separate rather than harmonized.

Theraephone was an Amazon or huntress who killed beasts herself. — No surviving myth shows Theraephone hunting, fighting, or killing. Her name etymology ('slayer of beasts') is onomastic only; her narrative role is entirely domestic/genealogical (daughter, wife, mother). The Amazon-like name is not supported by any feat, and she is never listed among Artemis' companions, Amazons, or huntresses.

Theraephone was the daughter of Dexamenus who was almost married to the centaur Eurytion and rescued by Heracles. — Ancient sources consistently make the centaur's intended bride a different daughter - Mnesimache (Apollodorus), Hippolyte (Diodorus), or Deianira (Hyginus) - not Theraephone. Theraephone is already or soon to be married to Eurytus Molionides in the only source that names her (Pausanias). Conflating the sisters collapses two separate traditions.

Theraephone and Theronice are the same person or variant spellings of one name. — Pausanias explicitly treats them as twin sisters with distinct husbands and sons (Theraephone-Eurytus-Thalpius vs Theronice-Cteatus-Amphimachus). They share the thēr- root but have different second elements (-phone vs -nike) and produce distinct lineages that both appear in the Iliad Catalogue.

Theraephone appears as a character in modern films, games, or novels about Heracles or the Trojan War. — No verified modern adaptation features Theraephone as a named character. She is absent from major Heracles films (Disney's Hercules 1997, Hercules 2014), Troy (2004), and games like Hades, Assassin's Creed Odyssey, or Total War: Troy, which include Thalpius or the Molionides only as background names if at all. Her reception is limited to scholarly and encyclopedic mentions.
