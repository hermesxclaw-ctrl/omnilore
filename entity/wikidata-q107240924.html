Who It Is

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where the wolf was sacred and the mountains touched the sky, there lived a prince named Titanas—one of fifty sons of the impious king Lycaon. His name, echoing the Titans of old, promised greatness, but his fate was sealed by a single, gruesome act that would draw the wrath of Zeus himself.

Titanas is described as one of the most nefarious and carefree of all people, along with his brothers. This suggests a personality characterized by arrogance, impiety, and a lack of moral restraint. The act of mixing a child's entrails into a meal for Zeus indicates a complete disregard for divine law and human decency.

Powers
None

Titanas is not attributed any supernatural powers or abilities in the sources. As a mortal prince, he possesses only the ordinary capabilities of a human. His significance lies in his role in the myth of divine punishment, not in any personal prowess.

Limit: Mortal, subject to death by divine means.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No specific physical description of Titanas is provided in ancient sources. As a prince of Arcadia, he would be imagined as a young man of noble bearing, possibly athletic, but no details are attested.

Symbols — No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Titanas individually. He is part of the collective group of the Lycaonids, who are sometimes depicted in ancient art as being punished by Zeus, but individual identification is not possible.

Domains — Titanas was an Arcadian prince, presumably residing in the region of Arcadia in the Peloponnese, likely near the royal seat of Lycosura or on Mount Lycaeon. The domain is associated with the worship of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice in some traditions.

Accounts I
The Impiety of the Lycaonids and Their PunishmentPseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Mythical age, before the flood of Deucalion (estimated by scholars to be set in a distant heroic past)

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Titanas. They were notorious for their impiety and arrogance. To test them, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. The brothers, to prove their wickedness or to mock the god, mixed the entrails of a child into the meal prepared for Zeus. The god, enraged by this abomination, overturned the table and struck the brothers and their father with lightning, killing them all. This act was part of the broader myth explaining the origin of the flood sent by Zeus to punish humanity. The death of Titanas and his brothers by lightning, the punishment of Lycaon, and the subsequent flood sent by Zeus to cleanse the earth of impious humanity. The story also explains the origin of the cult of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice on Mount Lycaeon, which may have been a later misinterpretation.
