The Approach

In the shadowed highlands of Arcadia, where the wolf was sacred and the mountains touched the sky, there lived a prince named Titanas—one of fifty sons of the impious king Lycaon. His name, echoing the Titans of old, promised greatness, but his fate was sealed by a single, gruesome act that would draw the wrath of Zeus himself.

Content advisory. Contains references to child sacrifice, divine punishment, and death by lightning.
Identity

Titanas

Pronunciation /taɪˈtænəs/ or /ˈtɪtənəs/ (Greek: Τιτάναν, Tītánān)

Etymology

The name Titanas is likely derived from the Greek word 'Titan', referring to the primordial deities who preceded the Olympians. It may signify 'of the Titans' or 'Titan-like', possibly indicating a family that claimed descent from or association with the Titans, or simply reflecting the parents' ambition or the child's formidable nature.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek, specifically Arcadian regional mythology

Disputed Identifications

There is no significant dispute over the identification of Titanas as a distinct figure. However, the name is sometimes confused with the Titans themselves or with other similarly named figures in Greek mythology. The source material does not provide any alternative identifications.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Titanas is provided in ancient sources. As a prince of Arcadia, he would be imagined as a young man of noble bearing, possibly athletic, but no details are attested.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols or iconography are associated with Titanas individually. He is part of the collective group of the Lycaonids, who are sometimes depicted in ancient art as being punished by Zeus, but individual identification is not possible.

Behaviour & Personality

Titanas is described as one of the most nefarious and carefree of all people, along with his brothers. This suggests a personality characterized by arrogance, impiety, and a lack of moral restraint. The act of mixing a child's entrails into a meal for Zeus indicates a complete disregard for divine law and human decency.

Domains & Habitats

Titanas was an Arcadian prince, presumably residing in the region of Arcadia in the Peloponnese, likely near the royal seat of Lycosura or on Mount Lycaeon. The domain is associated with the worship of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice in some traditions.

Version Differences

The myth of the Lycaonids is attested in several versions. In some accounts, only Lycaon is punished, while in others, all fifty sons are killed. The number of sons and the exact nature of the crime vary. In the version of Pseudo-Apollodorus, all sons are killed by lightning. Other sources, such as Pausanias, suggest that Lycaon was turned into a wolf, and the sons may have been punished differently. Titanas is only mentioned in the list of sons in Apollodorus.

Powers & Abilities

None

Source Version All versions

Description

Titanas is not attributed any supernatural powers or abilities in the sources. As a mortal prince, he possesses only the ordinary capabilities of a human. His significance lies in his role in the myth of divine punishment, not in any personal prowess.

Limitations

Mortal, subject to death by divine means.

Contradictions

Stories & Myths

The Impiety of the Lycaonids and Their Punishment

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1Culture Ancient Greek (Arcadian)Period Mythical age, before the flood of Deucalion (estimated by scholars to be set in a distant heroic past)

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, had fifty sons, including Titanas. They were notorious for their impiety and arrogance. To test them, Zeus visited them disguised as a peasant. The brothers, to prove their wickedness or to mock the god, mixed the entrails of a child into the meal prepared for Zeus. The god, enraged by this abomination, overturned the table and struck the brothers and their father with lightning, killing them all. This act was part of the broader myth explaining the origin of the flood sent by Zeus to punish humanity.

Participants

Consequences

The death of Titanas and his brothers by lightning, the punishment of Lycaon, and the subsequent flood sent by Zeus to cleanse the earth of impious humanity. The story also explains the origin of the cult of Zeus Lycaeus and the practice of human sacrifice on Mount Lycaeon, which may have been a later misinterpretation.

Variants

In some versions, Lycaon is turned into a wolf (e.g., Pausanias 8.2.3-6). In other accounts, the sons are not all killed; some may have been punished differently. The number of sons varies between 22 and 50. The crime also varies: sometimes it is the murder of a child, sometimes the serving of human flesh to Zeus, and sometimes the testing of Zeus's omniscience.

Dark History

Child sacrifice and cannibalism

Source Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

The Lycaonids, including Titanas, are said to have mixed the entrails of a child into a meal served to Zeus. This act is a clear reference to human sacrifice and cannibalism, which were considered abhorrent in ancient Greek culture.

Scholarly Context

Scholars interpret this myth as aetiologizing the practice of human sacrifice at the altar of Zeus Lycaeus, which may have been a ritual in archaic times but was later abhorred. The myth serves to distance the contemporary Greeks from such practices by attributing them to a mythical, impious past.

Contradictions & Disputes

Fate of Lycaon

Position A

Lycaon was killed by lightning along with his sons (Apollodorus).

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1: 'Zeus... struck them all with lightning.'

Position B

Lycaon was turned into a wolf (Pausanias, Ovid).

Position B — evidence

Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.2.3-6: 'Lycaon... was changed from a man into a wolf.'

Scholarly Consensus

Both versions exist; scholars often reconcile by suggesting that Lycaon's transformation was a separate punishment or that the sources reflect different traditions. The death by lightning is more common in the myth of the flood.

Number of sons

Position A

Fifty sons (Apollodorus).

Position A — evidence

Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1: 'fifty sons'.

Position B

Twenty-two sons (some sources, e.g., Pausanias lists a different number in a different context).

Position B — evidence

Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.17.6 mentions a different number in a list of Lycaon's sons.

Scholarly Consensus

The number varies; fifty is the most common in later sources, but earlier sources may have had fewer. The discrepancy is likely due to different local traditions.

Primary Evidence

Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Type textDate 1st-2nd century CELocator Book 3, Chapter 8, Section 1Language Ancient Greek

Translation

The translation used is by Sir James George Frazer (1921): 'But Lycaon, the son of Pelasgus, had fifty sons by many wives... and they were the most impious of men. Zeus, to test their impiety, came to them in the likeness of a day-laborer. They mixed the entrails of a child with the offerings, and Zeus, disgusted, overturned the table and struck them all with lightning.'
