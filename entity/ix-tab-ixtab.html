Who They Are

Ix Tab is the Maya goddess of suicide and the patroness of those who die by hanging. Far from being a negative figure, she was a merciful deliverer who escorted suicides directly to paradise — bypassing the dangerous underworld of Xibalba. In Maya culture, certain forms of death (including suicide by hanging, sacrifice, and death in childbirth) were considered direct paths to a blissful afterlife.

The Approach

In the shadowed galleries of the Maya underworld, where the souls of the drowned and the hanged drift between worlds, there stands a figure whose name is whispered as a mercy: Ix Tab, the goddess of the rope. But who was she, really? A gentle guide for suicides, a demon of the gallows, or a colonial invention? The threads of her story are tangled, knotted by centuries of conquest and reinterpretation.

Content advisory. This entry discusses suicide and death rituals in Maya culture, as well as colonial-era descriptions that may reflect European biases. It includes references to hanging and the afterlife. Reader discretion is advised.

Etymology

The name 'Ix Tab' is often interpreted as 'Lady of the Rope' or 'She of the Rope' in Yucatec Maya, from 'ix' (lady/female) and 'tab' (rope, cord, or snare). Some scholars suggest a connection to the verb 'tab' meaning 'to hang' or 'to strangle'.

Cultural Ownership

Yucatec Maya (pre-Columbian and colonial period), primarily from the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have questioned whether Ix Tab was a pre-Columbian deity or a post-conquest Christian-influenced figure. The only direct reference is from Diego de Landa's 16th-century 'Relación de las cosas de Yucatán', which describes a goddess of the hanged and suicides. No clear pre-Hispanic iconography or epigraphic evidence has been identified, leading to debate about her antiquity.

Description

Appearance

No specific physical description of Ix Tab survives from primary sources. Colonial-era illustrations are absent. Modern artistic depictions often show a hanging figure or a goddess with a rope, but these are speculative reconstructions.

Symbols & Iconography

The rope or noose is the primary symbol associated with Ix Tab. Some modern interpretations link her to the Maya cross or the world tree, but this is not supported by historical evidence.

Behaviour & Personality

According to Landa, Ix Tab was a benevolent guide who welcomed the souls of those who died by hanging, granting them entry to a paradise. She is not described as punishing or malevolent.

Domains & Habitats

Ix Tab's domain is the afterlife, specifically a paradise for the hanged and possibly other suicides. She is not associated with any earthly location.

Version Differences

Landa's account is the only historical version. Later colonial and modern interpretations vary: some see her as a demonic figure, others as a compassionate deity. No pre-Columbian version is known.

Suicide in Maya culture

Source Landa (1566)

Description

Landa reports that the Maya believed suicide by hanging led to paradise, possibly leading to suicides for trivial reasons or as a way to escape hardship. This has been interpreted as evidence of a cultural acceptance or even encouragement of suicide.

Scholarly Context

Modern scholars debate the extent to which this belief was widespread or whether Landa exaggerated or misinterpreted Maya practices.

Colonial distortion

Source Scholarly analyses of Landa's writings

Description

Landa, as a Spanish bishop, was engaged in the conversion of the Maya and may have portrayed their beliefs in a negative light to justify missionary work. Some scholars argue that Ix Tab may have been a colonial invention or a misrepresentation of a more complex deity.

Scholarly Context

Postcolonial scholarship emphasizes the need to critically assess colonial sources.

Cultural Significance

Ix Tab's role reflects very different attitudes toward suicide in Maya culture compared to the Western Christian tradition. The Maya did not condemn suicide; instead, they saw it as one of several 'privileged deaths' that guaranteed immediate access to paradise. Ix Tab is unique among world mythologies — there is no other major deity whose primary function is to preside over suicide. This has made her a figure of particular interest in studies of comparative religion and cultural attitudes toward death. In the modern era, Ix Tab has been reclaimed by some Maya as a symbol of the right to choose one's own death with dignity.
