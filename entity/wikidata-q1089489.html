Who It Is

Envy is the pain you feel not because you have too little, but because someone else has more — and for more than two thousand years philosophers, theologians, and scientists have argued whether that pain is a deadly sin, a social poison, or the hidden engine of justice itself.

Phenomenology: painful social comparison, sense of inferiority, perceived undeserved advantage of the target, hostility or longing, and action tendencies that differ by subtype. Malicious envy (afgunst): hostile, wishes the envied person would lose the advantage even at cost to self, associated with schadenfreude, undermining, and social aggression. Benign envy (benijden): grudging admiration, motivates self-improvement and emulation without ill will, still unpleasant. Powers
Attentional Capture and Memory Enhancement

Envy concentrates cognitive resources, focusing attention on the envied target and improving recall of information about the target's advantage. Demonstrated in lab studies where envious participants show superior memory for details about superior peers.

Limit: Effect is domain-specific and moderated by relevance and self-relevance; does not improve general cognition; can impair performance via rumination and distraction.

Motivational Drive - Benign Envy (Emulative Function)

Benign envy motivates self-improvement, emulation, and increased effort to close the gap with the envied person. Associated with positive thoughts about the other and admiration.

Limit: Still feels unpleasant; only beneficial if coped with via stimulus-focused and self-bolstering strategies; can tip into malicious envy if perceived control is low.

Hostile Motivation - Malicious Envy (Leveling Function)

Malicious envy motivates leveling-down: desire to bring down the better-off even at cost to self, willingness to pay to deprive target, schadenfreude when target fails, and social undermining.

Limit: Often suppressed due to social desirability; self-report underestimates prevalence; requires perceived undeserved advantage and low perceived control to improve self.

Social Comparison and Fairness Regulation

Hypothesized evolved function: envy tracks inequity and motivates interest in fairness and justice within groups. Frans de Waal's primate research suggests envy-like inequity aversion in capuchins and chimpanzees regulates fair outcomes.

Limit: Animal inequity aversion is contested as true envy vs. frustration/contrast effect; human fairness is also driven by other motives.

Evil Eye - Folk Magical Harm

In folklore, the envious gaze itself is believed to cause harm, illness, or loss to the envied person, object, or infant. Attributed power of Invidia/Phthonos across Mediterranean and global evil-eye traditions. Not a literal power but a culturally attributed effect.

Limit: No empirical support for supernatural harm; functions as social control for envy display and boasting.

Accounts I
Cain and Abel - The First Envy MurderHebrew Bible, Book of Genesis 4:3-8; also Qur'an 5:27-31

Final textual form c. 6th-5th century BCE; traditions older

Cain, a tiller of soil, and his brother Abel, a keeper of sheep, bring offerings to God. God favors Abel's offering. Cain becomes downcast and, in pain at his brother's favor, kills Abel in the field. God confronts Cain: 'Where is Abel your brother?' and curses Cain to wander. The narrative is the archetypal envy story: sorrow at another's good fortune turning to destructive action. Early Jewish midrash and Christian exegesis explicitly name Cain's motive as envy (phthonos/invidia). Establishes envy as a capital moral failure leading to fratricide; etiology of exile and mark of Cain; foundational for Christian teaching on envy as sin that destroys kinship.

Joseph and His BrothersHebrew Bible, Genesis 37-50

Final form c. 6th-5th century BCE

Jacob favors his son Joseph, giving him a ketonet passim (often translated as coat of many colors). Joseph dreams that his brothers' sheaves bow to his. His brothers envy him (Genesis 37:11: 'his brothers were jealous of him' - Hebrew vayeqan'u) and conspire to kill him, instead selling him into slavery in Egypt. Joseph later rises to power and eventually forgives them. The text explicitly links paternal favoritism, dreams of status, and fraternal envy. Joseph's descent and ascent; Israel's migration to Egypt; moral lesson on envy within families and divine providence turning envy to good.

Phthonos and the Envy of the GodsHerodotus, Histories; Pindar, Odes; Greek tragedy

5th century BCE

The Greeks feared phthonos theon - the envy of the gods. Herodotus has Solon warn Croesus and Artabanus warn Xerxes that the divine is phthoneron (grudging) and strikes down those who are too great or happy. Pindar warns victors not to incur phthonos. The daemon Phthonos personifies this grudging envy that begrudges human success. To avoid phthonos, Greeks practiced moderation and apotropaic self-deprecation. Cultural ethic of moderation (sophrosyne); ritual practices to deflect envy; explanation for sudden downfall of the great.

Accounts II
The Terrace of the Envious - Dante's PurgatorioDante Alighieri, Purgatorio Cantos 13-14

c. 1308-1321 CE

On the second terrace of Mount Purgatory, the envious sit against the cliff, cloaked in haircloth the color of livid stone, their eyelids sewn shut with iron wire. Dante speaks with Sapia of Siena, who confesses she rejoiced at her fellow Sienese's defeat, and with Guido del Duca, who laments the envy that has poisoned Romagna. Virgil explains envy as love of good perverted to sorrow at another's good. The souls hear the litany of charity and have the eyes that sinned sewn closed to learn to see with charity. Definitive medieval image of envy as spiritual blindness; contrapasso of sewn eyes becomes the dominant Western iconography of Invidia; influences all later Seven Sins art.

Snow White and the Envious QueenBrothers Grimm, Kinder- und Haus-Märchen, tale no. 53 'Sneewittchen' (Schneewittchen)

First published 1812, final edition 1857

A queen, envious of her stepdaughter Snow White's surpassing beauty, repeatedly asks her mirror 'Who is the fairest of all?' When the mirror answers Snow White, the queen's envy drives her to three murder attempts (laced bodice, poisoned comb, poisoned apple). Snow White is rescued by dwarfs and a prince. The queen is forced to dance to death in red-hot iron shoes. The tale makes envy (Neid) explicitly the motive for female rivalry and murder. Archetypal fairy-tale of beauty-envy; Disney's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) codifies the queen as the Disney villainess; feminist retellings reinterpret the envy as patriarchal competition.

Iago and Othello - Envy as Engine of TragedyWilliam Shakespeare, Othello (c. 1603)

Jacobean 1603-1604

Iago, passed over for promotion in favor of Cassio, is consumed by envy and resentment toward Othello and Cassio. He manipulates Othello into believing Desdemona is unfaithful, exploiting Othello's own jealousy. While Iago's motive is debated (envy, racism, ambition, motiveless malignity), Coleridge called it 'motiveless malignity' and modern critics center professional envy and status envy as his engine. Envy here is not coveting a possession but pain at another's advancement and desire to undo it. Destruction of all principals; locus classicus for malicious envy as social sabotage; source of the phrase 'green-eyed monster' (Act 3, Scene 3, actually describing jealousy but culturally merged with envy).

Are benign and malicious envy two distinct emotions or one emotion with different coping?Benign and malicious envy are qualitatively distinct subtypes with different appraisals, feelings (longing vs hostility), and action tendencies (improve self vs harm other). Supported by Dutch lexicon and factor analyses.There is one envy emotion (pain at another's advantage); what differs is coping, perceived control, and deservingness, not the emotion itself. Action tendencies should not define the emotion.
Is envy an evolved adaptation for fairness or a cultural byproduct of inequality?Envy is an evolved adaptation that tracks inequity, motivates social comparison, and regulates fairness; without it we would not care about justice. Primate inequity aversion and mammalian affective neuroscience support this.Envy is primarily culturally amplified by market societies, scarcity, and hierarchy; its intensity varies historically and is not a discrete module but a blend of pain, inferiority, and hostility shaped by learning.
Does envy motivate redistribution or is that a political misuse of the concept?Envy, especially malicious envy, increases support for economic redistribution even when it harms the envious, as shown in economic games where participants pay to reduce others' payoffs.Labeling support for redistribution as 'envy' is ideological and delegitimizes justice concerns; experimental willingness to pay may reflect fairness preferences, not envy.

Envy and jealousy are the same thing. — In psychology and philosophy, envy is wanting what another has (two-party: you and the envied other), while jealousy is fear of losing what you have to a rival (three-party: you, beloved, rival). You envy a colleague's promotion; you are jealous when you fear your partner will leave you for someone else. Everyday English conflates them, but the distinction is crucial for research and for understanding the emotions' different action tendencies.

Envy is always malicious and wants to destroy the other person. — Research distinguishes malicious envy (hostile, wanting the other to lose the advantage) from benign envy (unpleasant but admiring, motivating you to improve yourself). Benign envy can lead to emulation, harder work, and positive thoughts about the envied person. The Dutch words benijden vs afgunst capture this split that English lacks.

The 'green-eyed monster' is about envy. — Shakespeare's phrase in Othello (3.3.165-167: 'O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock The meat it feeds on') explicitly describes jealousy, not envy. Culture has merged the idioms 'green with envy' and 'green-eyed monster,' but the original is jealousy.

Envy is a modern product of social media and consumer culture. — Envy is documented in the earliest human texts (Aristotle's Rhetoric 4th c. BCE, Genesis, Greek phthonos) and was codified as a capital sin by 590 CE. Market societies and social media amplify and make visible envy, but they did not invent it. Anthropologists find envy in small-scale societies, often regulated by evil-eye beliefs.

Feeling envy makes you a bad or petty person. — Envy is a universal, normal emotion arising from relevant upward comparison. How you cope matters more than feeling it. Research shows stimulus-focused coping (focusing on how to improve) and self-bolstering (recalling own strengths) are more effective than suppression or self-blame, and benign envy can be harnessed for growth. Pathology lies in chronic, hostile rumination, not in the emotion itself.
