Accounts I
The Midwife's Secret

In a kampung (village) in rural Malaysia, a well-respected midwife attended every birth in the district. But a pattern emerged: every child she delivered would fall deathly ill within days, and several died mysteriously. A young mother, suspicious after the midwife visited and her newborn grew pale and weak overnight, set a trap. She scattered mengkuang thorns around her window and under the house. That night, she heard terrible shrieking — the Penanggalan's entrails had snagged on the thorns. At dawn, the midwife was found dead in her home, her body and head still separated, her organs tangled in the thorny leaves.

The Jar of Vinegar

A young man in Penang became suspicious of his beautiful new wife, who insisted on locking herself in the bathing room every evening and forbade him entry. One night, overcome with curiosity, he crept to the door and peered through a crack. He saw his wife's body sitting motionless in a large clay jar filled with vinegar, but her head and organs were gone. Terrified, he ran to the village bomoh (shaman) who advised him to turn the jar upside down. When the Penanggalan returned at dawn, unable to reattach to her body, she writhed in agony until sunrise destroyed her.

Accounts II
The Glowing Trail Across the Padi Fields

Farmers working late in the Kedah padi fields reported seeing a flickering ball of light crossing the night sky, trailing something that looked like wet ropes beneath it. An elderly villager warned them it was a Penanggalan hunting for a pregnant woman in the next village. The men formed a watch party, lighting torches and creating loud noises with bamboo clappers to drive the creature away. The glowing orb veered sharply and disappeared over the hill — but the next morning, the woman in the neighboring kampung gave birth safely, protected by the noise and fire that had scared the Penanggalan off.

The Penanggalan and Sir Stamford Raffles

In the Hikayat Abdullah (1845), Munshi Abdullah records how he described the Penanggalan to Sir Stamford Raffles, who was both horrified and fascinated by the concept of a flying head with trailing entrails. Raffles, ever the ethnographer, pressed Abdullah for more details about Malay folk beliefs, and Abdullah explained the full mechanics: the vinegar bath, the detachment ritual, and the creature's preference for the blood of childbirth. Raffles reportedly remarked that no Western ghost story compared with the sheer visceral horror of the Penanggalan.
