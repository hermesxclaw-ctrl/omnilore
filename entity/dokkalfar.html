Who It Is

In the Prose Edda, the cosmos is sketched in bold strokes: radiant halls, roaring wolves, and worlds stacked like storm clouds. Amid this architecture of gods and giants, Snorri pauses to mention beings who live ‘within the earth’, blacker than pitch and behaving quite unlike their sun-bright kin. These are the Dökkálfar—the dark elves—barely more than a shadow in the surviving texts, yet powerful enough as an idea to seed centuries of speculation, fantasy races, and moral tales about what lurks beneath the bright surface of the world.

Attested: Snorri’s brief contrast implies that Dökkálfar ‘look and particularly behave quite unlike’ Ljósálfar, but he does not specify their behavior. They are not explicitly called evil, but their darkness and subterranean dwelling suggest a more dangerous or uncanny nature. Reconstructed: Scholars often see Dökkálfar as embodying the ambivalent, potentially harmful side of elf belief—spirits associated with illness, death, or the underworld—mirroring broader Germanic traditions where elves could both bless and harm humans. Powers
Subterranean dwelling and earth association

Dökkálfar are explicitly said to dwell ‘within the earth’, marking them as beings of subterranean realms. This implies an affinity with darkness, stone, and possibly burial spaces, aligning them with chthonic aspects of Norse cosmology.

Limit: The text does not describe any specific powers—only their habitat. Any further abilities (e.g., control of earth, mining, or necromancy) are speculative.

Potential harmful or uncanny influence

By contrast with Ljósálfar (‘fairer than the sun’), Dökkálfar are darker and behave differently. In the broader Germanic context, elves could cause illness or misfortune as well as grant blessings. Dökkálfar may represent the more dangerous, uncanny side of elf belief.

Limit: No specific acts of harm or blessing are attributed to Dökkálfar in surviving Norse narratives. Their role remains largely conceptual.

Possible overlap with dwarven craftsmanship and magic

Svartálfar (‘black elves’) and Svartálfaheimr are associated with dwarfs who forge powerful artifacts (Gleipnir, Sif’s hair, other treasures). If Dökkálfar and svartálfar are synonymous or overlapping categories, Dökkálfar might share these traits of subterranean craftsmanship and magical skill.

Limit: Dökkálfar are never explicitly named as smiths or magicians in the medieval texts. The association is inferential and should be treated cautiously.

Cosmological role in light/dark duality

Dökkálfar function conceptually as the ‘dark’ counterpart to Ljósálfar, embodying a dual structure of elves in Norse cosmology. This duality may reflect broader patterns of light/dark, upper/lower worlds, and possibly life/death or blessing/harm.

Limit: The cosmological role is schematic rather than narrative; Dökkálfar do not appear in specific mythic episodes.

Accounts I
The contrast of light and dark elves in GylfaginningProse Edda, Gylfaginning 17 (Snorri Sturluson).

13th century (post‑conversion Christian context).

In the frame narrative of Gylfaginning, the disguised king Gylfi (Gangleri) questions the enthroned figure High about the structure of the cosmos. High describes Álfheimr as a beautiful place in heaven where Ljósálfar dwell, ‘fairer than the sun to look at’. He then contrasts them with Dökkálfar, who dwell within the earth and are ‘blacker than pitch’ and behave quite differently. Later in the same chapter, High mentions higher heavens (Andlàngr, Víðbláinn) where light-elves are believed to dwell. Dökkálfar thus appear only as a brief, schematic counterpart to light-elves, with no further narrative development. The passage establishes a dual classification of elves that has strongly influenced later interpretations of Norse cosmology and modern fantasy. It does not depict any actions by Dökkálfar but positions them conceptually as subterranean, dark beings.

Accounts II
Dökkálfar in Hrafnagaldr ÓðinsHrafnagaldr Óðins (Odin’s Raven-Galdr), stanza 25.

Probably late Middle Ages; exact dating uncertain.

In a dense, esoteric stanza, the poem lists various beings and forces in a cosmological and magical context, including Dökkálfar. The reference is brief and embedded in obscure imagery, offering no clear narrative about the dark elves themselves. The poem’s status—whether it is genuinely ancient or a later learned composition—remains debated, which affects how much weight scholars give its mention of Dökkálfar. The stanza confirms that the term Dökkálfar was known beyond Snorri’s prose, but it does not significantly expand their characterization. Its disputed authenticity limits its evidential value.

Forging of Gleipnir and treasures in Svartálfaheimr (comparative context)Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 33; Skáldskaparmál 35, 39.

13th century.

Odin sends Skírnir to Svartálfaheimr to dwarfs (svartálfar) who forge Gleipnir, the magical fetter that binds the wolf Fenrir. In Skáldskaparmál, Loki coerces the Sons of Ivaldi (svartálfar/dwarfs) to craft golden hair for Sif and other treasures for the gods, and later encounters the dwarf Andvari in Svartálfaheimr. These episodes show subterranean beings crafting powerful artifacts in a dark realm. While Dökkálfar are not named, many modern discussions treat these stories as illustrative of the broader ‘dark elf’ complex. The stories establish dwarfs/svartálfar as essential craftsmen in Norse myth, linking subterranean beings with transformative magic and peril. If Dökkálfar and svartálfar are related, these episodes indirectly inform our understanding of dark elves as chthonic artisans.
