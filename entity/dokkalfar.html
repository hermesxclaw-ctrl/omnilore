We are the ones who live under the earth, in the dark where the roots reach. The Light Elves shine above; we forge below. Our mounds are our kingdoms — pass them with respect.

Who It Is

The Dökkálfar (Dark Elves) are one of the two primary elf races in Norse cosmology, contrasted with the Ljósálfar (Light Elves). According to Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, the Light Elves dwell in Álfheimr and are 'fairer than the sun to look upon,' while the Dark Elves live underground in Svartálfaheimr and are 'blacker than pitch.' However, the distinction is blurry: the Dark Elves are often conflated with the Dvergar (Dwarves), who also live underground and are master smiths and craftsmen. Many scholars believe Snorri's Ljósálfar/Dökkálfar distinction was influenced by Christian angelology (angels of light vs. fallen angels) imposed on earlier, less binary Norse traditions. In the sagas, elves (álfar) are ambiguous beings associated with burial mounds, fertility, and ancestor worship — the álfablót (elf-sacrifice) was a domestic ritual conducted by the woman of the household to secure fertility and the favour of the mound-dwelling dead. Elves could cause illness through 'elf-shot' and could be propitiated with offerings. The Dökkálfar thus represent the chthonic, dangerous aspect of elf-belief — the spirits of the dead who live under the earth, who possess hidden knowledge and craft, and who must be appeased. The concept fed into later Scandinavian folk belief about the 'underjordiske' (underground folk) who live in mounds and who abduct humans. In modern fantasy (Tolkien, D&D), the 'dark elf' concept has evolved into the Drow and similar subterranean elf races — a significant departure that owes more to Victorian fairy lore and fantasy worldbuilding than to the original Norse sources.

Form

Attested: In Gylfaginning 17, High describes Dökkálfar as ‘blacker than pitch’ and dwelling within the earth. No further physical details are given—no height, clothing, or specific features. Reconstructed: Scholars infer that, as ‘dark elves’, they were imagined as shadowy, earth-colored or black-complexioned beings associated with subterranean spaces, perhaps visually contrasted with the radiant Ljósálfar. Some modern interpreters, influenced by fantasy tropes, depict them as slender, sinister fae-like figures with dark skin and eyes, but these are adaptation-specific and not grounded in medieval sources. Unknown: There is no direct medieval description of their size, gender presentation, or detailed anatomy.

Powers

Subterranean dwelling and earth association Dökkálfar are explicitly said to dwell ‘within the earth’, marking them as beings of subterranean realms. This implies an affinity with darkness, stone, and possibly burial spaces, aligning them with chthonic aspects of Norse cosmology. Attested in Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 17 (Snorri Sturluson). The text does not describe any specific powers—only their habitat. Any further abilities (e.g., control of earth, mining, or necromancy) are speculative. Some modern interpretations treat Dökkálfar as identical to dwarfs/svartálfar, who are master smiths and magical craftsmen; others insist that Dökkálfar are a separate elf class with unknown abilities. The primary sources do not resolve this.

Potential harmful or uncanny influence By contrast with Ljósálfar (‘fairer than the sun’), Dökkálfar are darker and behave differently. In the broader Germanic context, elves could cause illness or misfortune as well as grant blessings. Dökkálfar may represent the more dangerous, uncanny side of elf belief. Interpretive reconstruction based on Prose Edda’s contrast and comparative elf folklore; not explicitly stated in the text. No specific acts of harm or blessing are attributed to Dökkálfar in surviving Norse narratives. Their role remains largely conceptual. Some scholars resist moralizing the distinction, arguing that ‘dark’ may be primarily spatial (under the earth) rather than ethical. Others see Christian dualism (light vs. darkness) as shaping Snorri’s portrayal.

Possible overlap with dwarven craftsmanship and magic Svartálfar (‘black elves’) and Svartálfaheimr are associated with dwarfs who forge powerful artifacts (Gleipnir, Sif’s hair, other treasures). If Dökkálfar and svartálfar are synonymous or overlapping categories, Dökkálfar might share these traits of subterranean craftsmanship and magical skill. Attested for svartálfar/dwarfs in Prose Edda (Gylfaginning 33; Skáldskaparmál 35, 39); extended to Dökkálfar only in some scholarly and modern interpretations. Dökkálfar are never explicitly named as smiths or magicians in the medieval texts. The association is inferential and should be treated cautiously. Lindow, Simek, and others note that svartálfar appear to be dwarfs; whether Dökkálfar are the same beings is unresolved. Treating Dökkálfar as smiths risks conflating distinct categories.

Cosmological role in light/dark duality Dökkálfar function conceptually as the ‘dark’ counterpart to Ljósálfar, embodying a dual structure of elves in Norse cosmology. This duality may reflect broader patterns of light/dark, upper/lower worlds, and possibly life/death or blessing/harm. Attested in Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 17; elaborated in modern scholarship on Snorri’s systematization. The cosmological role is schematic rather than narrative; Dökkálfar do not appear in specific mythic episodes. Some scholars argue that the duality is largely Snorri’s literary construction, influenced by Christian angelology (angels of light and darkness), rather than a robust pre‑Christian belief system.

Accounts

In the frame narrative of Gylfaginning, the disguised king Gylfi (Gangleri) questions the enthroned figure High about the structure of the cosmos. High describes Álfheimr as a beautiful place in heaven where Ljósálfar dwell, ‘fairer than the sun to look at’. He then contrasts them with Dökkálfar, who dwell within the earth and are ‘blacker than pitch’ and behave quite differently. Later in the same chapter, High mentions higher heavens (Andlàngr, Víðbláinn) where light-elves are believed to dwell. Dökkálfar thus appear only as a brief, schematic counterpart to light-elves, with no further narrative development.

In a dense, esoteric stanza, the poem lists various beings and forces in a cosmological and magical context, including Dökkálfar. The reference is brief and embedded in obscure imagery, offering no clear narrative about the dark elves themselves. The poem’s status—whether it is genuinely ancient or a later learned composition—remains debated, which affects how much weight scholars give its mention of Dökkálfar.

Odin sends Skírnir to Svartálfaheimr to dwarfs (svartálfar) who forge Gleipnir, the magical fetter that binds the wolf Fenrir. In Skáldskaparmál, Loki coerces the Sons of Ivaldi (svartálfar/dwarfs) to craft golden hair for Sif and other treasures for the gods, and later encounters the dwarf Andvari in Svartálfaheimr. These episodes show subterranean beings crafting powerful artifacts in a dark realm. While Dökkálfar are not named, many modern discussions treat these stories as illustrative of the broader ‘dark elf’ complex.

Content Notes

Dökkálfar dwell ‘within the earth’, a phrase that can evoke burial mounds, graves, and the underworld. In Norse cosmology, subterranean beings (dwarfs, some elves) are linked to death, decay, and transformation. While Dökkálfar are not explicitly described as necromantic or as tormentors of the dead, their placement in the earth suggests a dark, chthonic dimension that may have been unsettling or taboo.

Scholars emphasize that Norse myth often associates underground realms with both danger and creative power (e.g., dwarfs as maggots transformed from Ymir’s corpse). Dökkálfar may participate in this ambivalent, death-linked symbolism.

Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 17; comparative discussion in Simek 2007 and Orchard 1997.

The light/dark elf distinction appears in a Christianized 13th‑century text. Anne Holtsmark and others have argued that Snorri’s description of higher heavens and light-elves draws on Christian angelology (angels of light vs. darkness). This framing can implicitly moralize darkness as negative, contributing to later associations of Dökkálfar with evil or demonic traits.

The hypothesis of Christian influence is widely discussed but not universally accepted. Scholars note that dualism between good and evil is not unique to Christianity, yet the specific imagery of multiple heavens and light-beings resembles Christian texts such as the Elucidarius.

Holtsmark’s studies on Snorri and Christian influence (exact article details not fully verified); Simek 2007; Lindow 2001.

In modern fantasy, ‘dark elves’ (often inspired by Norse Dökkálfar) are frequently depicted as inherently evil, sadistic, or racially coded others—sometimes with dark skin contrasted against ‘fair’ elves. These portrayals can involve slavery, torture, and genocidal conflict, especially in role‑playing games and novels. While not part of medieval Norse tradition, they represent a dark afterlife of the concept, entangled with modern racism and misogyny.

Scholars of fantasy and race critique how ‘dark’ races are constructed as violent and morally corrupt, drawing on long histories of color symbolism and colonial ideology. The Norse Dökkálfar are often cited as a distant mythic ancestor of these tropes, though the medieval sources themselves are sparse and non-racial in the modern sense.

Contemporary fantasy franchises (e.g., Dungeons & Dragons drow; various novels and games); critical discussions in modern scholarship on fantasy and race.

What We Don't Know

Major gaps include: (1) extremely sparse primary attestations—Dökkálfar appear only briefly in Gylfaginning and in a disputed poem, leaving their nature, stories, and cultic role largely unknown; (2) lack of archaeological or ritual evidence specifically tied to Dökkálfar, as opposed to broader álfar traditions; (3) uncertainty about the extent of pre‑Christian light/dark elf duality versus Snorri’s systematization; (4) limited access to some specialized scholarship (e.g., detailed works by Anne Holtsmark and other Scandinavian scholars) in this synthesis, which may refine or challenge interpretations; (5) no direct medieval narratives showing Dökkálfar interacting with humans or gods, making all reconstructions of their behavior and powers highly tentative; (6) incomplete documentation of modern popular culture uses of ‘dark elves’, which are numerous and diverse.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the Prose Edda, the cosmos is sketched in bold strokes: radiant halls, roaring wolves, and worlds stacked like storm clouds. Amid this architecture of gods and giants, Snorri pauses to mention beings who live ‘within the earth’, blacker than pitch and behaving quite unlike their sun-bright kin. These are the Dökkálfar—the dark elves—barely more than a shadow in the surviving texts, yet powerful enough as an idea to seed centuries of speculation, fantasy races, and moral tales about what lurks beneath the bright surface of the world.
