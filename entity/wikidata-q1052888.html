Who It Is

In the twilight of Athens' legendary kings, a monarch chose to die in disguise so that his city might live. Codrus, last of the semi-mythical line, slipped into an enemy camp as a peasant, provoked a fatal quarrel, and sealed his people's salvation with his own blood. His sacrifice was so profound that the Athenians, it is said, abolished the kingship itself, for no one felt worthy to follow him.

He is portrayed as a model of patriotic self-sacrifice, willing to die for his city. Lycurgus presents him as a paradigm of virtue. His cunning (disguise) and courage are emphasized.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Codrus (Greek: Κόδρος) is of uncertain etymology; it may be pre-Greek or connected to an obscure root. No definitive derivation is attested.

Pronunciation — KOD-rus (English), KO-dros (reconstructed Ancient Greek)

Aliases — Kodros, Κόδρος

Ownership — Ancient Greek, specifically Athenian; later appropriated by Roman and European traditions as an exemplar of patriotism.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No physical description survives in ancient sources. Later artistic depictions show a bearded king in heroic nudity or armor, but these are imaginative.

Symbols — Codrus appears in Greek vase painting, often in scenes of his death. He may be depicted as a king with a diadem or as a peasant. No fixed symbol is attested.

Domains — Athens; his story is set during the Dorian invasion, at the border between Athens and the Peloponnese.

Accounts I
The Self-Sacrifice of CodrusLycurgus, Against Leocrates; later versions in Pausanias, Suda, etc.

c. 1068 BC (legendary date)

During the Dorian invasion, the Delphic oracle prophesied that the Dorians would succeed if they spared the Athenian king. Codrus, learning of this, disguised himself as a peasant, provoked a quarrel with Dorian soldiers, and was killed. The Dorians, realizing they had killed the king, retreated, and Athens was saved. In gratitude, the Athenians abolished the kingship, deeming no one worthy to succeed Codrus. Athens was saved; the monarchy was replaced by the archonship, with Codrus's son Medon as first archon.
