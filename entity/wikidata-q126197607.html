Who It Is

Imagine a name that built the Colossi of Memnon, launched the world's first (and most controversial) monotheistic revolution, and then got chiseled off every monument in Egypt — all in the space of three generations. That name is Amenophis, the Greek whisper of an Egyptian title that means 'Amun is satisfied.' But the gods of Egypt were anything but satisfied. When one Amenophis decided the sun was the only god, he started a war with the priesthood that ended with his own name erased from history, his city abandoned to the desert, and his son — the boy king Tutankhamun — left to clean up the mess.

Behavior varies by individual. Amenhotep III was a diplomat and monumental builder — the 'Magnificent.' Amenhotep IV/Akhenaten was a religious revolutionary who suppressed the cult of Amun and promoted the Aten, the sun disk. Amenhotep I was a military leader who expelled the Hyksos' successors from Nubia and established the Valley of the Kings. The name alone does not define behavior.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Amenophis' is the Greek form of the ancient Egyptian name 'Imn-htp' (ꜣmn-ḥtp), meaning 'Amun is satisfied' or 'Amun is content.' Aliases — Amenhotep, Amenophis, Amunhotep, Imn-htp

Ownership — Ancient Egypt (New Kingdom period, 18th Dynasty). The name was borne by several pharaohs and high officials, most notably Amenhotep I, Amenhotep II, Amenhotep III, and Amenhotep IV

Powers
Kingship (Pharaonic Power)

As pharaoh, each Amenophis was the living Horus, the intermediary between the gods and the people. This included the power to command armies, levy taxes, build monuments, and maintain ma'at (cosmic order).

Limit: Limited by the power of the priesthood, the nobility, and the vagaries of the Nile flood. Akhenaten's power was absolute in theory but eroded by his religious reforms and neglect of foreign affairs.

Divine Status (Posthumous Cult)

Amenhotep I was deified after his death, becoming a protector god of the Theban workforce. He was believed to have the power to answer prayers, grant oracles, and protect the dead.

Limit: His power was localized to the Theban necropolis region and focused on the workmen of Deir el-Medina.

Religious Authority (Atenism)

Akhenaten (Amenhotep IV) claimed a unique prophetic relationship with the Aten, the solar disk, and wielded the power to establish a new state religion, build a new capital (Akhetaten/Amarna), and suppress the traditional pantheon.

Limit: His religious revolution did not survive his death; his successors (Tutankhamun, Ay, Horemheb) restored the old cults and actively erased his name and monuments.

Form & Signs

Appearance — As a generic name, there is no single appearance. Amenhotep III is depicted as a youthful, powerful ruler in his early colossi, and later as a more ponderous, divinized figure. Amenhotep IV/Akhenaten is famously depicted with an androgynous, elongated face, narrow eyes, full lips, a protruding belly, and wide hips — a radical departure from traditional pharaonic iconography. Symbols — The name is a cartouche name, symbolizing kingship. The hieroglyphic components — the reed (j), the mn (gameboard) and the htp (offering table) — are symbols of stability and satisfaction. Amenhotep III's Horus name 'Nebma'at' ('Lord of Truth') and his self-identification with the sun are iconic. Akhenaten's symbols include the Aten disk with radiating hands.

Domains — The name is a royal throne name, not a deity's domain. However, Amenhotep I was deified after his death and became a patron of the Theban necropolis, with a cult and oracle. Amenhotep III was celebrated as a living god, identified with the sun. Amenhotep IV/Akhenaten's domain was the sole worship of the Aten.

Accounts I
The Colossi of MemnonGreek and Roman tourists (e.g., Strabo, Pausanias); modern archaeology

c. 27 BCE – 4th century CE (tourist phenomenon); 18th Dynasty (original construction)

The two colossal statues of Amenhotep III at Thebes were damaged in an earthquake in 27 BCE. Afterward, the northern statue was said to emit a musical sound at dawn, a phenomenon attributed to the legendary Ethiopian hero Memnon greeting his mother Eos (the Dawn). The statue was known as the 'Vocal Memnon' and became a famous tourist attraction. Roman emperors and poets visited to hear the sound. The statue was repaired in 199 CE by Septimius Severus, and the sound ceased. The identification of the statue with Memnon is a classic example of Graeco-Roman reinterpretation of Egyptian monuments. It demonstrates the cultural 'translation' of a pharaoh into a Homeric hero.

Accounts II
Manetho's 'Amenophis' and the ExodusManetho (Aegyptiaca), via Josephus (Contra Apionem)

3rd century BCE (original); 1st century CE (preserved)

Josephus preserves a fragment of Manetho's history in which a pharaoh named 'Amenophis' is deceived by a priest named Osarseph (later identified as Moses). The pharaoh is tricked into expelling lepers and polluted people from Egypt, who then ally with the Hyksos and wreak havoc. This account is a hostile Egyptian counter-narrative to the biblical Exodus story. This text has been used both to support and to deny the historicity of the Exodus. It shows how Egyptian history was adapted into polemical narratives.

The Amarna RevolutionThe Amarna Letters, boundary stelae of Akhetaten, later king lists (erasure of Akhenaten's name)

c. 1353–1336 BCE

Amenhotep IV changed his name to Akhenaten ('Effective for the Aten') and initiated a religious revolution, moving the capital to a new city at Amarna, closing temples of other gods, and redirecting all state resources to the worship of the Aten. His wife Nefertiti played a prominent role. After his death, the old order was restored. The Amarna period was later called 'the heresy' by Egyptians. Akhenaten's name was chiseled from monuments, and his city was abandoned. The period remains one of the most studied and debated in Egyptology.

Amenophis is a single, specific pharaoh. — The name was borne by at least four pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty, plus officials and priests. Without a numeral or other context, the name is ambiguous.

Akhenaten (Amenhotep IV) was the 'first monotheist.' — This is a popular but contested claim. His religion was focused on the Aten, but it was not the same as Jewish, Christian, or Islamic monotheism. The Aten was a physical entity, and the royal family held a special divine status.

The Colossi of Memnon were built by the Greek hero Memnon. — They were built by Amenhotep III, centuries before the Greek hero Memnon was associated with them. The identification is a Graeco-Roman misinterpretation.

Manetho's story of 'Amenophis' is a historical account of the Exodus. — It is a polemical narrative written centuries after the events, likely as a counter-claim to the Jewish Exodus story. It is not considered a reliable historical source by modern scholars.
