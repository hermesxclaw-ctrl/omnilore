The Approach

In the darkness before time, the Muisca of the Colombian Andes believed there existed only one radiant being—Chiminigagua—whose very body held the light of creation. When she opened her belly and released two black birds, they carried the first light into the cosmos, birthing the sun, the moon, and all that exists. This is the story of a supreme deity whose worship shaped a civilization, and whose temples were consumed by the flames of conquest.

Content advisory. Contains descriptions of colonial destruction of sacred sites and religious suppression.
Identity

Chiminigagua

Pronunciation Reconstructed as *[ʂiminiɣaɣua]

Known As

ChiminichaguaChimichagua

Etymology

The name is from the Muisca language, with a reconstructed pronunciation *[ʂiminiɣaɣua]. The exact meaning is not definitively established, but it is associated with the supreme creative force.

Cultural Ownership

Muisca civilization, Altiplano Cundiboyacense, present-day Colombia

Disputed Identifications

Some early chroniclers and later scholars have drawn parallels with other creator deities (e.g., Viracocha, Tezcatlipoca), but these are comparative interpretations, not direct identifications. There is no evidence of a shared origin or syncretism.

Description

Appearance

Chiminigagua is described as a 'static deity without body'—she had no physical form. She was conceptualized as pure light and creative force.

Symbols & Iconography

The Sun and Moon (Sué and Chía) were the primary symbols of Chiminigagua's power. The Sun Temple at Sogamoso was a major sanctuary. The 'Cojines del zaque' (Zaque's Pillows) were ritual stones associated with solar worship.

Behaviour & Personality

Chiminigagua was universally good, benevolent, and omnipotent. She was not directly worshipped but venerated through lesser deities, indicating a transcendent, impersonal nature.

Domains & Habitats

She resided in the cosmos, particularly associated with the heavens. Her sanctuaries on Earth included Sugamuxi (Sogamoso), Guatavita, Bacatá (Bogotá), Guachetá, and Hunza (Tunja).

Version Differences

Chroniclers Simón and Castellanos provide the earliest accounts; later interpretations may vary. Some sources refer to Chiminigagua with masculine pronouns, but the name itself is not gendered. The description of her 'opening her belly' is a creation metaphor, not a literal anatomical reference.

Destruction of Sun Temple

Source Historical accounts of the Spanish conquest

Description

Spanish conquistadors, led by Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada, burned the sacred Sun Temple at Sogamoso, destroying one of the most important religious structures of the Muisca.

Scholarly Context

This event is part of the broader destruction of indigenous cultures during European colonization.

Contradictions & Disputes

Gender of Chiminigagua

Position A

Some sources refer to Chiminigagua with feminine pronouns (e.g., 'she').

Position A — evidence

The name itself is not gendered; descriptions in modern secondary sources often use 'she'.

Position B

Others use masculine or neutral terms.

Position B — evidence

Early chroniclers may have used masculine because of European gender norms.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; the deity is often considered genderless or beyond gender.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Limited access to primary sources in original Muisca language; most knowledge comes from Spanish colonial chronicles. Further research into museum collections and archaeological findings at Sogamoso could provide additional context. The exact nature of rituals and the full pantheon hierarchy remain partially unknown.
