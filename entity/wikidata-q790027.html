Form

Not described in surviving texts with physical detail. In folk art and modern interpretation, she is often depicted as a beautiful young maiden with a radiant, golden or rosy complexion, holding a torch or crown of light. She is the bringer of the morning light, scattering the darkness and dew.

Powers

Bringer of Dawn Her primary power is to initiate the sunrise. She lights the fire for the Sun, or opens the gates of the sky, dispelling the darkness. Common to all versions of the myth. She does not control the sun's path; she only prepares its way. None significant.

Dispeller of Darkness and Evil Her light is said to drive away evil spirits, nightmares, and the dangers of the night. She is a purifying and protective force. Implied in folk beliefs and greeting rituals. Her power is confined to the morning hours. None.

Protector of Lovers As the morning star, she is a witness to love and a patron of secret meetings. She is invoked for luck in love. Inferred from folk songs and customs. This is a more romanticized, folkloric aspect rather than a clearly defined mythic power. None.

Accounts

Aušrinė is depicted as the first to awaken. She rises, lights a fire, and prepares the Sun's chariot or golden crown. She then accompanies Saulė on her journey across the sky, or returns to rest after her task is complete.

At dawn, Saulė bathes in the sea or a lake. Aušrinė is often present, holding a towel or a golden cup to catch the water, or lighting the way for her. This is a moment of sacred renewal.

Aušrinė is identified with the planet Venus when it appears in the morning sky. She is the 'Morning Star', a brilliant herald of the day. Her appearance is a signal for the world to wake.

Scholarly Debates
Relationship to Saulė
Aušrinė is the Sun's daughter. ⇌ Aušrinė is the Sun's servant or handmaiden.
Evidence for A: Some dainos explicitly call her Saulė's daughter.
Evidence for B: Other songs describe her as a subordinate preparing the Sun's way, without a familial title.
Identity with Vakarinė
Aušrinė and Vakarinė are the same goddess, seen at different times. ⇌ They are two separate sisters or deities.
Evidence for A: Both are identified with the planet Venus in modern scholarship.
Evidence for B: Some folk songs treat them as distinct characters.
Gender of Auštaras
Auštaras is a male deity, possibly the brother of Aušrinė. ⇌ Auštaras is a variant name for Aušrinė herself.
Evidence for A: The masculine grammatical form of the name.
Evidence for B: Lack of clear mythic material for a separate male figure.
What We Don't Know

The primary gap is the lack of extensive, pre-Christian written records. Our knowledge relies on later chronicles, folk songs, and archaeological inference. The exact nature of her cult, her temples (if any), and her formal rituals are unknown. The relationship with Auštaras and Vakarinė requires further study. A deeper analysis of all available dainos for specific epithets and narrative details would be valuable.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Aušrinė as the ultimate cosmic assistant manager. Her job is to make sure the biggest star in the sky gets to work on time. She's the one who wakes up early, starts the coffee, and holds the flashlight so the Sun can find its keys. She's the personification of that quiet, reliable, and brilliant moment of potential that exists just before the world fully wakes up.
