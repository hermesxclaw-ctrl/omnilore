The Approach

She has no face you can name and no myth where she speaks. In the hill country of Kizzuwatna, when impurity clung to a house and dreams turned foul, priests carried her image into darkness, dug a pit to the underworld, and whispered in Hurrian to the Goddess of the Night — a deity whose true name was already lost to her Hittite translators, remembered only as DINGIR.GE6, the Deity of Night.

Content advisory. Ritual animal sacrifice and blood manipulation; sexual impotence and ritual sexuality; impurity/pollution concepts; underworld and chthonic imagery; no graphic mythic violence attested
Identity

Goddess of the Night

Pronunciation English /ˌɡɒdɛs əv ðə ˈnaɪt/; logogram conventionally read aloud as Hittite išpantariš šiunaš or Akkadian ilat muši; Hurrian pronunciation unknown

Known As

DINGIR.GE6DINGIR GE6ilat muši (Akkadian calque)Dea Nocturna (modern scholarly Latin)Goddess of the Night of Kizzuwatna

Etymology

Not a proper name but a descriptive title. Written with Sumerogram DINGIR.GE6 literally 'deity of the night' (Sumerian GE6 = mušu 'night'). Hittite scribes read it as Hittite išpant- 'night' (likely išpantariš DINGIR-LIM) or Akkadian ilat muši. The underlying Hurrian name, if there was one, was not preserved in the Hittite transmission; the ritual texts are Hittite translations/adaptations of Hurrian Kizzuwatnean Vorlagen with embedded Hurrian incantations.

Cultural Ownership

Hurrian religious tradition of the kingdom of Kizzuwatna (classical Cilicia, centered on Kummanni/Comana, southeastern Anatolia, ca. 16th-13th century BCE). Adopted into the Hittite state pantheon at Hattusa after Kizzuwatna's incorporation into the Hittite sphere, where her rituals were curated by Kizzuwatnean ritual experts. Belongs to the Hurrian-Hittite cultural sphere; not Greek, Egyptian, or Mesoamerican despite English homonymy.

Disputed Identifications

1) Often conflated in popular lists with Nyx (Greek primordial night), Nut (Egyptian sky/night), and Oxomoco (Aztec). These are unrelated deities sharing an English gloss. 2) Scholarly dispute whether DINGIR.GE6 is an independent goddess, a local hypostasis of Šawuška (Hurrian Ištar), or identical with Pirinkir (another Hurro-Hittite Ištar-like goddess). Miller and Beckman argue for functional overlap but ritual distinctness; Haas and Wegner lean toward Šawuška-circle. 3) Distinguished from Hittite moon-god Arma and sun-goddess of Arinna; she is not a lunar deity. 4) Not to be confused with the modern Destiny-class cruise ship Goddess of the Night or the Daughters of the Moon novel volume.

Description

Appearance

No securely identified anthropomorphic image survives. The ritual texts describe handling of her cult image/statuette (Hittite ešri-, ALAM) which is bathed, anointed, clothed, veiled, and carried in procession, then returned to her temple or shrine. The image is treated as animate and susceptible to pollution, requiring purification. One passage describes drawing a curtain or veil before her. No textual physical description (hair, clothing color, attributes) is preserved beyond that she receives offerings like other Hurrian goddesses. Modern artistic reconstructions showing a star-cloaked woman are inventions without textual basis. Attested: that she had a portable image; reconstructed: that image likely followed Hurrian goddess iconography (long robe, horned crown); unknown: facial features, symbols held.

Symbols & Iconography

Symbols are ritual rather than iconographic: night/darkness itself, the pit (abi) dug to the underworld, the curtain/veil, the brazier and incense, wool threads (black, white, red), figurines of the patient, and offerings of birds, sheep and bread. Stars and crescent are NOT attested as her attributes in texts, though sometimes assumed by modern writers conflating her with Ištar/Shawushka. Her association is with darkness as a liminal time when impurity can be transferred, not with astral iconography.

Behaviour & Personality

Not a mythic actor with personality traits like Greek Nyx. In ritual logic she is powerful, ambivalent, and mediatory: invoked to witness oaths, to receive impurity, to intercede with underworld deities (Allani, Hutena-Hutellura), and to grant favor or remove affliction. She is approached with elaborate respect and fear; the Old Woman (MUNUS ŠU.GI) ritualist speaks to her in Hurrian, suggesting esoteric, non-Hittite knowledge. She is neither benevolent mother nor hostile demon but a potent chthonic-nocturnal power who must be properly evoked, fed, and sent back.

Domains & Habitats

Domain is night (išpant-) as a temporal and ritual space, plus purification, magic (alwanzatar), dream and incubation, and mediation between the upper world and the underworld. Habitat in ritual is twofold: her temple/shrine in Kizzuwatna (and a chapel at Hattusa) and the temporary ritual locus — a tent or curtained enclosure erected for the rite, with a pit opening to the netherworld. She is also associated with the road and the wilderness where impurity is expelled. Not a sky goddess or moon goddess; her night is earthly darkness and underworld darkness.

Version Differences

Kizzuwatnean original (Hurrian) vs. Hattusa adaptation (Hittite): Hurrian incantations retained but Hittite ritual frame added; some manuscripts show expanded Hittite instructions. CTH 482 exists in multiple recensions with varying length and sequence, indicating living ritual adaptation over ~150 years. CTH 481 appears to be a shorter evocatio variant. In Paškuwatti's ritual (CTH 406) she appears in a more specialized role concerning sexuality and virility, possibly reflecting Arzawan syncretism. Later Hittite offering lists show her integrated into the official pantheon alongside Hebat, Teššub and Šawuška, whereas earlier texts treat her as a distinctly Kizzuwatnean import.

Powers & Abilities

Nocturnal epiphany and evocation

Source VersionCTH 481, CTH 482 — Hattusa recensions

Can be summoned (evoked) from her temple to the ritual locus at night and subsequently dismissed/sent back. Presence is signaled by ritual efficacy, not by narrative epiphany.

Limitations

Requires correct Hurrian incantations, night-time performance, and a qualified Old Woman; failure to dismiss properly risks lingering impurity

Contradictions

Whether evocation is literal travel or ritual activation is debated

Purification and transfer of impurity

Source VersionCTH 482 main ritual; Strauß 2006

Core power: to absorb or receive impurity (papratar), evil, curse, and pollution from patient, house, or army, which is then transferred to substitute figurines, animals, or wool and expelled to the steppe or underworld.

Limitations

Not automatic; requires elaborate offerings, washing, and scapegoat-like transfer; impurity can rebound if rite is flawed

Contradictions

None; central consensus

Mediation with the underworld

Source VersionCTH 482 pit ritual; Haas 1994

Acts as intermediary to chthonic deities — Allani (underworld queen), Hutena and Hutellura (fate goddesses), and ancestral spirits — via the pit (abi). Messages and offerings pass through her.

Limitations

Does not rule the underworld herself; she opens access but does not command the dead

Contradictions

Some scholars see her as underworld goddess herself; Miller argues she is liminal, not chthonic proper

Oneiromancy and dream incubation

Source VersionCTH 482 dream section; CTH 406

Night association implies power over dreams; ritual patient sleeps in purified space after rite to receive favorable dream or relief. Paškuwatti ritual links her to sexual dreams and potency.

Limitations

No explicit 'sends dreams' myth; inference from ritual context and night domain

Contradictions

Taracha notes dream function is reconstructed, not explicitly stated

Protection and favor (pangur?)

Source VersionCTH 482 concluding blessings

If properly honored with food, drink, and jewelry offerings, she grants protection from witchcraft, enemy curses, and divine anger; withdrawal of favor leaves patient exposed.

Limitations

Favor is conditional and temporary; requires periodic re-performance

Contradictions

None

Association with magic and witchcraft counteraction

Source VersionHaas 1994; Beckman 1999

Classified by Hittitologists as a deity of 'black magic' and counter-magic (alwanzatar). Her ritual is used against sorcery-induced illness.

Limitations

Not a sorceress herself; she counters human witchcraft

Contradictions

Modern popular sources mislabel her as 'goddess of witchcraft' as if she teaches spells

Stories & Myths

The Great Ritual of the Goddess of the Night (CTH 482) — Purification of a House and Person

Source CTH 482 composite text (KUB 29.7 etc.)Culture Hurrian (Kizzuwatna) via HittitePeriod Late Bronze Age, 14th-13th c. BCE

Summary

The most complete narrative: A patron (king, queen, or elite) whose household is polluted commissions the Old Woman. At night, a curtained enclosure is erected, a pit is dug to the underworld, and the goddess's image is brought in procession. Offerings of sheep, birds, bread, honey, oil and wine are made. Hurrian incantations are recited. Impurity is transferred onto wool threads tied to the patient and onto clay figurines representing the patient and family. The figurines and threads are placed in the pit or sent to the wilderness. The patient is washed, anointed, and made to sleep in the purified space to await a favorable dream. The goddess is then reverently returned to her temple with gifts. The text gives stage directions, incantations, and lists of deities invoked as witnesses.

Participants

Goddess of the Night (DINGIR.GE6)

Old Woman (MUNUS ŠU.GI) ritualist

Patron (EN.SISKUR)

Allani, Hutena-Hutellura, Teššub, Hebat (witness deities)

Substitute figurines and scapegoat animals

Consequences

Model for Kizzuwatnean purification magic; demonstrates Hurrian ritual technology adopted as Hittite state ritual; source for understanding Hittite concepts of impurity and substitution

Variants

Multiple manuscripts (A, B, C) differ in length, order of offerings, and Hurrian incantation variants; Miller identifies an older Kizzuwatna layer and a younger Hattusa redaction

The Evocatio of the Goddess of the Night (CTH 481)

Source CTH 481 (KUB 32.130 etc.)Culture Hurro-HittitePeriod 13th c. BCE

Summary

A shorter, more focused rite whose core is the formal summoning and sending-back of the goddess. The Old Woman calls her from Kizzuwatna/Kummanni to Hattusa, offers her a new residence (tent/shrine), feeds her, and asks her to take away evil that has been brought by an enemy or by witchcraft. The goddess is entreated not to be angry and to intercede with the fate goddesses. The rite ends with a formal dismissal and road provisions for her return journey.

Participants

Goddess of the Night

Old Woman

Fate goddesses Hutena-Hutellura

Enemy/sorcerer as source of evil

Consequences

Shows mobility of deities in Hittite thought; deities can be invited to travel; illustrates Kizzuwatna-Hattusa cult transfer

Variants

Fragmentary; relationship to CTH 482 debated — possibly excerpt or separate ritual for same goddess

Paškuwatti's Ritual Against Sexual Impotence (CTH 406)

Source KUB 9.27 + KUB 9.28Culture Arzawan/Luwian with Kizzuwatnean deityPeriod 14th c. BCE

Summary

Paškuwatti, a woman ritualist from Arzawa, invokes the Goddess of the Night among other deities to cure a man's impotence attributed to sorcery or divine anger. The ritual involves sympathetic magic: spindle and distaff (female tools) are held by the patient to symbolize his unmanliness, then replaced with bow and arrows (male tools) as potency is restored. The Goddess of the Night is asked to remove the evil that binds his virility, with offerings and Hurrian spells. The patient is passed through a gate of reeds and washed.

Participants

Goddess of the Night

Paškuwatti (MUNUS ŠU.GI)

Impotent male patient

Uliliyassi (possibly related goddess)

Sorcerer who caused affliction

Consequences

Important evidence for Hittite attitudes to sexuality, gender symbolism, and magic; shows Goddess of the Night's role beyond house purification into personal physiology

Variants

Two duplicate manuscripts with minor variations in incantations

The Goddess of the Night in Hurrian Offering Lists — The Divine Assembly at Hattusa

Source KUB 27.1 and parallel offering lists (KUB 45.21, KBo 20.73)Culture Hurro-Hittite official cultPeriod 13th c. BCE

Summary

Not a narrative myth but a liturgical sequence: in state festivals, the Goddess of the Night receives offerings in fixed order among the Hurrian pantheon — after Teššub, Hebat, Šawuška and before Hutena-Hutellura and Allani. The list prescribes one sheep, bread and beer libation. Her placement reveals her rank and theological grouping: clustered with Ištar-type and underworld goddesses, indicating her liminal status. The scribes note her Kizzuwatnean origin.

Participants

Goddess of the Night

Teššub, Hebat, Šawuška, Hutena-Hutellura, Allani

Hittite king as offerant

Hurrian singer (LÚ NAR hurli)

Consequences

Demonstrates integration of Kizzuwatnean deity into Hittite state religion; helps reconstruct Hurrian pantheon structure

Variants

Different lists vary order slightly; some omit her, suggesting local vs. capital cult differences

The Purification of the Army and the King (Kizzuwatna-type arkuwar ritual referencing DINGIR.GE6)

Source Cult inventories and arkuwar (plea) rituals: KUB 38.12, KUB 30.34 + KUB 39.71Culture Hittite-KizzuwatneanPeriod 13th c. BCE

Summary

Fragmentary rituals where the Goddess of the Night is invoked to purify the Hittite army before campaign or the king after ill-omened dreams. The ritual logic mirrors CTH 482 but on a collective scale: impurity contracted from battlefield corpses or enemy sorcery is transferred to prisoners or animals led to the border. The goddess is asked to 'stand before' the king in the night and turn away the evil. The text breaks off but parallels the house ritual.

Participants

Goddess of the Night

Hittite king and troops

Old Woman or AZU priest

Enemy dead as source of impurity

Consequences

Shows extension of private Kizzuwatnean magic to state and military sphere

Variants

Very fragmentary; reconstruction relies on parallel CTH 482 structure

Modern Scholarly Re-discovery — From Hattusa Tablets to Miller's Reconstruction

Source Excavation reports of Hattusa (1906-), publication of KUB/KBo, and Miller 2004 monographCulture Modern Assyriology/HittitologyPeriod 1906-2004 CE (modern reception as story)

Summary

After millennia of oblivion, German excavations at Boğazköy uncovered thousands of cuneiform tablets. For decades DINGIR.GE6 was an enigmatic logogram. Volkert Haas identified her Kizzuwatnean Hurrian origin in the 1970s-90s, Gary Beckman highlighted her distinct ritual profile, and Jared Miller's 2004 critical edition finally assembled all fragments of CTH 482 into a coherent ritual sequence, revealing a complete Hurrian-Hittite purification system. This modern philological detective story restored the goddess to history.

Participants

Hugo Winckler (excavator)

Volkert Haas, Gary Beckman, Jared L. Miller, Rita Strauß, Piotr Taracha (philologists)

Museum curators

Consequences

Established Goddess of the Night as key case study for cultural transfer between Hurrians and Hittites; corrected earlier conflation with Šawuška

Variants

Interpretive differences remain between Miller's and Strauß's reconstructions of tablet joins

Relationships
Goddess of the Night→Šawuška (Hurrian Ištar, Hittite Šauška)ally

Most debated relationship. Both are Hurrian goddesses with overlapping domains (night, sexuality, magic) and both receive similar offerings. Some offering lists place them adjacent. Haas and Wegner suggest DINGIR.GE6 is a local form or hypostasis of Šawuška; Beckman and Miller argue ritual texts treat them as distinct — Šawuška has her own separate rituals and the Goddess of the Night is never called Šawuška in CTH 482. The Old Woman invokes both separately in some festivals.

Goddess of the Night→Hutena and Hutellura (Hurrian fate goddesses)ally

Pair of Hurrian goddesses of fate and birth, regularly invoked immediately after the Goddess of the Night in CTH 482 and in the pit ritual. The Goddess of the Night mediates access to them; they are asked to spin favorable fates after impurity is removed. They share chthonic and nocturnal associations.

Goddess of the Night→Allani (Hurrian queen of the underworld, Hittite Allatum)ally

Underworld goddess to whom the pit (abi) leads. The Goddess of the Night's ritual opens a pit to Allani's realm; offerings are sent down to Allani through the goddess's mediation. Allani is not subordinate but is the recipient of the expelled impurity.

Goddess of the Night→Hebat and Teššub (Hurrian queen and storm-god, heads of Hurrian pantheon)ally

Supreme Hurrian divine couple who head the offering lists that include the Goddess of the Night. She is part of their extended Kizzuwatnean court but not their child. The king's purification ultimately restores his relationship to Teššub.

Goddess of the Night→The Old Woman (MUNUS ŠU.GI / MUNUS hazqara) — Kizzuwatnean ritual expertworshipper

Human ritualist, always female, who alone knows the Hurrian incantations and procedures to summon and dismiss the goddess. She acts as intermediary between patron and goddess, handling the image, reciting spells, and manipulating substitute figurines. The goddess's power is only accessible through this specialist's knowledge.

Goddess of the Night→The Patron (EN.SISKUR — ritual client, often king/queen or elite householder)worshipper

Human beneficiary whose impurity, illness, or dream-affliction motivates the ritual. The patron funds the offerings, provides the house to be purified, and undergoes washing and dream incubation. Relationship is contractual: patron gives offerings, goddess removes evil.

Goddess of the Night→Pirinkir (Hurrian goddess, also Ištar-like)hostile

Another Hurrian goddess sometimes equated with DINGIR.GE6 in older scholarship (Gurney, Goetze). Pirinkir appears in similar offering contexts and also has astral aspects. Modern consensus separates them, but the confusion persists in encyclopedias.

Goddess of the Night→Arma (Hittite moon-god) and Ishara (oath goddess)ally

Nocturnal deities who share the night domain but are distinct. Arma governs the moon; Ishara governs oaths. The Goddess of the Night is invoked alongside them in some purification contexts but is not lunar. Confusion arises from English 'night' gloss.

Animal sacrifice and blood manipulation

Source CTH 482 KUB 29.7 i 30-45; Strauß 2006

Description

Ritual prescribes slaughter of sheep and birds, smearing of blood on patient and figurines, and burning of fat. Blood is a purifying and transfer medium. This is standard Bronze Age ritual violence, not mythic cruelty.

Scholarly Context

Haas and Miller contextualize as Hurrian substitution theology; not gratuitous but central to impurity transfer logic; modern readers may find graphic

Sexuality, impotence and gendered humiliation magic

Source KUB 9.27

Description

Paškuwatti's ritual (CTH 406) treats male impotence as caused by sorcery and cures it by ritually feminizing then re-masculinizing the patient (spindle/distaff vs. bow/arrows). The text describes the patient's shame and uses gendered objects to manipulate virility. Contains frank discussion of sexual function and ritual transvestism symbolism.

Scholarly Context

Hoffner and Miller note this reflects Hittite anxieties about masculinity and witchcraft; not sexual violence but ritual handling of sexuality that may be sensitive

Warning: Impurity, scapegoating and expulsion to the wilderness — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Impurity, scapegoating and expulsion to the wilderness

Source CTH 482

Description

Impurity (papratar) is treated as a quasi-physical substance transferred to living substitutes (figurines, animals) that are then expelled to the steppe or sent into the underworld pit, effectively condemning the substitute. This reflects Bronze Age notions of collective pollution and social exclusion.

Scholarly Context

Strauß compares to Levitical scapegoat and Hittite nakkušši- rituals; scholarly debate on whether this is 'dark magic' or normative purification

Warning: Colonial and interpretive distortion — erasure of Hurrian identity — Show unsanitized · Archive policy & legal

Colonial and interpretive distortion — erasure of Hurrian identity

Source Miller 2004 introduction; Haas 1994

Description

Early Hittitology labeled all Hattusa rituals 'Hittite,' erasing Kizzuwatnean Hurrian authorship. The Goddess of the Night was long filed as a 'Hittite' deity, obscuring Hurrian cultural ownership and the Old Women's expertise. Modern scholarship corrects this but popular sources still misattribute.

Scholarly Context

Post-colonial critique in Anatolian studies emphasizes Kizzuwatna as Hurrian cultural center, not mere Hittite province

Contradictions & Disputes

Identity: Is DINGIR.GE6 a form of Šawuška/Ištar or an independent goddess?

Position A

She is a local Kizzuwatnean hypostasis of Šawuška (Hurrian Ištar) — same goddess under a descriptive title

Position A — evidence

Shared offering contexts (KUB 27.1 adjacent listings), overlapping domains (night, sexuality, magic), similar jewelry/clothing offerings, and Ištar's known epithet 'Lady of the Night' in Mesopotamia; Haas 1994, Wegner 1995

Position B

She is a distinct goddess in the circle of Šawuška but with separate cult, personnel and ritual texts

Position B — evidence

CTH 482 never calls her Šawuška; Šawuška has separate rituals (CTH 481? vs. CTH 700 series); Miller 2004 shows distinct Old Woman traditions and that Hattusa scribes kept their archives separate; Beckman 1999 notes different Hurrian incantation corpora

Scholarly Consensus

Current majority (Miller, Beckman, Strauß, Taracha) favors distinct but closely related deities within the Ištar-circle; older handbooks favored identification; issue remains open due to lack of Hurrian name

Nature: Chthonic underworld goddess vs. liminal mediator

Position A

She is an underworld goddess ruling part of the netherworld, akin to Allani

Position A — evidence

Pit (abi) ritual, association with Allani and Hutena-Hutellura, night as underworld darkness; Haas

Position B

She is a liminal/mediator who opens access to the underworld but dwells in her temple/heaven, not below

Position B — evidence

Text says pit is dug 'for' the underworld deities, not that she lives there; she is evoked from Kizzuwatna temple and returns there; Miller's spatial analysis

Scholarly Consensus

Mediatory/liminal view now preferred, but both describe her as chthonic-adjacent

Lunar vs. nocturnal character

Position A

Popular and some older scholarly sources call her a moon goddess

Position A — evidence

English gloss 'night' invites lunar association; Pirinkir confusion (Pirinkir has astral aspects)

Position B

She is goddess of night-time/darkness, not the moon; Hittites had separate moon-god Arma

Position B — evidence

No text links her to moon (Arma), stars, or calendar; ritual is timed to night for purity reasons, not lunar phase; Taracha 2009 explicitly denies lunar character

Scholarly Consensus

Strong consensus: not lunar; night = darkness/ritual time, not celestial body

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

Hurrian original name of the goddess unknown — all forms are Sumerographic/Akkadian/Hittite glosses. No securely identified iconography, cult statue, or temple plan excavated at Kummanni or Hattusa; appearance entirely reconstructed from ritual handling instructions. Hurrian incantations in CTH 482 are only partially understood due to limited Hurrian lexicon. Exact geographic extent of Kizzuwatna cult beyond Hattusa copies unclear; Sirkeli Höyük/Kinet Höyük excavations have not yielded archives. Relationship to Pirinkir and precise degree of syncretism with Šawuška remains unresolved. No mythic narrative survives, so personality and cosmology are inferred from ritual function. No verified public-domain image exists; any illustration must be marked as hypothetical. Modern reception beyond scholarship is minimal and largely homonymous.

Research Scope

Focused dossier on the Hurrian-Hittite Goddess of the Night (Wikidata Q107710151, Sumerogram DINGIR.GE6) attested in Kizzuwatnean rituals at Hattusa (CTH 481-482, 406). Disambiguates from homonymous Greek Nyx, Egyptian Nut, Aztec Oxomoco, modern cruise ship, novel series and song that share the English descriptive title. Based on cuneiform primary texts in Hittite/Hurrian, critical editions by Miller, Strauß, Haas, Beckman and Taracha. No exhaustive archaeological iconography exists; Hurrian original name unknown.
