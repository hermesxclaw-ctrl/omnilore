Who It Is

She has no face you can name and no myth where she speaks. In the hill country of Kizzuwatna, when impurity clung to a house and dreams turned foul, priests carried her image into darkness, dug a pit to the underworld, and whispered in Hurrian to the Goddess of the Night — a deity whose true name was already lost to her Hittite translators, remembered only as DINGIR.GE6, the Deity of Night.

Not a mythic actor with personality traits like Greek Nyx. In ritual logic she is powerful, ambivalent, and mediatory: invoked to witness oaths, to receive impurity, to intercede with underworld deities (Allani, Hutena-Hutellura), and to grant favor or remove affliction. She is approached with elaborate respect and fear; the Old Woman (MUNUS ŠU.GI) ritualist speaks to her in Hurrian, suggesting esoteric, non-Hittite knowledge. She is neither benevolent mother nor hostile demon but a potent chthonic-nocturnal power who must be properly evoked, fed, and sent back.

Powers
Nocturnal epiphany and evocation

Can be summoned (evoked) from her temple to the ritual locus at night and subsequently dismissed/sent back. Presence is signaled by ritual efficacy, not by narrative epiphany.

Limit: Requires correct Hurrian incantations, night-time performance, and a qualified Old Woman; failure to dismiss properly risks lingering impurity

Purification and transfer of impurity

Core power: to absorb or receive impurity (papratar), evil, curse, and pollution from patient, house, or army, which is then transferred to substitute figurines, animals, or wool and expelled to the steppe or underworld.

Limit: Not automatic; requires elaborate offerings, washing, and scapegoat-like transfer; impurity can rebound if rite is flawed

Mediation with the underworld

Acts as intermediary to chthonic deities — Allani (underworld queen), Hutena and Hutellura (fate goddesses), and ancestral spirits — via the pit (abi). Messages and offerings pass through her.

Limit: Does not rule the underworld herself; she opens access but does not command the dead

Oneiromancy and dream incubation

Night association implies power over dreams; ritual patient sleeps in purified space after rite to receive favorable dream or relief. Paškuwatti ritual links her to sexual dreams and potency.

Limit: No explicit 'sends dreams' myth; inference from ritual context and night domain

Protection and favor (pangur?)

If properly honored with food, drink, and jewelry offerings, she grants protection from witchcraft, enemy curses, and divine anger; withdrawal of favor leaves patient exposed.

Limit: Favor is conditional and temporary; requires periodic re-performance

Association with magic and witchcraft counteraction

Classified by Hittitologists as a deity of 'black magic' and counter-magic (alwanzatar). Her ritual is used against sorcery-induced illness.

Limit: Not a sorceress herself; she counters human witchcraft

Form & Signs

Appearance — No securely identified anthropomorphic image survives. The ritual texts describe handling of her cult image/statuette (Hittite ešri-, ALAM) which is bathed, anointed, clothed, veiled, and carried in procession, then returned to her temple or shrine. The image is treated as animate and susceptible to pollution, requiring purification. One passage describes drawing a curtain or veil before her. Accounts I
The Great Ritual of the Goddess of the Night (CTH 482) — Purification of a House and PersonCTH 482 composite text (KUB 29.7 etc.)

Late Bronze Age, 14th-13th c. BCE

The most complete narrative: A patron (king, queen, or elite) whose household is polluted commissions the Old Woman. At night, a curtained enclosure is erected, a pit is dug to the underworld, and the goddess's image is brought in procession. Offerings of sheep, birds, bread, honey, oil and wine are made. Hurrian incantations are recited. Impurity is transferred onto wool threads tied to the patient and onto clay figurines representing the patient and family. The figurines and threads are placed in the pit or sent to the wilderness. The patient is washed, anointed, and made to sleep in the purified space to await a favorable dream. The goddess is then reverently returned to her temple with gifts. The text gives stage directions, incantations, and lists of deities invoked as witnesses. Model for Kizzuwatnean purification magic; demonstrates Hurrian ritual technology adopted as Hittite state ritual; source for understanding Hittite concepts of impurity and substitution

The Evocatio of the Goddess of the Night (CTH 481)CTH 481 (KUB 32.130 etc.)

13th c. BCE

A shorter, more focused rite whose core is the formal summoning and sending-back of the goddess. The Old Woman calls her from Kizzuwatna/Kummanni to Hattusa, offers her a new residence (tent/shrine), feeds her, and asks her to take away evil that has been brought by an enemy or by witchcraft. The goddess is entreated not to be angry and to intercede with the fate goddesses. The rite ends with a formal dismissal and road provisions for her return journey. Shows mobility of deities in Hittite thought; deities can be invited to travel; illustrates Kizzuwatna-Hattusa cult transfer

Paškuwatti's Ritual Against Sexual Impotence (CTH 406)KUB 9.27 + KUB 9.28

14th c. BCE

Paškuwatti, a woman ritualist from Arzawa, invokes the Goddess of the Night among other deities to cure a man's impotence attributed to sorcery or divine anger. The ritual involves sympathetic magic: spindle and distaff (female tools) are held by the patient to symbolize his unmanliness, then replaced with bow and arrows (male tools) as potency is restored. The Goddess of the Night is asked to remove the evil that binds his virility, with offerings and Hurrian spells. The patient is passed through a gate of reeds and washed. Important evidence for Hittite attitudes to sexuality, gender symbolism, and magic; shows Goddess of the Night's role beyond house purification into personal physiology

Accounts II
The Goddess of the Night in Hurrian Offering Lists — The Divine Assembly at HattusaKUB 27.1 and parallel offering lists (KUB 45.21, KBo 20.73)

13th c. BCE

Not a narrative myth but a liturgical sequence: in state festivals, the Goddess of the Night receives offerings in fixed order among the Hurrian pantheon — after Teššub, Hebat, Šawuška and before Hutena-Hutellura and Allani. The list prescribes one sheep, bread and beer libation. Her placement reveals her rank and theological grouping: clustered with Ištar-type and underworld goddesses, indicating her liminal status. The scribes note her Kizzuwatnean origin. Demonstrates integration of Kizzuwatnean deity into Hittite state religion; helps reconstruct Hurrian pantheon structure

The Purification of the Army and the King (Kizzuwatna-type arkuwar ritual referencing DINGIR.GE6)Cult inventories and arkuwar (plea) rituals: KUB 38.12, KUB 30.34 + KUB 39.71

13th c. BCE

Fragmentary rituals where the Goddess of the Night is invoked to purify the Hittite army before campaign or the king after ill-omened dreams. The ritual logic mirrors CTH 482 but on a collective scale: impurity contracted from battlefield corpses or enemy sorcery is transferred to prisoners or animals led to the border. The goddess is asked to 'stand before' the king in the night and turn away the evil. The text breaks off but parallels the house ritual. Shows extension of private Kizzuwatnean magic to state and military sphere

Modern Scholarly Re-discovery — From Hattusa Tablets to Miller's ReconstructionExcavation reports of Hattusa (1906-), publication of KUB/KBo, and Miller 2004 monograph

1906-2004 CE (modern reception as story)

After millennia of oblivion, German excavations at Boğazköy uncovered thousands of cuneiform tablets. For decades DINGIR.GE6 was an enigmatic logogram. Volkert Haas identified her Kizzuwatnean Hurrian origin in the 1970s-90s, Gary Beckman highlighted her distinct ritual profile, and Jared Miller's 2004 critical edition finally assembled all fragments of CTH 482 into a coherent ritual sequence, revealing a complete Hurrian-Hittite purification system. This modern philological detective story restored the goddess to history. Established Goddess of the Night as key case study for cultural transfer between Hurrians and Hittites; corrected earlier conflation with Šawuška

The Goddess of the Night is the same as Greek Nyx, Egyptian Nut, or Aztec Oxomoco — These are four unrelated deities from different cultures and millennia who happen to share the English descriptive phrase 'goddess of the night.' Nyx is a primordial Greek cosmogonic figure (Hesiod, Theogony); Nut is Egyptian sky goddess; Oxomoco is Aztec calendrical deity; DINGIR.GE6 is a Hurrian-Hittite purification goddess. Conflation is an artifact of English translation, not ancient syncretism.

She is a moon goddess or star goddess with crescent/star iconography — No cuneiform text describes her with lunar or astral symbols, and no identified image exists. The Hittite moon deity is Arma (male). Her 'night' is ritual darkness and underworld darkness, not the night sky. Star-cloak images are modern inventions.

We know her Hurrian name and she was called 'Nocturna' in antiquity — Her Hurrian name is unknown; DINGIR.GE6 is a Sumerogram used by Hittite scribes, read in Hittite or Akkadian. 'Dea Nocturna' is modern scholarly Latin convenience, not ancient.

She was a goddess of witchcraft who taught spells — She is a deity invoked to counter witchcraft and remove impurity caused by sorcery, not a patron of witches. The expertise lies with the human Old Woman, not the goddess teaching magic.

There is a well-known myth where she appears as a character — No mythic narrative (like a theogony or epic) featuring her as actor survives. Evidence is entirely ritual and offering lists — we know how she was worshipped, not stories about her deeds. Any 'myth' retold online is invented.
