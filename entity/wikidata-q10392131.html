The Approach

In the blood-soaked heart of Tenochtitlan, atop the Templo Mayor, a god of war and the sun demanded the ultimate tribute: human hearts torn from living chests. Known to the Spaniards as Huichilobos and later as Vitzliputzli, this hummingbird deity was no mere idol but the embodiment of a people's cosmic mission, driving the Aztec Empire's relentless conquests and its most terrifying rituals.

Content advisory. This page discusses human sacrifice, ritual violence, dismemberment, and cannibalism in the context of Aztec religion. It also includes colonial-era accounts that may reflect bias and misunderstandings. Reader discretion is advised.
Identity

Huitzilopochtli

Pronunciation [wiːt͡siloːˈpoːt͡ʃt͡ɬi] (Classical Nahuatl); Spanish: [wit͡siloˈpot͡ʃt͡ɬi]; German: [vɪtsliˈpʊtsli]

Known As

VitzliputzliHuichilobosUitzilopochtliWitzilopochtliLeft-Handed HummingbirdHummingbird of the South

Etymology

The name is composed of two Nahuatl elements: 'huītzilin' (hummingbird) and 'ōpōchtli' (left side). The common translation is 'Left-Handed Hummingbird' or 'Hummingbird of the South,' as the south was associated with the left in Aztec cosmology. However, Frances Karttunen notes that Nahuatl compounds are typically head-final, suggesting a possible translation of 'the left/south side of the hummingbird.'

Cultural Ownership

Aztec (Mexica) civilization, central Mexico, pre-Columbian and early colonial period

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars have identified Huitzilopochtli with the sun god or with Tezcatlipoca, but these identifications are debated. The name 'Vitzliputzli' is a Europeanized corruption, not an authentic indigenous variant.

Description

Appearance

Huitzilopochtli is typically depicted as a warrior with a hummingbird helmet or headdress, holding a serpent-shaped fire weapon (Xiuhcoatl). He is often painted with blue or black stripes on his face and body. In some representations, he is shown as a hummingbird itself. His costume includes a shield and darts, and he may be adorned with feathers and gold ornaments.

Symbols & Iconography

Hummingbird, fire serpent (Xiuhcoatl), turquoise (xiuh), shield, darts, blue body paint, white feathers, and the eagle. The Templo Mayor's red shrine was dedicated to him.

Behaviour & Personality

Huitzilopochtli is a fierce and aggressive war deity, embodying the sun's power and the necessity of sacrifice. He is relentless in his pursuit of his siblings (the stars and moon), representing the sun's daily battle against darkness. He is also a patron and protector of the Mexica people, guiding them on their migration and establishing Tenochtitlan.

Domains & Habitats

He resides in the heavens, particularly the south, and is associated with the sun. His earthly home is the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, where his temple atop the pyramid represents Coatepec (Serpent Mountain).

Version Differences

In the Mexica version, he is the son of Coatlicue and the champion who defeats Coyolxauhqui. In another origin story, he is one of the sons of Ometeotl, the creator couple, alongside Quetzalcoatl, Xipe Totec, and Tezcatlipoca. Some accounts describe him as a historical leader who was deified after death.

Stories & Myths

The Birth of Huitzilopochtli and the Defeat of Coyolxauhqui

Source Diego Durán, Historia de las Indias de Nueva EspañaCulture AztecPeriod Pre-Columbian

Summary

Coatlicue, the earth goddess, is impregnated by a ball of feathers while sweeping on Mount Coatepec. Her daughter, Coyolxauhqui, and her four hundred sons, the Centzonuitznaua, plot to kill her to preserve their honor. As they ascend the mountain, Huitzilopochtli bursts from his mother's womb fully armed and kills his sister, dismembering her and casting her body down the mountain. He then pursues his brothers, scattering them across the sky as stars.

Participants

Huitzilopochtli

Coatlicue

Coyolxauhqui

Centzonuitznaua

Consequences

The myth explains the daily solar cycle: the sun (Huitzilopochtli) chases the moon (Coyolxauhqui) and stars (the brothers). It also establishes the importance of sacrifice to maintain the sun's strength.

Variants

A similar story exists with Quetzalcoatl as the hero, and in some versions, Coatlicue is replaced by other deities.

The Creation of the World

Source Codex FlorentineCulture AztecPeriod Pre-Columbian

Summary

Huitzilopochtli and Quetzalcoatl are instructed by their parents, Ometeotl, to bring order to the world. Together they create fire, the first humans, the Earth, and the Sun.

Participants

Huitzilopochtli

Quetzalcoatl

Ometeotl

Consequences

Establishes Huitzilopochtli as a creator deity and his close association with Quetzalcoatl.

Variants

This story is less prominent than the Coatepec myth and may reflect later syncretism.

The Migration of the Mexica

Source Codex Boturini, Codex AzcatitlanCulture AztecPeriod Mythical-historical

Summary

Huitzilopochtli guides the Mexica people on their long migration from Aztlan to the Valley of Mexico. He appears in visions and as a hummingbird, directing them to settle where they see an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a snake, which becomes Tenochtitlan.

Participants

Huitzilopochtli

The Mexica people

Consequences

The Mexica establish their capital and rise to power, fulfilling Huitzilopochtli's prophecy.

Variants

Various codices provide different details of the migration and Huitzilopochtli's guidance.

Dark History

Human sacrifice

Source Durán, Sahagún, Cortés

Description

The Aztecs performed human sacrifices to Huitzilopochtli, primarily by cutting open the victim's chest and removing the still-beating heart. The bodies were often disposed of by pushing them down the pyramid steps, where the Coyolxauhqui stone lay, symbolizing the dismemberment of the goddess. The flesh was sometimes consumed in ritual cannibalism.

Scholarly Context

Scholars debate the scale and purpose of Aztec sacrifice, with some interpreting it as a political tool and others as a religious necessity. The accounts of Spanish conquistadors may be exaggerated for propaganda purposes.

Ritual bloodletting

Source Florentine Codex

Description

Self-sacrifice by bloodletting was a daily practice among the Aztecs, who offered their own blood to nourish the gods, including Huitzilopochtli.

Scholarly Context

Autosacrifice was a common Mesoamerican practice, symbolizing penitence and reciprocity with the divine.

War and tribute

Source Various

Description

Huitzilopochtli's cult promoted warfare as a means to capture victims for sacrifice. Wars were often waged specifically to obtain prisoners.

Scholarly Context

This militaristic ideology was central to Aztec expansion and control over vassal states.

Contradictions & Disputes

Etymology of the name

Position A

The name means 'Left-Handed Hummingbird' or 'Hummingbird of the South'.

Position A — evidence

Common translation in many sources.

Position B

The name may mean 'The left/south side of the hummingbird' due to Nahuatl compounding rules.

Position B — evidence

Linguistic analysis by Frances Karttunen.

Scholarly Consensus

No consensus; both interpretations are plausible.

Origin story

Position A

Huitzilopochtli is the son of Coatlicue and born on Coatepec.

Position A — evidence

Durán, Florentine Codex

Position B

He is one of the sons of Ometeotl, the creator couple.

Position B — evidence

Florentine Codex (Book 3)

Scholarly Consensus

The Coatlicue story is more prominent, but both exist in different sources.

Role as sun god

Position A

Huitzilopochtli is the sun god.

Position A — evidence

Aztec mythology describes him as the sun chasing the moon and stars.

Position B

He is not originally a sun god but was elevated to that status by Tlacaelel.

Position B — evidence

Historical accounts suggest his cult was reformed to emphasize solar aspects.

Scholarly Consensus

He is widely recognized as a solar deity, but his original role may have been different.

Human sacrifice scale

Position A

Human sacrifice was widespread and frequent.

Position A — evidence

Indigenous accounts and archaeological evidence.

Position B

The scale may have been exaggerated by Spanish sources.

Position B — evidence

Some scholars argue the numbers were inflated for propaganda.

Scholarly Consensus

Human sacrifice was real, but the exact scale is debated.

Known Gaps

1. The original Nahuatl pronunciation and etymology are not fully resolved. 2. The exact scale and frequency of human sacrifice to Huitzilopochtli are debated. 3. The role of Huitzilopochtli in earlier Mesoamerican cultures is unclear. 4. The name 'Vitzliputzli' appears in German literature; more research is needed on its usage. 5. Some archaeological findings from the Templo Mayor have not been fully published. 6. The relationship between Huitzilopochtli and other deities, such as Tezcatlipoca, is complex and not fully understood.
