Who It Is

In the blood-soaked heart of Tenochtitlan, atop the Templo Mayor, a god of war and the sun demanded the ultimate tribute: human hearts torn from living chests. Known to the Spaniards as Huichilobos and later as Vitzliputzli, this hummingbird deity was no mere idol but the embodiment of a people's cosmic mission, driving the Aztec Empire's relentless conquests and its most terrifying rituals.

Huitzilopochtli is a fierce and aggressive war deity, embodying the sun's power and the necessity of sacrifice. He is relentless in his pursuit of his siblings (the stars and moon), representing the sun's daily battle against darkness. He is also a patron and protector of the Mexica people, guiding them on their migration and establishing Tenochtitlan.

Etymology — The name is composed of two Nahuatl elements: 'huītzilin' (hummingbird) and 'ōpōchtli' (left side). The common translation is 'Left-Handed Hummingbird' or 'Hummingbird of the South,' as the south was associated with the left in Aztec cosmology. Powers
Solar Power

Huitzilopochtli is the sun, providing light and warmth. He requires blood sacrifices to maintain his strength in the cosmic battle against the forces of darkness.

Limit: He must be nourished by human blood and hearts, otherwise he weakens and the world is endangered.

War Magic

As a war deity, he grants victory to his followers in battle, guiding them and providing supernatural aid.

Limit: His favor depends on proper offerings and rituals.

Weapon: Xiuhcoatl (Fire Serpent)

A mythical serpent made of turquoise or fire, used as a weapon to strike down enemies, particularly his sister Coyolxauhqui.

Limit: Only he can wield it effectively.

Shapeshifting

He can transform into a hummingbird, a form he used to communicate with the Mexica people.

Limit: Not well documented; transformation is implied in his name and iconography.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Huitzilopochtli is typically depicted as a warrior with a hummingbird helmet or headdress, holding a serpent-shaped fire weapon (Xiuhcoatl). He is often painted with blue or black stripes on his face and body. In some representations, he is shown as a hummingbird itself. His costume includes a shield and darts, and he may be adorned with feathers and gold ornaments.

Symbols — Hummingbird, fire serpent (Xiuhcoatl), turquoise (xiuh), shield, darts, blue body paint, white feathers, and the eagle. The Templo Mayor's red shrine was dedicated to him.

Domains — He resides in the heavens, particularly the south, and is associated with the sun. His earthly home is the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, where his temple atop the pyramid represents Coatepec (Serpent Mountain).

Accounts I
The Birth of Huitzilopochtli and the Defeat of CoyolxauhquiDiego Durán, Historia de las Indias de Nueva España

Pre-Columbian

Coatlicue, the earth goddess, is impregnated by a ball of feathers while sweeping on Mount Coatepec. Her daughter, Coyolxauhqui, and her four hundred sons, the Centzonuitznaua, plot to kill her to preserve their honor. As they ascend the mountain, Huitzilopochtli bursts from his mother's womb fully armed and kills his sister, dismembering her and casting her body down the mountain. He then pursues his brothers, scattering them across the sky as stars. The myth explains the daily solar cycle: the sun (Huitzilopochtli) chases the moon (Coyolxauhqui) and stars (the brothers). It also establishes the importance of sacrifice to maintain the sun's strength.

Accounts II
The Creation of the WorldCodex Florentine

Pre-Columbian

Huitzilopochtli and Quetzalcoatl are instructed by their parents, Ometeotl, to bring order to the world. Together they create fire, the first humans, the Earth, and the Sun. Establishes Huitzilopochtli as a creator deity and his close association with Quetzalcoatl.

The Migration of the MexicaCodex Boturini, Codex Azcatitlan

Mythical-historical

Huitzilopochtli guides the Mexica people on their long migration from Aztlan to the Valley of Mexico. He appears in visions and as a hummingbird, directing them to settle where they see an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a snake, which becomes Tenochtitlan. The Mexica establish their capital and rise to power, fulfilling Huitzilopochtli's prophecy.

Vitzliputzli is a distinct deity from Huitzilopochtli. — Vitzliputzli is a Germanized corruption of Huitzilopochtli, used in European literature.

Huitzilopochtli was the supreme god of the Aztecs. — He was one of the most important deities, but not necessarily supreme. Ometeotl was the creator, and Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, and Tlaloc were also major gods.

The Aztecs sacrificed thousands of people daily to Huitzilopochtli. — While human sacrifice was common, the scale is debated. Most sacrifices were of war captives, and the frequency varied.

Huitzilopochtli was a historical person who was deified. — There is no historical evidence for a real person named Huitzilopochtli. He is purely mythological.
