Who It Is

The name Medon means 'ruler' — but in Greek myth no single Medon ever truly rules. Instead the name echoes across palaces, battlefields and wedding halls as a half-brother left to command, a herald forced to serve murderous suitors, an archon who traded a crown for a magistracy, and a centaur who never left the fight alive. To follow Medon is to follow how the Greeks reused a good name for very different kinds of survival.

Medon the herald: loyal but coerced; described as faithful to Penelope (he informs her of Telemachus' danger at 4.677) and pitiable when begging for life (22.371-377). He claims he cared for Telemachus as a child and acted under compulsion from suitors. Telemachus vouches for him, suggesting a gentle, non-combatant character. Powers
Heraldic inviolability and palace access

As keryx (herald) in Ithaca, Medon has privileged access to both suitors' councils and Penelope's quarters, and can move between them to carry messages. His status grants him a claim to mercy as a non-combatant sacred to Hermes/Zeus. Telemachus explicitly invokes this to save him during the mnesterophonia (slaughter of suitors).

Limit: Immunity is not absolute — Odysseus initially intends to kill him; only Telemachus' intervention saves him. He is powerless against suitors' coercion and must serve them despite loyalty to Odysseus' house. No martial prowess is attributed.

Martial command (Locrian contingent)

Medon son of Oileus assumes command of the Locrian-Methone contingent after Ajax the Lesser's death (or absence), leading troops and fighting in the front ranks. He is a competent warrior-leader sufficient to be named in the Catalogue of Ships tradition.

Limit: His command is explicitly secondary and illegitimate by birth (nothos). He is killed by Aeneas with a spear to the head/arm — no aristeia, no divine protection. He does not have Ajax's speed or armor.

Archonship and civic foundation

Medon son of Codrus is credited as first archon (archon for life) of Athens, replacing hereditary kingship after Codrus' death. He institutes the archonship as accountable magistracy, a constitutional power rather than personal might.

Limit: Power is deliberately curtailed: archon is elected/accountable, not absolute king. Tradition says Athenians denied his brother Acastus kingship due to Codrus' oath, so Medon's rule is contested from start. Later reform reduces archonship from life to ten years to one year.

Centaur strength and combat

As a centaur, Medon possesses equine strength, speed, and ability to wield trees/rocks and spears in the Centauromachy. Ovid lists him among combatants at Pirithous' wedding.

Limit: Mortal and killable; Ovid notes he is wounded (variously translated as killed or injured) in the melee. No individual feat is singled out; he is one of many casualties. No shapeshifting or magic.

Accounts I
Medon the Herald Spared During the Slaughter of the SuitorsHomer, Odyssey Books 4, 16, 22, 24

Archaic, c. 750-700 BCE (mythic setting: c. 12th century BCE, return from Troy)

Medon is a herald (keryx) in Odysseus' palace on Ithaca who remains loyal to Odysseus' house while forced to serve the 108 suitors occupying it. He overhears the suitors plotting to ambush and murder Telemachus on his return from Pylos and secretly warns Penelope (4.677-715), causing her distress but exposing the plot. When Odysseus, Telemachus and allies slaughter the suitors, Medon cowers under a chair wrapped in a fresh ox-hide, begging Telemachus to save him, claiming he only served under compulsion and had cared for Telemachus as a child. Telemachus vouches for him to Odysseus, who spares him along with the bard Phemius. Medon then emerges to announce the suitors' death to the palace women and later appears among the Ithacans in Book 24. Medon is one of only two men in the hall spared (with Phemius), establishing the Greek norm that heralds and bards are inviolable. His survival allows a witness to legitimize Odysseus' revenge as justice, not indiscriminate murder. His warning to Penelope is the first internal leak of the suitors' plot, accelerating the narrative toward Telemach

Medon Son of Oileus, Bastard Half-Brother of Ajax, Killed at TroyHomer, Iliad Books 2, 13, 15; Apollodorus Epitome 3.11, 6.15b

Archaic (Iliad) with Classical mythography

Medon is the bastard (nothos) son of Oileus, king of Locris, by his mistress Rhene, making him half-brother to the greater Locrian hero Ajax (Aias) son of Oileus. Because of his illegitimacy, he does not inherit Locris. After Ajax's death (in post-Iliadic tradition Ajax dies by suicide/shipwreck), Medon takes command of Ajax's Locrian troops stationed at Methone. In Iliad 15, during the Trojan counter-attack, Aeneas kills Medon: Aeneas strikes him as he tries to rally, described as hitting him near the head/arm, and Medon falls. His death is noted as a loss for the Locrians but without extended lament. Medon's death exemplifies the Iliad's theme that war consumes even secondary leaders. His temporary command shows how bastard sons could achieve status through war but remained expendable. Later Locrian tradition does not develop a cult for him, unlike Ajax who received hero cult at Opus.

Medon Son of Codrus, First Archon of AthensPausanias 1.19.3, 7.2.1; Aristotle Athenaion Politeia 3.3; Strabo 9.1.7; Castor of Rhodes via Eusebius Chronicle

Mythic early Iron Age (traditional date c. 1068-1048 BCE); sources are Classical-Hellenistic to Imperial (5th century BCE - 2nd century CE)

After the Dorian invasion, King Codrus of Athens sacrifices himself to save the city (an oracle said Athens would fall if its king was spared). His sons Medon and Acastus quarrel over succession. The Athenians, honoring Codrus' sacrifice, declare no one worthy to be called 'king' after him and abolish kingship, electing Medon as first archon (ruler) for life. Medon governs as archon, not basileus, marking Athens' shift from monarchy to oligarchic magistracy. He reigns 20 years (in Eusebian chronology). His descendants, the Medontids (or Codrids), hold the archonship for generations until it is limited to ten years and then to annual archons. Athenian constitutional myth: Medon's archonship is presented as the origin of Athenian republicanism and the eponymous archon list used for dating. The Medontid clan legitimized aristocratic rule. Historically, the story retrojects later archon institutions into the Bronze Age to explain the end of kingship. No contemporary inscription attests Medon; he is historiographic.

Accounts II
Medon the Centaur at Pirithous' Wedding (Centauromachy)Ovid, Metamorphoses 12.303-304; cf. Hesiod Shield of Heracles 178-190 (general Centauromachy without Medon)

Augustan Latin, 8 CE (mythic setting: generation of Theseus/Pirithous)

At the wedding of Pirithous and Hippodameia, the centaurs, drunk, attempt to abduct the Lapith women. A massive brawl erupts. Ovid catalogues centaurs and Lapiths fighting. Among them, Medon the centaur is listed as wounded or killed — in Ovid's rapid catalogue, 'Medon' appears in a line with Thaumas and Mimas, struck down in the melee. No individual deed is given; his death is part of the collective punishment of centaur hubris. The Centauromachy becomes a moral exemplum of barbarism vs. civilization, frequently depicted on temples (Parthenon metopes, Bassae). Medon's inclusion shows Ovid's encyclopedic expansion of the roster, but he has no independent tradition. His death reinforces the total defeat of centaurs.

Medon Son of Pylades and Electra — The Orestean GrandsonPausanias 2.16.7; Apollodorus Epitome (genealogical lists); Hyginus Fabulae 122 (variant)

Imperial mythography reflecting Classical tragedy tradition (5th century BCE plays as background)

After Orestes avenges Agamemnon, his sister Electra marries his friend Pylades (son of Strophius). They have two sons: Medon and Strophius (the younger). Medon is thus grandson of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra on his mother's side, and of Strophius and Anaxibia on his father's. He belongs to the post-Trojan generation that resettles Mycenae/Phocis. No narrative adventure is attached to him; his name survives only in genealogical lists to extend the house of Atreus. The genealogy closes the Oresteia by giving Electra legitimate issue, contrasting with her childless portrayal in Aeschylus and Euripides. It provides a stemma for later Phocian aristocrats claiming descent from Pylades. Medon himself has no cult or story, illustrating how mythographers filled genealogical gaps with eponymous sons.

Medon the Suitor of Penelope from DulichiumApollodorus, Epitome 7.26-27 (list of suitors); cf. Homer Odyssey 16.247-251 (Dulichian suitors generally)

1st-2nd century CE (compiling Archaic epic tradition)

In the catalogue of Penelope's 108 suitors, Apollodorus lists among the 57 suitors from Dulichium a man named Medon. Like the other suitors, he occupies Odysseus' palace, consumes his wealth, courts Penelope, and plots against Telemachus. He is killed with the other suitors by Odysseus. This Medon is entirely distinct from Medon the herald who serves the suitors — the homonymy creates confusion, but the herald is spared precisely because he is not a suitor. The suitor Medon's death is part of the collective punishment of the suitors, with no individual distinction. His inclusion shows how common the name was in epic onomastics. The existence of two Medons in the same palace narrative (herald and suitor) is a test case for oral epic's reuse of stock names.

Medon was a single famous Greek hero like Achilles or Odysseus. — Medon is a common heroic name meaning 'ruler' borne by at least seven minor figures with no unified biography. The most important are a herald, a bastard warrior, and an Athenian magistrate — none has an independent epic. Collapsing them creates a fictional composite.

Medon was King of Athens after Codrus. — Athenian tradition is explicit that kingship ended with Codrus. Medon was first archon (elected magistrate for life), not king. Later chronicles loosely call him king, but Aristotle and Pausanias distinguish the offices. The archonship was deliberately non-royal.

Tion Medon from Star Wars is named after the Greek mythological Medon. — No evidence links George Lucas' Tion Medon (Utapau administrator, 2005) to Greek myth. The name appears to be invented science-fiction onomastics. The Greek Medon and Star Wars Medon are unrelated homonyms that coincide in disambiguation lists.

Medon the herald was a traitor who supported the suitors. — Homer presents Medon as coerced and loyal to Penelope/Odysseus: he warns Penelope of the ambush plot (Od. 4.677) and Telemachus testifies he cared for him as a child. He begs for mercy claiming compulsion, and is believed. Later moralizing retellings recast him as collaborator, but the primary text exonerates him.
