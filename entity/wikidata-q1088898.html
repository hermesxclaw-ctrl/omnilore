Who It Is

Four different women share one golden name — Chrysothemis, 'golden law' — and each tests what obedience costs. One wins the first song ever sung for Apollo at Delphi. One guards the golden apples at the edge of the world. One murders her husband on her wedding night. And one, the most famous, chooses to survive inside a house of murder by refusing to rebel, standing in Sophocles' shadows while her sister Electra burns to avenge their father.

Characterization survives primarily for Sophoclean Atreid Chrysothemis, and is sharply defined against Electra. Sophocles presents her as pragmatic, cautious, survival-oriented, obedient to power (Clytemnestra/Aegisthus), affectionate to family but unwilling to risk death for vengeance. She argues for sophrosyne (prudence, self-control) and cites lack of power: 'I am not strong enough' (Electra 997-1000). She loves Electra and Orestes but prioritizes living. Powers
Hymnic / Musical Excellence (Cretan Chrysothemis)

Attested as first victor in the singing contest at the Pythian Games, performing a hymn to Apollo. Implies exceptional vocal and citharodic skill, piety, and favor of Apollo. Not supernatural power but preeminent technē.

Limit: One-time victory; no further feats. The contest at that date was only hymn-singing, before athletic events added. Does not imply prophetic or priestly power.

Nymphic Longevity and Garden Guardianship (Hesperid Chrysothemis)

As Hesperid, shares nymph attributes: agelessness, connection to evening/west, guardianship of golden apples with sisters and Ladon. Not individually distinguished in power.

Limit: Hesperides are not Olympian goddesses; they are minor nymphs, vulnerable to Heracles who steals apples. No individual feat for Chrysothemis.

Pragmatic Survival and Diplomatic Persuasion (Atreid Chrysothemis)

Sophoclean characterization: ability to navigate tyrannical household, maintain favor with Clytemnestra/Aegisthus while secretly honoring Agamemnon (carrying offerings). Rhetorical skill in agon with Electra, though ultimately fails to persuade.

Limit: Explicitly lacks power to act: she states she has no army, no allies, and would be killed if she rebelled. Her prudence is presented as both virtue and limitation.

Danaid Lethal Obedience (Danaid Chrysothemis)

Participates in collective murder of husband Asterides on wedding night at father's command, using dagger/sword or pin. Implies capability for deception and killing, under paternal coercion.

Limit: Act is not personal power but obedience; Danaids are then punished (in many versions) with eternal water-carrying in Hades, though Hypermnestra alone is spared. Whether Chrysothemis is among punished or spared varies by source - Apollodorus does not specify individual fates beyond Hypermnestra.

Accounts I
The First Pythian VictoryPausanias, Description of Greece 10.7.2; supplemented by Grimal s.v. Carmanor

Mythic early history, recorded 2nd c. CE; reflects tradition of Pythian Games foundation after Apollo slays Python

After Apollo kills Python and is purified by Carmanor in Crete (at Tarrha), the god establishes the Pythian Games at Delphi. The earliest contest is not athletic but musical: a hymn to Apollo. Chrysothemis, daughter of Carmanor, enters and wins, becoming the first Pythian victor. Her victory links Cretan purification tradition to Delphic prestige. Later sources make her either wife of Staphylus (son of Dionysus and Ariadne, associated with grapes) or lover of Apollo himself, by whom she bears Parthenos, eponym of Parthenos cult. Establishes Chrysothemis as culture heroine of Delphic music; explains Cretan connection to Delphi; provides aetiology for hymnic contest. Her daughter Parthenos becomes a minor goddess worshipped at Bubastis/Karpathos, dying young and apotheosized.

The Hesperid on the VaseVase painting described in Walters & Birch, History of Ancient Pottery Vol.2 p.92; Perseus Encyclopedia entry

c. 5th century BCE (Classical)

On a red-figure vase (provenance not fully digitized), four Hesperides are named by inscription: Asterope, Chrysothemis, Hygieia, and Lipara. They stand in the Garden of the Hesperides, presumably near the apple tree and Ladon. Unlike literary canon (Hesiod's three), this vase gives a local or artistic tetrad, with Chrysothemis as one guardian nymph. No narrative action survives beyond naming; her presence indicates regional variation in Hesperid number and names. Demonstrates fluidity of mythic catalogues; shows Chrysothemis name used for nymphic beings beyond heroic genealogy. Cited by modern mythographers as evidence of name's popularity.

The Danaid Wedding NightPseudo-Apollodorus Bibliotheca 2.1.5; Hyginus Fabulae 170

Mythic heroic age, compiled 1st-2nd c. CE

Danaus flees Egypt to Argos with his 50 daughters to avoid marriage to the 50 sons of his brother Aegyptus. Forced to consent, he gives each daughter to a son of Aegyptus but arms them with daggers and orders them to kill their husbands on the wedding night. Chrysothemis, daughter of Danaus (by an unnamed mother in Apollodorus; by Atlanteia or Phoebe in Hyginus), is wed to Asterides (also called Asterius/Chaetus in variants) son of Aegyptus, and murders him. All sisters except Hypermnestra obey; the Danaids are later purified but in many versions punished in Hades by carrying water in perforated jars. Mass murder leads to Danaids' proverbial punishment and to foundation of Argive line through Hypermnestra and Lynceus. Chrysothemis's individual fate not distinguished; she shares collective guilt/punishment.

Accounts II
Agamemnon's Daughter in the IliadHomer, Iliad 9.142-145, 9.281-285

Archaic, c. 750-700 BCE

During the embassy to Achilles, Agamemnon offers reconciliation: if Achilles returns to battle, he may choose one of Agamemnon's three daughters — Chrysothemis, Laodice, and Iphianassa — as wife, with a great dowry and seven cities. The offer is rejected. Chrysothemis appears only as a name in a diplomatic bride-list, indicating her status as a high-value princess for heroic alliance, before the later tragic tradition reshapes the family. Establishes oldest attested Atreid Chrysothemis; later tragedians keep Chrysothemis but replace Laodice/Iphianassa with Electra/Iphigenia, creating continuity issue debated by scholars. No death or action in this version.

The Sisters' Quarrel in Sophocles' ElectraSophocles, Electra, lines 328-471, 871-1057, 1360-1380

Classical, c. 419-410 BCE

Years after Clytemnestra and Aegisthus murdered Agamemnon, Mycenae is ruled by the usurpers. Electra lives as a mourning, rebellious prisoner in the palace, while her younger sister Chrysothemis has adapted, enjoying status and warning Electra to submit. In their first agon, Chrysothemis brings funerary offerings to Agamemnon's grave at Clytemnestra's command but is convinced by Electra to dedicate them to Agamemnon instead. She later brings false news of Orestes' death (a trick by Orestes) and is devastated, then overjoyed when Orestes is revealed alive. She begs Electra not to attempt to kill Aegisthus alone, refusing to join the matricide plot, and disappears before the murders. She survives, unlike Electra who participates. Defines Chrysothemis as foil to Electra: prudence vs. heroic intransigence. Her survival vs. Electra's risk raises tragic question of right action under tyranny. Sophocles leaves her fate after Orestes' revenge unspoken; she presumably remains in palace under Orestes' new rule.

Mother of Parthenos (Cretan Variant)Pseudo-Apollodorus fragment via Diodorus Siculus 5.62?; Hyginus Astronomica 2.25; scholia to Pausanias

Hellenistic-Roman mythography

In a Cretan local legend, Chrysothemis daughter of Carmanor becomes lover of Apollo and bears a daughter Parthenos (Virgin). Parthenos dies young and Apollo apotheosizes her, or she becomes a goddess at Karpathos/Bubastis. In another variant, Chrysothemis is wife of Staphylus (son of Dionysus) and mother of Parthenos, Rhoio, and Molpadia/Hemithea. The confusion reflects overlapping grape/virgin goddess traditions linking Apollo, Dionysus, and Cretan purification myths. Provides aetiology for Parthenos cult and for Staphylus's wine-associated family. Shows how Chrysothemis functions as genealogical bridge between Cretan and Delphic traditions.

Chrysothemis was Agamemnon's daughter who won the Pythian Games. — Two different women: Cretan Chrysothemis daughter of Carmanor won the Pythian hymn; Atreid Chrysothemis daughter of Agamemnon was a Mycenaean princess in tragedy. No source makes Agamemnon's daughter a Pythian victor.

Chrysothemis helped Electra and Orestes kill Clytemnestra. — In Sophocles, Chrysothemis explicitly refuses to join the murder plot, arguing they lack strength and will be killed. She disappears before the matricide; only Electra (and Orestes) participate. Euripides and Aeschylus omit her from revenge entirely.

Chrysothemis is just a minor, invented character with no mythic importance. — While less famous than Electra, she is attested from Homer (8th c. BCE) through Sophocles and Pausanias, and embodies important Greek concept of sophrosyne (prudence). Her Pythian victory is aetiologically important for Delphic history, and her Danaid and Hesperid names show name's breadth.

Chrysothemis means 'golden flower' (like Chrysanthemum). — It means 'golden law/custom' (chrysos + themis), not flower. Chrysanthemum is chrysos + anthemon (flower). Themis is divine law/order, giving name juridical/religious resonance, not botanical.
