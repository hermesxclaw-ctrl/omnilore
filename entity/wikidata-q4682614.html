Form

In her abstract, transcendent aspect Adi Parashakti is generally formless (nirguna), the unmanifest Para Brahman. In her manifest (saguna) form she is described — especially in the Devi Bhagavata Purana and the Lalita Sahasranama — as Bhuvaneshvari/Lalita seated on a jewel-throne on Manidvipa, her face radiating the light of millions of stars, so brilliant that even the assembled Trimurti could not gaze upon her. Cosmically she is given a macranthropic body: in the Devi Gita's vision to King Himalaya, Satyaloka is her forehead, the universe her hairs, the sun and moon her eyes, the four directions her ears, the Vedas her words, death and emotion her teeth, and Maya her smile. As Tripura Sundari she is iconised as a radiant sixteen-year-old (Shodashi), lotus-seated on the supine body of Sadashiva on a throne whose legs are Brahma, Vishnu, Isvara, and Rudra.

Powers

Cosmic creation, maintenance, and destruction (as Para Brahman) In Shakta theology Adi Parashakti is the ultimate reality itself: she is Para Brahman, the source, embodiment, and terminal dissolution of the cosmos. The Devi Bhagavata Purana declares her 'Supreme, eternal and primordial', the power behind all deities; V. R. Ramachandra Dikshitar's gloss — 'Brahman is static Shakti and Shakti is dynamic Brahman' — captures how the Goddess is both the unchanging absolute and its active force. She alternately manifests the three gunas: as Maha-Lakshmi (sattva), Maha-Saraswati (rajas), and Maha-Kali (tamas). Devi Mahatmya; Devi Bhagavata Purana; Shakta Upanishads In her supreme (para) aspect she is 'beyond all form', nirguna, and incomprehensible; to be known she must voluntarily become saguna, manifesting a perceivable form. Creation is an act of love between her and Shiva (the Bindu as seed of the universe), not a solitary mechanical act. Non-Shakta Hindu schools (Advaita Vedanta, Vaishnavism, Shaivism) do not grant the feminine supreme status; for them the Goddess is a manifestation, not the Absolute itself. Shaktism itself is internally split over which devi embodies the sovereign power.

Lord of maya and liberation The Goddess is not merely the wielder of maya but is maya itself — while the Bhagavata Purana presents Vishnu as the controller and possessor of maya, the Devi Bhagavata Purana states the goddess 'actually is maya'. Paradoxically, the same power that binds the world in delusion is also the liberative milieu (as Liberatrix) through which the Goddess delivers souls from ignorance. The Devi Gita teaches Advaita liberation: spiritual release comes from comprehending the identity of one's soul with the Goddess/Brahman. Devi Bhagavata Purana; Devi Gita (Skandha 7) Liberation is not automatic but achieved through detachment, meditation on one's own soul, and the Goddess's grace (anugraha); salvation in a Shakta view is 'not deterministic but evolutionary' (per the Jayadrathayamala). The tension between a personal loving mother goddess and a gender-neutral nirguna absolute is resolved differently in Srikula (benign/beautiful Lalita) versus Kalikula (terrifying Kali) practice.

Power of the Trimurti (creators' animation) The Goddess is held to be the energy that makes the gods act: the Trimurti are 'her subordinates who cannot function without her power'. In the Devi Mahatmya the gods are reduced to helplessness and must 'surrender their potency to her', channelling their essential powers into a single feminine being. The Mahadevi alone wields the threefold power corresponding to Brahma's creation, Vishnu's preservation, and Shiva's dissolution. Devi Mahatmya 1.56–59; Shakta tradition This supremacy is a Shakta and Smarta claim, not shared doctrinally by stringent Vaishnava or Shaiva theology, which subordinate her to Vishnu/Shiva respectively. Some Puranas (Kurma Purana) present consort goddesses like Lakshmi as 'lower to her husband'; the Devi Mahatmya/Devi Bhagavata Purana reverse this entirely. The two registers coexist across the Hindu corpus.

Manifestation in multiform: Panchaprakritis and the Mahavidyas The Goddess reveals herself through numerous faces, all 'sprung from a part of' her. Her five primary forms (Panchaprakritis or Panchadevis) are Durga (Parvati), Lakshmi, Saraswati, Gayatri (Savitri), and Radha. Her ten tantric manifestations (the Mahavidyas) — Kali, Tara, Tripura Sundari, Bhuvaneshvari, Chinnamasta, Bhairavi, Dhumavati, Bagalamukhi, Matangi, Kamala — 'show that through her various forms, Mahadevi pervades all aspects of reality' (David Kinsley). As Tripura Sundari she is the foremost of the Mahavidyas and primary goddess of Sri Vidya. Devi Bhagavata Purana (Panchadevis); Mahavidya texts (Kubjika, Todala, Mundamala tantras); Kinsley, Hindu Goddesses Each form has a limited or specialised function — benign goddesses award grace and welfare, fierce ones demand that devotees confront fear; none alone exhausts her totality. Sectarian ranking of the forms varies (which Mahavidya or which Panchaprakriti is 'highest'), and some lists order or expand the standard ten differently.

Warrior destruction of demons (as Durga-Kali-Chandika) As the divine warrior, the Goddess annihilates asuras (demons) — above all Mahishasura (the buffalo demon), Sumbha-Nisumbha, Madhu and Kaitabha, and (as Lalita) Bhandasura. She fights with full independence, creating female peers from herself (like Kali) rather than relying on a male ally. The Devi Mahatmya frames this as the goddess's vow to manifest 'whenever her help is needed' at cosmic crisis, parallel to Vishnu's avatara doctrine. The sword of Kali 'destroys evil and cuts the worldly attachments' that feed the ego. Devi Mahatmya; Lalitopakhyana (Lalita Sahasranama); Shakti article 'Goddess as divine warrior' Her destructive power is protective, not gratuitous: warriors 'will protect anyone who comes to them with a spirit of humility or the attitude of a child'. Even the fiercest forms are internally benign-and-fierce. The violent iconography is frequently misread in the West as a 'goddess of death', disregarding her positive and liberating functions (see Kali paradox).

Kundalini and tantric realization Adi Parashakti is identified with the Kundalini Shakti, the dormant serpent-energy coiled at the base of the spine in tantric physiology. Rising through the chakras to fuse with Shiva at the crown is the process of self-transformation toward liberation (samadhi). She is also Shabda Brahman, the ultimate reality as absolute sound, from which the world emanates as nada (vibration) and mantra; siddhis (magical powers) are attained through the mantra as the Goddess's subtle form. 'Shiva without Shakti is but a corpse' (Brihannila Tantra). Tantras; Kundalini/Hatha traditions; Woodroffe, The Serpent Power; Jayadrathayamala Tantric realization requires qualified guru initiation (diksha) and practice; the left-handed (vamamarga) rites are secret and socially marginalised. In Buddhist Tantrism the active female Shakti is re-read oppositely (as static Prajna), showing the concept is not universal. European scholarship long disparaged Shakta Tantra as degeneracy (Zimmer, Woodroffe challenged this); the Samaya school puritanically denies being Tantric at all while remaining technically so.

Accounts

When the asuras endanger the existence of the devas, the gods create an all-powerful goddess from their combined anger and essential powers, channelling them into a single feminine being. She is assented as the Mahadevi, the supreme goddess wholly independent of the gods and the embodiment of shakti with additional powers of her own. Crucially, when her work is done she does not return to the gods; she vanishes, demonstrating her independence.

The buffalo-demon Mahishasura, invincible by any male god due to a boon, takes the gods' combined energies. The Goddess manifests as Durga, riding her lion, and in an epic battle kills the demon as he shifts forms (the buffalo-demon). The narrative is the heart of the autumn Navaratri festival, whose concluding Durga Puja in Bengal is the region's grandest religio-cultural event. The story is ritually retold as Kali-ised (Kali's force destroying evil) and symbolically read as the divine warrior's protective function.

Before incarnating as Parvati, the Goddess appeared to King Himalaya and revealed divine, eternal knowledge to him. She explained herself in the words of the Vedas as having neither beginning nor end: she is the only eternal truth, the whole universe is her creation, she is the only victor and the manifestation of victory. She then displayed her scarcely-seen cosmic form — Satyaloka in her forehead, the universe as her hairs, sun and moon as her eyes, the four directions in her ears, the Vedas as her words, death and emotion and affection as her teeth, and Maya by her smile.

The fourth book of the Brahmanda Purana recounts the epic battle between the forces of Lalita Tripura Sundari and those of the arch-demon Bhandasura ('Demon of the Aham/ego' in esoteric readings), symbolising the triumph of the Divine Feminine over ignorance and egotism. The goddess, as the Adi Parashakti in her Srikula form, establishes cosmic order. The victory is celebrated in the Sri Vidya tradition, which reveres her with the Sri Chakra and the thousand-name Lalita Sahasranama.

In Shaiva-inflected Shakta narrative, Adi Parashakti incarnated in material form as Parama Prakriti from the left half of Shiva (as Parabrahman) at the beginning of the universe; the Linga Purana holds that Adi Shakti brings about the evolution of life in every universe through the union of every Shiva and every Parvati. The androgyne god Ardhanarishvara (half Shiva, half Shakti) iconises the nondual unity of the masculine and feminine principles. Yet in strict Shakta theology the Goddess is not merely Shiva's better half but his source: 'Shiva without Shakti is but a corpse'.

After the decline of Buddhism in India, elements of Hindu and Buddhist goddess worship merged, culminating in the Mahavidyas — a group of ten fierce and esoteric goddesses, generally ordered Kali, Tara, Tripura Sundari, Bhuvaneshvari, Chinnamasta, Bhairavi, Dhumavati, Bagalamukhi, Matangi, and Kamala. Texts such as the Guhyatiguhya, Todala, and Mundamala tantras compare the ten Mahavidyas to the ten avatars of Vishnu, though (Kinsley notes) they serve less cosmic roles; their purpose is to show that through her forms the Goddess pervades all of reality, including its frightening, transgressive, and death-adjacent dimensions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Long before the gods of Olympus, long before Thor's hammer, the oldest and most insistent claim in the Hindu world was not about a king of the gods at all — it was that the entire machinery of the universe — creation, preservation, destruction, the very life-vitality of Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva — runs on the power of a single Goddess who is herself the supreme reality, the one who can say 'I created all worlds at my will, without any higher being.' Adi Parashakti, the Primordial Supreme Power, is the great 'if…' of theology: if the Shakta doctors are right, every god you have ever been taught to fear or worship is merely a mask she wears, and the Trimurti are not kings but her officers, holding their thrones only because she lends them the strength to sit.
