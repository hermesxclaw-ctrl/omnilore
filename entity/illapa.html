Who It Is

Illapa (also known as Apu Illapu, Illapu, or Ilyap'a) is the Inca god of thunder, lightning, rain, and war — the third most important deity in the official Inca pantheon after Inti (Sun) and Viracocha (Creator). He was imagined as a powerful man in shining garments who carried a great sling. Thunder was the crack of his sling, lightning was the flash of his stones, and the Milky Way was the celestial river from which he drew water to pour as rain upon the earth. He was also associated with the constellation we call Orion, and the Incas believed the Pleiades were his servants. Illapa was particularly important in an agricultural empire entirely dependent on the seasonal rains for crops: when rain failed, the entire empire mobilized in prayer and sacrifice to him. Children and llamas were sacrificed on high mountain peaks (an archaeological discovery confirmed by the famous capacocha mummies found in the Andes) to bring his attention. Illapa was also the patron of warriors, and Inca armies invoked him before battle, believing his thunder and lightning foreshadowed the violence of combat. His image was kept in the Coricancha alongside those of Inti and Mama Quilla, and his festival (Celebrated in July during the dry season) involved fasting, dancing, and animal sacrifice to petition for the coming of the rains. After the Spanish conquest, the worship of Illapa syncretized with Santiago (Saint James the Greater, also associated with thunder in Spanish folk Catholicism), and rural Andean communities continued to offer him sacrifices well into the colonial period. Illapa represents the fusion of meteorological and martial power in Andean thought — the same energy that brings life-giving rain also brings the lethal flash.
