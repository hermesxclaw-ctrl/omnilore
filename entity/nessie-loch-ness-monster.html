Who It Is

The waters of Loch Ness are the color of strong tea, stained dark by peat from the surrounding hills. Twenty-three miles long, nearly 800 feet deep, holding more fresh water than all the lakes of England and Wales combined — it is a place where 'I can't see what's down there' feels less like an admission and more like an invitation. For nearly a century, people have looked at this loch and seen something. A hump. A long neck. A wake where no boat should be. Science says there is nothing there. But on a misty Highland morning, with the water like black glass and the hills silent all around, you might understand why the question has never been fully answered — because maybe Loch Ness doesn't want to give up its secrets.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Named after Loch Ness, the body of water it allegedly inhabits. The name 'Ness' derives from the River Ness, ultimately from a Celtic word possibly meaning 'roaring one.' The nickname 'Nessie' was popularized by British tabloids in the 1930s.

Aliases — Nessie, Niseag (Scottish Gaelic), The Loch Ness Beast, The Water Kelpie (historical conflation)

Accounts I
Saint Columba and the River Ness Beast (565 CE)Adomnán of Iona, Life of St. Columba, Book 2, Chapter 27 (c. 700 CE)

The earliest recorded encounter with a 'monster' in the Ness watershed. According to Adomnán, St. Columba encountered Picts burying a man killed by a 'water beast' in the River Ness. Columba ordered one of his followers, Lugne Mocumin, to swim across the river. When the beast rose to attack, Columba made the sign of the cross and commanded: 'Go no further! Do not touch the man! Go back at once!' The beast fled 'as if pulled back by ropes.' This account, written a century after Columba's death, is the foundational text of Nessie lore — though skeptics note it describes a river encounter, not a loch sighting.

The Spicer Sighting (1933) — The Modern Nessie Is BornThe Inverness Courier, August 1933

On July 22, 1933, George Spicer and his wife were driving along the new A82 road beside Loch Ness when they saw 'a most extraordinary form of animal' crossing the road ahead. They described a creature with a long neck, a bulky body approximately 4 feet high and 25 feet long, which lurched across the road and disappeared into the loch. The Spicer report was the first widely publicized Nessie sighting and triggered a media frenzy. Within weeks, dozens of additional sightings poured in, and the Loch Ness Monster was born as a global phenomenon. Skeptics later suggested the Spicers may have seen a line of deer crossing the road.

Accounts II
The Surgeon's Photograph (1934) — The Hoax That Defined An IconDaily Mail, April 21, 1934; hoax exposed by The Sunday Telegraph, 1994

The most famous image in cryptozoology was taken by London gynecologist Robert Kenneth Wilson (though the photographer was actually Marmaduke Wetherell). The photograph showed a small head and long neck emerging from the loch's surface — the definitive 'plesiosaur' image. For 60 years, it was treated as the strongest physical evidence for Nessie's existence. In 1994, Christian Spurling, Wetherell's stepson, confessed on his deathbed that the 'monster' was a toy submarine fitted with a sculpted clay head and neck, created as revenge after the Daily Mail had humiliated Wetherell over an earlier Nessie hoax. Despite the confession, the image remains the most recognizable depiction of Nessie — a hoax that permanently shaped how the world imagines an unknown creature.

The 2018 eDNA Survey — Science Weighs InGemmell et al., University of Otago, New Zealand, 2018-2019

An international team led by Professor Neil Gemmell collected 250 water samples from Loch Ness and analyzed environmental DNA (eDNA) — genetic material shed by organisms into their environment. The study found over 3,000 distinct species but ZERO evidence of plesiosaur DNA, sturgeon DNA, catfish DNA, or any other large unknown aquatic vertebrate. What they did find: an abundance of eel DNA. Gemmell proposed the 'giant eel hypothesis' — that large European eels (Anguilla anguilla), which can grow over 1.5 meters, might account for some sightings when surfacing. He was careful not to rule out Nessie entirely: 'There may well be a monster in Loch Ness. We didn't find it.' But the survey effectively closed the book on the plesiosaur hypothesis.

The 1972 'Flipper' Photograph and the Rines ExpeditionsAcademy of Applied Science / Robert Rines, 1972-1975

In 1972, a team led by American lawyer Robert Rines captured underwater photographs appearing to show a diamond-shaped flipper. The images were analyzed by the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and presented to the UK Parliament. In 1975, additional photographs appeared to show a long-necked creature's head and body. These images generated tremendous excitement but have since been dismissed by most scientists as degraded images of the loch bottom, silt clouds, or sonar artifacts. The Rines expeditions represented the peak of serious scientific investment in Nessie — and the beginning of its decline as a credible research subject.
