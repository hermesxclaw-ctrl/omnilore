Inanna, the queen of heaven and earth, who brings both love and war, who rises as the evening star and descends to the underworld.

Who They Are

Inanna (Ishtar in Akkadian/Babylonian) is the Sumerian goddess of love, beauty, sex, desire, fertility, war, and political power. She is the most complex and contradictory deity in the Mesopotamian pantheon — simultaneously the goddess of procreation and destruction, of temple prostitutes and blood-soaked battlefields. Depicted as a winged goddess standing on two lions (her sacred animals), she wears a horned headdress and carries a star-studded rod. Her symbol is the eight-pointed star (Venus). Inanna's most famous myths include: her descent to the underworld (where she is stripped of her powers and killed by her sister Ereshkigal, then resurrected through Enki's intervention); her marriage to the shepherd Dumuzi (who is eventually sent to the underworld as her substitute); and her role in the Epic of Gilgamesh (where Gilgamesh rejects her advances, and she sends the Bull of Heaven to destroy him). Her temple at Uruk (Eanna, 'House of Heaven') was one of the largest religious complexes in the ancient world.

Attested in Sumerian Temple Hymns / The Descent of Inanna / Gilgamesh EpicEra Sumerian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 3000-500 BCE)

Form

Inanna is traditionally depicted as a youthful, beautiful goddess often adorned with a horned headdress signifying divinity. She is frequently shown standing on or accompanied by lions, symbolizing her martial prowess and dominance. Iconography includes wings or wing-like motifs, and she carries the rod and ring symbolizing divine authority. The eight-pointed star, representing the planet Venus, is her primary emblem. Artistic depictions vary from reliefs and cylinder seals to statues, showing her in both martial and sensual poses.

Powers

Domain over Love and Sexuality Inanna governs sexual love, fertility, and procreation, controlling human desires and reproductive forces. She influences both sacred and profane aspects of sexuality, including temple prostitution rituals. Sumerian Temple Hymns; Descent of Inanna Her sexual power is ambivalent, often connected to both life-giving and destructive outcomes; sometimes requires ritual mediation. While predominantly a fertility goddess, some texts emphasize her as a harsh enforcer of chastity or vengeance for sexual violations.

Warrior and Political Power She commands armies, wields weapons, and determines victory in battle. She embodies divine kingship and enforces political authority. Royal inscriptions; Epic of Gilgamesh; Sumerian myths Her martial power can be capricious, sometimes leading to devastation for both enemies and her own followers. Some myths present her as a protector of cities, others as a destroyer; her war aspect varies between regions.

Control over the Mes Inanna is the recipient and possessor of the mes—divine decrees representing civilization's fundamental powers, including crafts, laws, and social institutions. Inanna and Enki myth Mes can be lost and recovered; Inanna’s possession is contested by other deities. Some sources suggest Enki never intended to fully cede the mes to Inanna; her authority over them is sometimes symbolic rather than absolute.

Descent and Return from the Underworld Inanna possesses the unique power to enter the underworld, face death, and be resurrected, symbolizing death and rebirth cycles. Descent of Inanna Her death in the underworld is absolute until rescued; she must provide a substitute for release. Interpretations vary on whether her descent symbolizes seasonal cycles, spiritual transformation, or political allegory.

Divine Justice and Retribution She enforces divine law, punishes transgressions, and exacts vengeance on offenders, including gods and mortals. Various myths including those of Shukaletuda and Bilulu Her retributive actions are sometimes personal and emotional, thus unpredictable. Her justice can be seen as either righteous or cruel depending on the narrative context.

Accounts

Inanna descends into the underworld ruled by her sister Ereshkigal, is struck dead by the seven judges, and after three days is rescued by Enki's agents. She must find a substitute to take her place in the underworld, resulting in her husband Dumuzid's capture.

Inanna visits Enki, the god of wisdom, who drunkenly gives her the mes—symbols of civilization's powers. She brings them to Uruk, establishing her city as a cultural center.

Inanna nurtures a sacred Huluppu tree that is infested by evil creatures. With the help of Gilgamesh, she destroys them and fashions a throne and bed from the tree.

Ishtar proposes marriage to Gilgamesh, who scorns her. In retaliation, she unleashes the Bull of Heaven to punish him, resulting in the death of Enkidu.

After the mortal Shukaletuda rapes Inanna in her sleep, she tracks him down and kills him in retribution.

Content Notes

A mortal man rapes Inanna in her sleep, an act of profound violation against a goddess, leading to her pursuing and killing him in violent retribution.

This myth reflects on divine sovereignty, sexual violence, and justice, illustrating complex gender and power dynamics in Mesopotamian religion.

Sumerian myth

Inanna's death and the substitution of Dumuzid in the underworld hint at ritualistic themes of sacrifice and seasonal death-rebirth cycles.

Interpreted as proto-fertility ritual or symbolic of political power transfer; some scholars argue for a connection with human sacrifice practices.

Descent of Inanna

Ishtar unleashes the Bull of Heaven after Gilgamesh rejects her, leading to the death of Enkidu, a narrative involving divine vengeance with fatal consequences.

Reflects themes of divine capriciousness and the mortal cost of offending gods; the narrative can be seen as a reflection on gendered power and rejection.

Epic of Gilgamesh

Inanna’s enforcement of justice includes destruction of cities (Mount Ebih), execution of offenders, and violent retribution against transgressors.

Demonstrates the harshness of divine law and the intertwining of political and religious violence.

Scholarly Debates
Identity equivalence of Inanna and Ishtar
Inanna and Ishtar are the same goddess with different names in Sumerian and Akkadian cultures. ⇌ Inanna and Ishtar originated as separate deities with distinct cults and attributes before syncretism.
Evidence for A: Overlapping myths, shared symbols, and merged worship sites support unity.
Evidence for B: Differences in emphasis (warrior vs. fertility), cultic practices, and some textual distinctions suggest separate origin
Nature of Inanna’s descent
Symbolizes agricultural and seasonal cycles of death and rebirth. ⇌ Represents a spiritual or political allegory about power and authority.
Evidence for A: Seasonal parallels, agricultural fertility themes, and Dumuzid’s substitution.
Evidence for B: Court politics, divine justice, and gender dynamics interpretations focus on sociopolitical meanings.
Dumuzid’s role in the underworld myth
Passive victim sacrificed in place of Inanna. ⇌ Active participant in the seasonal cycle, embodying fertility’s death and return.
Evidence for A: Texts emphasize Inanna’s substitution demand and Dumuzid’s capture.
Evidence for B: Later texts and interpretations frame Dumuzid’s death as voluntary and cyclical.
Inanna’s moral character
Benevolent goddess of love and justice. ⇌ Capricious, vengeful, and destructive deity.
Evidence for A: Hymns and positive myths celebrating fertility and protection.
Evidence for B: Myths involving violence, wrathful punishment, and destruction.
What We Don't Know

Significant gaps remain in fully understanding the early development and differentiation between Inanna and Ishtar due to fragmentary and sometimes contradictory archaeological and textual evidence. The precise nature of her cult practices, especially concerning temple prostitution and human sacrifice, is debated and poorly documented. The evolution of her worship across the vast timespan from early Sumer to Neo-Babylonian periods is unevenly documented, with many texts lost or untranslated. Her influence on later Mediterranean goddesses is hypothesized but lacks direct evidence. Many Sumerian texts remain untranslated or unpublished, limiting full comprehension of her mythos.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a goddess who embodies the fierce contradictions of human existence — the tender embrace of love and the brutal clash of war, the life-giving fertility of the earth and the chilling finality of death. Inanna, the ancient Queen of Heaven, rises from the dawn of civilization itself as a divine force whose power and mystery continue to captivate and confound. Her myths echo through millennia, revealing a deity who is at once nurturing and ruthless, a symbol of cosmic cycles and earthly power struggles, challenging our notions of divinity, gender, and justice.
