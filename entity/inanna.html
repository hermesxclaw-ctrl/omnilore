Inanna, the queen of heaven and earth, who brings both love and war, who rises as the evening star and descends to the underworld.

Who They Are

Inanna (Ishtar in Akkadian/Babylonian) is the Sumerian goddess of love, beauty, sex, desire, fertility, war, and political power. She is the most complex and contradictory deity in the Mesopotamian pantheon — simultaneously the goddess of procreation and destruction, of temple prostitutes and blood-soaked battlefields. Depicted as a winged goddess standing on two lions (her sacred animals), she wears a horned headdress and carries a star-studded rod. Her symbol is the eight-pointed star (Venus). Inanna's most famous myths include: her descent to the underworld (where she is stripped of her powers and killed by her sister Ereshkigal, then resurrected through Enki's intervention); her marriage to the shepherd Dumuzi (who is eventually sent to the underworld as her substitute); and her role in the Epic of Gilgamesh (where Gilgamesh rejects her advances, and she sends the Bull of Heaven to destroy him). Her temple at Uruk (Eanna, 'House of Heaven') was one of the largest religious complexes in the ancient world.

Attested in Sumerian Temple Hymns / The Descent of Inanna / Gilgamesh EpicEra Sumerian to Neo-Babylonian (c. 3000-500 BCE)

Form

Inanna is traditionally depicted as a youthful, beautiful goddess often adorned with a horned headdress signifying divinity. She is frequently shown standing on or accompanied by lions, symbolizing her martial prowess and dominance. Iconography includes wings or wing-like motifs, and she carries the rod and ring symbolizing divine authority. The eight-pointed star, representing the planet Venus, is her primary emblem. Artistic depictions vary from reliefs and cylinder seals to statues, showing her in both martial and sensual poses.

Powers

Domain over Love and Sexuality Inanna governs sexual love, fertility, and procreation, controlling human desires and reproductive forces. She influences both sacred and profane aspects of sexuality, including temple prostitution rituals. Sumerian Temple Hymns; Descent of Inanna Her sexual power is ambivalent, often connected to both life-giving and destructive outcomes; sometimes requires ritual mediation. While predominantly a fertility goddess, some texts emphasize her as a harsh enforcer of chastity or vengeance for sexual violations.

Warrior and Political Power She commands armies, wields weapons, and determines victory in battle. She embodies divine kingship and enforces political authority. Royal inscriptions; Epic of Gilgamesh; Sumerian myths Her martial power can be capricious, sometimes leading to devastation for both enemies and her own followers. Some myths present her as a protector of cities, others as a destroyer; her war aspect varies between regions.

Control over the Mes Inanna is the recipient and possessor of the mes—divine decrees representing civilization's fundamental powers, including crafts, laws, and social institutions. Inanna and Enki myth Mes can be lost and recovered; Inanna’s possession is contested by other deities. Some sources suggest Enki never intended to fully cede the mes to Inanna; her authority over them is sometimes symbolic rather than absolute.

Descent and Return from the Underworld Inanna possesses the unique power to enter the underworld, face death, and be resurrected, symbolizing death and rebirth cycles. Descent of Inanna Her death in the underworld is absolute until rescued; she must provide a substitute for release. Interpretations vary on whether her descent symbolizes seasonal cycles, spiritual transformation, or political allegory.

Divine Justice and Retribution She enforces divine law, punishes transgressions, and exacts vengeance on offenders, including gods and mortals. Various myths including those of Shukaletuda and Bilulu Her retributive actions are sometimes personal and emotional, thus unpredictable. Her justice can be seen as either righteous or cruel depending on the narrative context.

Possession of the Me (Civilizational Authority) Inanna obtained the me — the fundamental divine decrees governing every aspect of civilization, from kingship to crafts to sexual intercourse to lamentation — from Enki. After getting Enki drunk at a feast, he gave her over one hundred me, which she loaded onto her Boat of Heaven and transported to Uruk. This makes her the deity who brought civilization itself to humanity. Even Enki's demons could not retrieve them. Inanna and Enki: The Transfer of the Me (ETCSL 1.3.1) The me originally belonged to Enki; Inanna needed his (drunken) consent to obtain them. They could theoretically be reclaimed. The exact number and nature of the me varies across sources; some scholars debate whether they represent abstract powers or concrete ritual objects.

Warrior Prowess and Divine Wrath Inanna is a terrifying warrior goddess. She destroyed Mount Ebih for refusing to show her proper respect, unleashing storm, flood, and devastation against it. When Šukaletuda raped her, she turned the water of Sumer to blood and unleashed plagues until she found and killed him. Her battle fury is described in hymns as overwhelming and indiscriminate — she 'rains down fire' and 'makes the earth tremble.' Inanna and Ebih; Inanna and Šukaletuda; The Exaltation of Inanna In Inanna and Ebih, she requires An's permission to destroy the mountain (though she proceeds despite his warning). Her wrath does not make her invulnerable — she can be killed, as in the Descent. The historicity of the Ebih campaign (whether it reflects a real Sumerian conflict) is debated.

Descent into and Return from the Underworld Inanna is the only deity in Sumerian myth who successfully descends into the underworld and returns. Unlike all other gods, she is able to traverse both the heavens and the netherworld. She dies at Ereshkigal's throne and hangs as a corpse for three days and nights before Enki's agents revive her with the food and water of life. This power is unique and represents her ability to transcend cosmic boundaries. Inanna's Descent to the Underworld (Sumerian version) She does not return by her own power — she requires Enki's rescue. The laws of the underworld require a substitute, and she must hand over her husband Dumuzid. The Akkadian version adds fertility themes absent from the Sumerian version. Some scholars argue the myth is primarily about Inanna's astral nature (Venus disappearing and reappearing) rather than death and resurrection.

Association with the Planet Venus Inanna is identified with the planet Venus in both its morning and evening star aspects — a rare recognition in the ancient world that both were the same celestial body. Her movements in myth correspond to Venus's astronomical movements: descending into the underworld mirrors Venus setting in the west, while her reemergence mirrors Venus's return as the morning star. This astral dimension underscores her duality and her ability to traverse boundaries. Multiple Sumerian hymns; cylinder seals from Jemdet Nasr period show awareness that morning and evening stars are the same She does not control Venus; she IS Venus. Her power is tied to the planet's cycles rather than mastery over them. Some scholars argue the astral identification was secondary to her chthonic and royal aspects.

Enforcement of Divine Justice Alongside her twin brother Utu (the sun-god), Inanna is an enforcer of divine justice. She punishes those who transgress against divine order, whether a disrespectful mountain range (Ebih), a rapist gardener (Šukaletuda), or the murderers of her husband (Bilulu and Girgire). Her justice is personal and retributive rather than abstract and procedural. Inanna and Ebih; Inanna and Šukaletuda; Inanna and Bilulu Her justice is reactive — she responds to wrongs against her personally rather than proactively maintaining cosmic order. It is not impartial. Her own actions (the capricious sentencing of Dumuzid, the manipulation of Enki) can be seen as unjust by modern standards.

Love and Sexual Desire Inanna presides over erotic love, sexual desire, and sensuality. She is the subject of the oldest love poetry in world literature (the Courtship of Inanna and Dumuzid), filled with explicit erotic imagery. Mesopotamians invoked her in love incantations. She is sexually active and desiring, taking pleasure in her own sensuality rather than being merely a fertility principle. The Courtship of Inanna and Dumuzid; love incantations; hymns She is specifically NOT a goddess of marriage, motherhood, or domestic fertility. Her love domain is desire and sex, not family. The tension between her role as love goddess and her treatment of Dumuzid (whom she condemns to death) complicates any simple reading of her as a 'love goddess.'

Behavior & Habitat

Inanna is portrayed as young, impetuous, ambitious, and constantly striving for more power than was originally allotted to her. Unlike other gods with static roles, she moves from conquest to conquest — taking the me from Enki, claiming the Eanna temple from An, destroying Mount Ebih for disrespect, and descending into the underworld to extend her authority there. She is fiercely protective of her prerogatives and enforces divine justice ruthlessly against those who wrong her. She is sexually active and desiring but explicitly NOT a mother goddess or a goddess of marriage. She is capricious in love: her treatment of Dumuzid veers from passionate affection to vengeful fury. She is, in the words of one hymn, the goddess who 'turns men into women and women into men.' Her emotional range is extreme — from tender love poetry to world-threatening rage.

Accounts

Inanna, seeking to extend her power into the underworld (or, as she tells the gatekeeper, to attend the funeral of Ereshkigal's husband Gugalanna), descends through the seven gates of Kur. At each gate she is stripped of one piece of her divine regalia — her turban, wig, jewelry, measuring rod, and dress — until she stands naked before her sister Ereshkigal. She attempts to seize Ereshkigal's throne but is struck dead by the seven judges. Her corpse is hung on a hook. After three days, her loyal servant Ninshubur pleads with the gods. Enlil, Nanna, and An all refuse, but Enki creates two sexless beings who enter the underworld, empathize with Ereshkigal's labor pains, and receive Inanna's corpse as a gift. They revive her with the food and water of life. But the galla demons follow her out, demanding a substitute. After protecting her loyal servants Ninshubur, Shara, and Lulal, she finds her husband Dumuzid on her throne, lavishly dressed and un-mourning. Enraged, she condemns him to take her place. Later, moved by the grief of Dumuzid's sister Geshtinanna and his mother, she decrees Dumuzid will spend half the year in the underworld and half above, with Geshtinanna taking the other half.

Inanna travels from Uruk to Eridu to visit Enki, the god of wisdom who possesses the me — the divine decrees governing civilization. Enki hosts a feast and, intoxicated, generously offers her the me one by one. Inanna receives over one hundred me, including kingship, the arts of war, sexual intercourse, music, craftsmanship, and lamentation. She loads them onto her Boat of Heaven and departs for Uruk. When Enki sobers up, he sends various demons and monsters to retrieve the me, but Inanna's servant Ninshubur successfully defends the boat. Inanna arrives in Uruk and delivers the me to her city, making it the center of Sumerian civilization.

Inanna surveys her domains and finds Mount Ebih (possibly the Zagros Mountains) has failed to show her proper reverence. She petitions the sky-god An for permission to destroy it. An warns her that the mountain is fertile and well-watered, producing abundant vegetation, and advises her against attacking. Inanna ignores his warning, declaring herself a warrior goddess. She attacks Ebih with storm, flood, and fire, devastating the mountain completely. The poem is both a hymn to Inanna's destructive power and a statement of her absolute demand for recognition.

Inanna rests beneath a poplar tree in a garden. The gardener Šukaletuda, who has been watching her, removes her clothes and rapes her while she sleeps. When Inanna wakes and realizes what happened, she becomes furiously enraged. She fills all the wells of Sumer with blood, unleashing devastating plagues. Šukaletuda hides among the crowds of the city on his father's advice. Inanna searches the eastern mountains, then sends storms, but still cannot find him. Finally, she threatens to abandon her temple in Uruk unless Enki helps her. Enki assists, and she flies 'across the sky like a rainbow' to locate and kill the gardener.

A cycle of love poems detailing the courtship and marriage of Inanna and the shepherd-god Dumuzid. Inanna initially favors the farmer Enkimdu but is persuaded by her brother Utu to choose Dumuzid. The poems are filled with lush erotic imagery, metaphors of gardens and plowing, and passionate dialogue between the lovers. Dumuzid brings gifts of milk and cream. The poems conclude with their marriage and Inanna's sexual awakening — among the oldest love poetry known to humanity.

What We Don't Know

The exact origins of Inanna — whether she was originally Sumerian, Semitic, or predated both — remain unresolved. The nature of the 'sacred marriage' ritual, if it actually involved physical enactment, is hotly debated. The precise relationship between the Sumerian Inanna and the Semitic Ishtar before their syncretism is only partially understood, as pre-Sargonic evidence for Ishtar is limited. The extent to which Inanna's myths encode astronomical observations of Venus versus being purely narrative is debated. Her full role in daily Sumerian religious life, as opposed to state-level temple worship, is poorly documented. The relationship between Inanna's literary-theological persona and actual women's experience in Mesopotamian society remains a contested topic in gender studies and Assyriology.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine a goddess who embodies the fierce contradictions of human existence — the tender embrace of love and the brutal clash of war, the life-giving fertility of the earth and the chilling finality of death. Inanna, the ancient Queen of Heaven, rises from the dawn of civilization itself as a divine force whose power and mystery continue to captivate and confound. Her myths echo through millennia, revealing a deity who is at once nurturing and ruthless, a symbol of cosmic cycles and earthly power struggles, challenging our notions of divinity, gender, and justice.
