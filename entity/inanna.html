Who It Is

Imagine a goddess who embodies the fierce contradictions of human existence — the tender embrace of love and the brutal clash of war, the life-giving fertility of the earth and the chilling finality of death. Inanna, the ancient Queen of Heaven, rises from the dawn of civilization itself as a divine force whose power and mystery continue to captivate and confound. Her myths echo through millennia, revealing a deity who is at once nurturing and ruthless, a symbol of cosmic cycles and earthly power struggles, challenging our notions of divinity, gender, and justice.

Inanna embodies complex dualities: she is simultaneously nurturing and destructive, loving and warlike, benevolent and wrathful. She is assertive, ambitious, and often depicted as a divine force enforcing justice and power. Her personality in myths reveals traits of cunning, assertiveness, and occasionally vengefulness. She exercises authority over life and death, love and war, and is known for both her compassion and ruthless retribution.

Powers
Domain over Love and Sexuality

Inanna governs sexual love, fertility, and procreation, controlling human desires and reproductive forces. She influences both sacred and profane aspects of sexuality, including temple prostitution rituals.

Limit: Her sexual power is ambivalent, often connected to both life-giving and destructive outcomes; sometimes requires ritual mediation.

Warrior and Political Power

She commands armies, wields weapons, and determines victory in battle. She embodies divine kingship and enforces political authority.

Limit: Her martial power can be capricious, sometimes leading to devastation for both enemies and her own followers.

Control over the Mes

Inanna is the recipient and possessor of the mes—divine decrees representing civilization's fundamental powers, including crafts, laws, and social institutions.

Limit: Mes can be lost and recovered; Inanna’s possession is contested by other deities.

Descent and Return from the Underworld

Inanna possesses the unique power to enter the underworld, face death, and be resurrected, symbolizing death and rebirth cycles.

Limit: Her death in the underworld is absolute until rescued; she must provide a substitute for release.

Divine Justice and Retribution

She enforces divine law, punishes transgressions, and exacts vengeance on offenders, including gods and mortals.

Limit: Her retributive actions are sometimes personal and emotional, thus unpredictable.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Inanna is traditionally depicted as a youthful, beautiful goddess often adorned with a horned headdress signifying divinity. She is frequently shown standing on or accompanied by lions, symbolizing her martial prowess and dominance. Iconography includes wings or wing-like motifs, and she carries the rod and ring symbolizing divine authority. The eight-pointed star, representing the planet Venus, is her primary emblem. Symbols — The eight-pointed star is the quintessential symbol of Inanna/Ishtar, directly linked to Venus, the morning and evening star. Lions symbolize strength and war. Other symbols include the rod and ring, the date palm (fertility), and occasionally weapons such as a bow or sword. The triple aspect of Inanna—in morning star, evening star, and princely forms—is sometimes represented through specific iconographic elements.

Domains — Inanna’s primary domain is Uruk, the city where her chief temple, the Eanna, was located. Her influence spans love, fertility, sexuality, war, political power, and the celestial realm of Venus. She is linked to the heavens, the earth, and the underworld through her mythic descent. Her presence is cosmically significant as the embodiment of both creation and destruction, with seasonal cycles tied to her narratives.

Accounts I
The Descent of InannaSumerian myth, Old Babylonian copies

circa 1900–1600 BCE

Inanna descends into the underworld ruled by her sister Ereshkigal, is struck dead by the seven judges, and after three days is rescued by Enki's agents. She must find a substitute to take her place in the underworld, resulting in her husband Dumuzid's capture. Symbolizes cycles of death and rebirth, the seasonal agricultural cycle, and political power transfer.

Inanna and Enki: The Transfer of the MesSumerian myth

circa 2100 BCE

Inanna visits Enki, the god of wisdom, who drunkenly gives her the mes—symbols of civilization's powers. She brings them to Uruk, establishing her city as a cultural center. Legitimizes Uruk's political and religious primacy; associates Inanna with civilization's foundations.

Accounts II
Inanna and the Huluppu TreeSumerian myth

circa 2000 BCE

Inanna nurtures a sacred Huluppu tree that is infested by evil creatures. With the help of Gilgamesh, she destroys them and fashions a throne and bed from the tree. Highlights Inanna's role in order and civilization and introduces Gilgamesh as a heroic figure.

Ishtar's Proposal to Gilgamesh and the Bull of HeavenEpic of Gilgamesh, Standard Akkadian version

circa 1300–1000 BCE

Ishtar proposes marriage to Gilgamesh, who scorns her. In retaliation, she unleashes the Bull of Heaven to punish him, resulting in the death of Enkidu. Triggers the central crisis of Gilgamesh’s epic journey and confrontation with mortality.

Inanna’s Revenge on ShukaletudaSumerian myth

circa 2000 BCE

After the mortal Shukaletuda rapes Inanna in her sleep, she tracks him down and kills him in retribution. Demonstrates her power to punish sexual transgression and protect divine sovereignty.
