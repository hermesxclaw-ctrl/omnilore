Hathor, the golden one, lady of joy, who fills the world with music and the hearts of the living with love.

Who They Are

Hathor is one of the most ancient and beloved Egyptian goddesses, embodying love, music, dance, fertility, and the sky. She is depicted as a cow, a woman with cow's horns and a solar disk headdress, or a woman with cow's ears. She is the celestial cow who gave birth to the sun, the goddess who welcomes the dead in the afterlife (offering them food and drink from her sycamore tree), and the fierce Eye of Ra who punishes humanity. Her cult center was at Dendera, where the magnificent Temple of Hathor still stands. She was the patroness of miners and workers in foreign lands (as 'Lady of Byblos'). In the Myth of the Heavenly Cow, Ra sent Hathor as his eye to destroy rebellious humans, but she became Sekhmet in her bloodlust. Hathor is associated with the sistrum, the menat necklace, and the mirror. She is the wife of Horus (the Haroeris aspect) and mother of Ihy, the child god of music.

Attested in Pyramid Texts / Coffin Texts / Book of the DeadEra Old Kingdom to Roman Egypt (c. 2600 BCE-400 CE)

Form

Hathor is most commonly depicted as a woman with a cow's head, or as a woman with cow's horns and a solar disk. She is also shown as a full cow, a lioness (in her destructive aspect as the Eye of Ra), a snake, a sycamore tree, or a woman with the ears of a cow. Her iconography often includes a menat necklace (a heavy beaded collar), a sistrum (a rattle used in her cult), and a sun disk between her horns. In her cow form, she is the 'Great Wild Cow' who carries the sun on her back across the sky.

Powers

Celestial Cow As the 'Cow of Heaven,' Hathor is the mother of the sun god Ra. She lifts the sun into the sky each morning and carries it across the heavens, and she swallows it at dusk to be reborn at dawn. This cycle of death and rebirth is central to Egyptian cosmology. Pyramid Texts; Book of the Dead The cycle is dependent on the sun's journey; Hathor is not independent of the cosmic order (Ma'at). In some texts, Nut is the sky goddess who gives birth to the sun, while Hathor is the mother of Horus. The two roles are sometimes conflated.

Eye of Ra Hathor is the personification of the Eye of Ra, the sun god's destructive and protective power. In this aspect, she is a lioness (or a cobra) who annihilates the enemies of the sun god. She is also the force that brings the inundation of the Nile, fertilizing the land. Pyramid Texts; Myth of the Heavenly Cow Her destructive power is unleashed only when Ra commands it, and it can be mitigated by ritual and offerings. The Eye of Ra is also identified with Sekhmet, Bastet, and Tefnut. Hathor and Sekhmet are sometimes seen as two aspects of the same goddess, with Hathor being the calm and Sekhmet the rage.

Goddess of Love and Fertility Hathor governs love, sexuality, and fertility. She is invoked in love spells and fertility rituals, and she is the patron of women in childbirth. She is also associated with the inundation of the Nile, which brings fertility to the land. Papyrus Chester Beatty I; Deir el-Medina votive stelae Her blessing is conditional on proper ritual observance and moral behavior. Hathor's erotic aspect is sometimes at odds with her maternal aspect, though the Egyptians did not see these as contradictory.

Mistress of the West Hathor welcomes the dead into the afterlife, providing them with food, drink, and protection. She is often depicted as a cow emerging from the mountains of the west, the entrance to the underworld. Book of the Dead, Chapter 186 Access to her afterlife is dependent on the judgment of the deceased in the Hall of Ma'at. The role of guide to the afterlife is shared with Anubis, Osiris, and Isis. Hathor's role is more nurturing than judgmental.

Behavior & Habitat

Hathor's personality is dualistic: she is the nurturing mother and the fierce avenger. In her gentle aspect, she is the goddess of love, music, dance, fertility, and joy, who brings abundance and protects women and children. In her destructive aspect, she is the Eye of Ra, a lioness who punishes humanity for their transgressions. This duality is not a contradiction but a reflection of the Egyptian belief that creation and destruction are two sides of the same cosmic force.

Accounts

Ra, the sun god, grew old and humanity plotted against him. Ra sent his Eye, Hathor, to punish humanity. She descended as a lioness and slaughtered humans, wading in their blood. Ra, seeing the destruction, repented and devised a plan to stop her. He had 7,000 jars of beer mixed with red ochre and pomegranate juice, creating a blood-like liquid, and poured it over the battlefield. Hathor, thinking it was blood, drank it and became drunk, falling asleep. When she woke, she had forgotten her rage and returned to Ra as a gentle cow. This myth explains the origin of the Nile inundation (the 'blood' of the flood) and the festival of Hathor, where beer was consumed in great quantities to honor the goddess's drunkenness.

At Dendera, Hathor was worshipped as the consort of Horus of Edfu. Each year, a festival was held where Hathor's statue was brought from Dendera to Edfu to celebrate their divine marriage. The festival was a time of joy, music, and feasting, symbolizing the fertility of the land and the renewal of kingship. The union of Hathor and Horus was seen as a model for the marriage of the pharaoh and his queen.

Hathor is often depicted as a sycamore tree, or as a woman emerging from a sycamore, offering food and drink to the dead. The sycamore was a sacred tree in Egypt, and Hathor was its guardian. In the afterlife, the deceased could find shade and nourishment beneath the sycamore of Hathor, who provided them with bread, beer, and cool water.

The Seven Hathors were a group of seven goddesses who appeared at the birth of a child to foretell their fate. They are depicted as women with cow's horns and solar disks, and they are associated with the seven stars of the Pleiades. In the Tale of the Doomed Prince, the Seven Hathors foretell that a prince will die by a crocodile, a serpent, or a dog, setting the tragic plot in motion.

Hathor vs. Sekhmet as the Eye of Ra
Hathor is the Eye of Ra, as described in the Book of the Heavenly Cow, where she slaughters humanity and then becomes a gentle cow. ⇌ Sekhmet is the Eye of Ra, as described in other versions of the myth, where she is the fierce lioness who nearly destroys humanity.
Evidence for A: Book of the Heavenly Cow (New Kingdom) explicitly names Hathor as the Eye of Ra.
Evidence for B: Numerous hymns and reliefs depict Sekhmet as the Eye of Ra, and she is often called 'the Powerful One.'

What We Don't Know

Further research is needed on: (1) the exact nature of Hathor's cult rituals, particularly any evidence for ritual sexuality; (2) the regional variations in her worship, especially in the Sinai and Nubia; (3) the relationship between Hathor and the Greek Aphrodite in the context of Hellenistic religious syncretism; (4) the role of Hathor in the personal religion of women, particularly in relation to childbirth and fertility; (5) the significance of the Seven Hathors and their connection to the Pleiades; (6) the reception of Hathor in modern neopagan and Kemetic reconstructionist movements.

A way to hold it in your mind

Hathor is the ultimate 'both/and' goddess. She's not just a mother or a lover, a nurturer or a destroyer — she's all of it at once. Think of her as the cosmic force that both creates and destroys, the energy that makes the sun rise and the Nile flood, the joy that makes you dance and the rage that makes you fight. She's the ancient Egyptian answer to the question: 'Can one being be both the gentle hand that feeds you and the fierce eye that judges you?' And her answer is a resounding 'Yes.'
