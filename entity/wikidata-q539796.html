Anna Perenna's domains were the year, its cycle, renewal, spring, abundance, and the people of Rome. She was also closely associated with the Tiber River, as her sacred grove was located near its banks, and with magic and divination, as evidenced by the curse tablets found at her grove.

Form

No definitive ancient description of Anna Perenna's appearance survives. Ovid does not describe her physical form. She is not depicted on any known ancient coin or in any surviving sculpture, suggesting she was a relatively obscure figure whose cult was more important than her iconography. In later artistic representations, she is typically shown as a mature matron, sometimes with a cornucopia or a vessel, reflecting her role as a goddess of abundance and the year's bounty.

Powers

Renewal of the Year Anna Perenna's primary power was to ensure the successful renewal of the year, to guarantee that the cycle of seasons continued, and that the new year would be fruitful. Behavior & Habitat

As a goddess of the year and its renewal, Anna Perenna's behavior is inferred from the nature of her festival. She was a goddess of joyful, unrestrained celebration, of drinking and dancing, of the return of spring and the promise of the new year. She was also a goddess of the people, worshipped by plebeians and senators alike, with a festival that was notably democratic and open to all.

Accounts

Ovid describes the festival of Anna Perenna, celebrated on the Ides of March (March 15). The festival was held at a grove near the Tiber River, where the common people would gather, drink wine, dance, and sing obscene songs. They would pray for as many years as they could drink cups of wine, asking the goddess to grant them a life as long as the cups they could consume. The festival was a time of unrestrained joy and the welcoming of the new year.

Ovid records a myth explaining Anna Perenna's origins. He relates that when the plebeians seceded from Rome to the Mons Sacer (Sacred Mount) in the 5th century BCE, they ran out of food. An old woman named Anna from Bovillae supplied them with cakes. After the reconciliation, the plebeians honored her with a cult, and she became the goddess Anna Perenna.

The most elaborate myth Ovid tells is that Anna Perenna was Anna, the sister of Dido, the legendary Queen of Carthage. After Dido's suicide, Anna fled Carthage and was shipwrecked on the coast of Latium. She was welcomed by Aeneas, who had once been Dido's lover. Aeneas's wife, Lavinia, became jealous and plotted against Anna. Warned in a dream by Dido's ghost, Anna fled and threw herself into the river Numicius, where she became a nymph and was renamed Anna Perenna.

Ovid also records a comic tale in which the god Mars, enamored with the goddess Minerva, sought Anna Perenna's help in wooing her. Anna pretended to be Minerva and went to Mars's bed, only to reveal her true identity. Mars was embarrassed and laughed at, and the other gods mocked him.

Scholarly Debates
Her identity and origins
She was a native Roman goddess of the year and its renewal. ⇌ She was a foreign goddess, either from Carthage (as Dido's sister) or from Greece (as Io).
Evidence for A: Her festival on the Ides of March and her association with the agricultural year.
Evidence for B: Ovid's myths and Macrobius's identification with Io.
The meaning of her name
The name means 'the perennial year' or 'the lasting year'. ⇌ The name is derived from the Greek 'Io' or from the verb 'amare' (to love).
Evidence for A: The linguistic connection to 'annus' and 'perennis'.
Evidence for B: Macrobius's etymology and the romantic tales associated with her.
What We Don't Know

The full text of Varro's De Lingua Latina, which may have contained a more detailed account of Anna Perenna, is lost. The exact rituals of her festival are only partially known, and the nature of the curse tablets found at her grove has not been fully published. The relationship between her cult and the plebeian secession is unclear, and the extent of her worship outside of Rome is unknown.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine the Roman year as a circle, and Anna Perenna is the point where the circle closes and begins again — a goddess of the eternal return, celebrated with the kind of wild, democratic party that would make a modern New Year's Eve look tame. She is the patron saint of second chances, of the spring's promise, and of the simple, joyful belief that the world will renew itself again.
