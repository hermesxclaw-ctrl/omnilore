Who It Is

Before the Ides of March meant death for a dictator, it was a day for the Roman people to get drunk in a sacred grove, sing lewd songs, and pray for a life as long as the number of wine cups they could down. The goddess they toasted was Anna Perenna, the year incarnate — a deity of renewal, abundance, and the kind of unpretentious joy that makes even the gods blush.

As a goddess of the year and its renewal, Anna Perenna's behavior is inferred from the nature of her festival. She was a goddess of joyful, unrestrained celebration, of drinking and dancing, of the return of spring and the promise of the new year. She was also a goddess of the people, worshipped by plebeians and senators alike, with a festival that was notably democratic and open to all.

Powers
Renewal of the Year

Anna Perenna's primary power was to ensure the successful renewal of the year, to guarantee that the cycle of seasons continued, and that the new year would be fruitful. She was a fertility and abundance goddess for the Roman people.

Limit: Her power was tied to the specific date of her festival and the rituals performed there.

Divination and Prophecy

The discovery of curse tablets and magical objects at her grove suggests that Anna Perenna was also a goddess of magic and divination, able to influence the future and to answer the prayers of her devotees, particularly in matters of love and fortune.

Limit: This power is inferred from archaeological evidence and is not explicitly described in literary sources.

Protection of the Roman People

As a goddess of the year and of the people, Anna Perenna was seen as a protector of the Roman state and its citizens, ensuring their prosperity and longevity.

Limit: This is an inferred role based on her status as a public cult.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No definitive ancient description of Anna Perenna's appearance survives. Ovid does not describe her physical form. She is not depicted on any known ancient coin or in any surviving sculpture, suggesting she was a relatively obscure figure whose cult was more important than her iconography. In later artistic representations, she is typically shown as a mature matron, sometimes with a cornucopia or a vessel, reflecting her role as a goddess of abun

Symbols — Her primary symbols were the year itself (often represented by a circle or a serpent eating its own tail, though this is not directly attested for her), the vessel for wine, and the blossoming spring. Her sacred day, the Ides of March, was itself a symbol of her power. The grove and fountain dedicated to her were also sacred symbols.

Domains — Anna Perenna's domains were the year, its cycle, renewal, spring, abundance, and the people of Rome. She was also closely associated with the Tiber River, as her sacred grove was located near its banks, and with magic and divination, as evidenced by the curse tablets found at her grove.

Accounts I
The Festival of Anna PerennaOvid, Fasti 3.523-696

Imperial (c. 8 CE)

Ovid describes the festival of Anna Perenna, celebrated on the Ides of March (March 15). The festival was held at a grove near the Tiber River, where the common people would gather, drink wine, dance, and sing obscene songs. They would pray for as many years as they could drink cups of wine, asking the goddess to grant them a life as long as the cups they could consume. The festival was a time of unrestrained joy and the welcoming of the new year. The festival reinforced the social bonds of the Roman people and ensured the goddess's favor for the coming year.

Anna Perenna and the Flight of the PlebeiansOvid, Fasti 3.663-696

Imperial (c. 8 CE)

Ovid records a myth explaining Anna Perenna's origins. He relates that when the plebeians seceded from Rome to the Mons Sacer (Sacred Mount) in the 5th century BCE, they ran out of food. An old woman named Anna from Bovillae supplied them with cakes. After the reconciliation, the plebeians honored her with a cult, and she became the goddess Anna Perenna. This story explains Anna Perenna's association with the common people and her role as a provider of sustenance.

Accounts II
Anna Perenna as Dido's SisterOvid, Fasti 3.545-656

Imperial (c. 8 CE)

The most elaborate myth Ovid tells is that Anna Perenna was Anna, the sister of Dido, the legendary Queen of Carthage. After Dido's suicide, Anna fled Carthage and was shipwrecked on the coast of Latium. She was welcomed by Aeneas, who had once been Dido's lover. Aeneas's wife, Lavinia, became jealous and plotted against Anna. Warned in a dream by Dido's ghost, Anna fled and threw herself into the river Numicius, where she became a nymph and was renamed Anna Perenna. This myth connects Anna Perenna to the epic history of Rome and to the legendary figure of Aeneas, giving her a noble and tragic origin.

Anna Perenna and MarsOvid, Fasti 3.675-696

Imperial (c. 8 CE)

Ovid also records a comic tale in which the god Mars, enamored with the goddess Minerva, sought Anna Perenna's help in wooing her. Anna pretended to be Minerva and went to Mars's bed, only to reveal her true identity. Mars was embarrassed and laughed at, and the other gods mocked him. This story explains the ribald, obscene songs sung at her festival, which were said to mock the gods and to celebrate trickery.

Anna Perenna was a minor and unimportant goddess. — While she was not one of the major deities of the Roman pantheon, her festival was a major public celebration, and her cult was important enough to have a sacred grove and to be the subject of multiple literary and antiquarian accounts.

Anna Perenna was a goddess of the new year as celebrated on January 1st. — Her festival was celebrated on the Ides of March (March 15), which was the original Roman new year before the calendar was reformed. Her association was with the agricultural and ritual year, not the civil year.

Anna Perenna was a purely mythological figure with no real cult. — The discovery of her sacred grove and the curse tablets found there prove that she had a real, active cult with devotees who made offerings and sought her help.
