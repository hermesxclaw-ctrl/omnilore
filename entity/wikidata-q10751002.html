Who It Is

In the shadowed halls of early Germanic legend, where history and myth entwine like the roots of Yggdrasil, there stands a figure whose name echoes through the ages: Hild, the warrior-maiden of the shield-maiden tradition, a woman whose very being is a testament to the fierce and unyielding spirit of the North. Yet, as with many figures from the misty reaches of the Dark Ages, her story is a tapestry of fragments, some woven from the threads of historical reality, others from the loom of poetic imagination. To meet Hild is to peer into a world where the boundaries between the living and the legendary blur, and where the deeds of a single woman could inspire songs that would outlast empires.

In Saxo's version, Hild is passive, an object of desire and dispute. In the Hildesage, she is more active, deliberately reviving the fallen to perpetuate the battle, suggesting a willful and perhaps malevolent nature. In the Eddic tradition, as a valkyrie, she is a martial figure, serving Odin and determining the fates of warriors.

It is a common element in Germanic female names, often appearing in compounds such as Brunhild (armored battle) and Kriemhild (battle mask). Powers
Valkyrie powers

As a valkyrie, Hild is able to fly over battlefields, choose which warriors die, and serve them in Valhalla. She is also immune to the normal constraints of mortality.

Limit: Her powers are subject to the will of Odin, and she is not omnipotent.

Revival of the dead

In the Hildesage, Hild uses magic to revive the fallen warriors each night, ensuring that the battle never ends.

Limit: The power seems to be limited to the battlefield and requires her active will.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Depictions of Hild are scarce and vary. In Saxo's account, she is described as a beautiful maiden, the daughter of a king. In later Norse tradition, she is often imagined as a valkyrie, a warrior woman with shield and spear, riding a horse and choosing the slain. No consistent physical description survives.

Symbols — As a valkyrie, Hild is associated with symbols of battle: the shield, spear, and raven. In the Hildesage, she is a catalyst for endless conflict, symbolizing the futility and cyclical nature of war.

Domains — Hild is associated with the battlefield and the realm of the dead. As a valkyrie, she resides in Valhalla or travels the battlefields of Midgard. In the Hildesage, the setting is a mythical island or battlefield where the endless battle takes place.

Accounts I
The Abduction of HildaSaxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum

Medieval (c. 1200)

Hilda, daughter of King Högni, is abducted by Hedin, a warrior. Högni pursues them, leading to a battle between the two men. The conflict is eventually resolved, but the tale is a classic example of a bride-rape motif. The battle between Hedin and Högni becomes legendary, and Hild is the cause of a deadly feud.

Accounts II
The Everlasting BattleHildesage (Þáttr Þorsteins)

Late medieval (c. 14th century)

Hild, daughter of Högni, is abducted by Hedin. Högni pursues, and the two armies clash. Each night, Hild revives the dead, so the battle continues forever. The saga ends with the battle still raging. The endless battle becomes a warning against the futility of vengeance and the destructive power of a woman's will.

Hild as ValkyriePoetic Edda, Völuspá

Viking Age (oral tradition), written c. 13th century

Hildr is listed among the valkyries who choose the slain and serve in Valhalla. No individual story is told, but she is part of the mythological framework. She is a minor figure in the pantheon, but her name is used as a poetic term for battle.
