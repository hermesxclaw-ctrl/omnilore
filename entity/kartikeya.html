Who It Is

Kartikeya, also known as Skanda, Murugan, Subrahmanya, and Shanmukha (the Six-Faced One), is the Hindu god of war and the commander-in-chief of the divine armies. He is the son of Shiva and Parvati, born for the specific purpose of defeating the demon Tarakasura, who had obtained the boon that he could only be killed by a son of Shiva. At the time of Kartikeya's birth, Shiva was deep in meditation, and the task of bearing the seed of his divine energy was extraordinarily complex: the seed was so intensely potent that Agni (fire god) had to carry it, Ganga (the river goddess) had to receive it, and six Krittika (Pleiades) nymphs nursed the infant, from whom he gained his six faces (so he could drink from all six simultaneously) and his name Kartikeya ('son of the Krittikas'). In his most iconic form, Kartikeya is a handsome, eternally youthful warrior riding a peacock named Paravani, wielding a spear called Vel—a divine weapon given by his mother Parvati that never misses its target. He defeated Tarakasura and his brothers, establishing cosmic order against demonic chaos. Kartikeya's worship is particularly fervent in South India, especially Tamil Nadu, where he is known as Murugan and is arguably the most popular deity after Ganesha. The six sacred abodes of Murugan (Arupadai Veedu) in Tamil Nadu are major pilgrimage sites. In North India, his worship declined significantly after the Gupta period, though he remains important in the Skanda Purana. By contrast, in Sri Lanka and among the Tamil diaspora in Malaysia, Singapore, and Mauritius, Murugan worship is extremely vibrant. Kartikeya is associated with the planet Mars, the color red, the rooster (which appears on his battle standard), and Tuesday. The festival of Thaipusam, celebrated with dramatic acts of devotion including body piercing, is dedicated to Murugan receiving the Vel from his mother.

Form

Attested across Sanskrit and Tamil sources, Kartikeya/Murugan is typically portrayed as a youthful, radiant warrior deity. Iconographically, he often has six faces (Ṣaṇmukha) and twelve arms, each bearing weapons or symbolic attributes, with a strong emphasis on the spear (Vel). He is usually shown standing or riding a peacock (mayil), sometimes accompanied by a rooster emblem. His body is frequently described as resplendent, red or golden in hue, embodying youth, beauty, and martial vigor. In some North Indian depictions, he appears with a single face and fewer arms, reflecting regional iconographic simplification. These features are attested in Puranic descriptions and Tamil devotional poetry; details such as exact color or ornamentation vary by temple and artistic tradition.

Powers

Command of celestial armies (Devasenāpati) Kartikeya is repeatedly described as the commander-in-chief of the gods’ armies, leading them against powerful asuras such as Tārakāsura and Sūrapadman. This role includes strategic leadership, martial prowess, and the ability to inspire divine and semi-divine warriors. Skanda Purāṇa; Śiva Purāṇa; Tamil Kandha Purāṇam; general Puranic tradition. His command is exercised within the framework of dharma; he fights to restore cosmic order rather than for personal gain. Narratives do not present him as omnipotent—he is empowered by boons and divine mandate, and his battles require preparation, weapons, and support from other deities. No major contradictions about his role as commander, though some regional traditions emphasize his spiritual teacher aspect more than his martial leadership.

Martial prowess and demon-slaying He is famed for slaying or subduing demons such as Tārakāsura, Simhamukha, and Sūrapadman, often in elaborate battle narratives. His combat skills include use of the Vel, other weapons, and supernatural strength. Skanda Purāṇa; Kandha Purāṇam; various Puranic and Tamil texts. His victories are often contingent on specific boons or conditions (e.g., only a son of Śiva can kill Tārakāsura). He must undergo training and receive weapons from deities like Pārvatī. Different texts vary in the list of demons and the exact sequence of battles; some emphasize Tārakāsura, others Sūrapadman, and details of how demons are transformed or killed differ.

Use of the Vel (spear) as weapon and spiritual instrument The Vel is his primary weapon, given by Pārvatī, used to pierce and destroy demonic forces. Devotional literature also treats the Vel as a symbol of cutting through ignorance and granting protection. Tamil devotional texts (Thiruppugazh, Kanda Sashti Kavasam); Skanda Purāṇa; temple iconography. The Vel’s power is invoked through devotion, ritual, and Murugan’s grace; it is not depicted as an autonomous force acting without his will. No major doctrinal contradictions, though interpretations range from literal weapon to purely symbolic spiritual tool.

Youthful vitality and bestowal of courage Murugan embodies eternal youth and vigor, granting courage, strength, and confidence to devotees, especially in facing obstacles and enemies. Tamil devotional literature; general Puranic descriptions. This is a devotional and theological attribute rather than a discrete narrative power; its effects are mediated through faith and ritual. None significant; emphasis varies by devotional tradition.

Spiritual wisdom and teaching In some South Indian traditions, Murugan is portrayed as a teacher who explains the meaning of Om or other esoteric doctrines, even to Śiva, symbolizing his mastery of spiritual knowledge. South Indian temple myths (e.g., Swamimalai); Tamil commentarial traditions. These narratives are localized and not universally attested across all Puranic sources; they function as theological statements about his wisdom. In broader Hindu theology, Śiva is often the supreme teacher; stories where Murugan instructs Śiva can be seen as regional reversals or playful theological inversions.

Protection and healing through devotion Hymns such as Kanda Sashti Kavasam present Murugan as a protector who shields devotees from physical and spiritual harm, including disease, misfortune, and malevolent forces. Kanda Sashti Kavasam; Thiruppugazh; modern devotional practice. Protection is conditional on devotion, recitation of hymns, and moral conduct; not portrayed as automatic or mechanical. Devotional claims of miraculous protection vary and are not subject to historical verification; they belong to lived religious experience rather than textual contradiction.

Accounts

Tārakāsura, a powerful demon, gains a boon that only a son of Śiva can kill him. Śiva, immersed in ascetic meditation after Satī’s death, seems unlikely to father a child. The gods enlist Kāmadeva to awaken Śiva, but Śiva burns Kāma with his third eye. Through Pārvatī’s intense tapas, Śiva eventually accepts her as his consort. From Śiva’s fiery energy (tejas) emerge six sparks, carried by Agni and deposited in a reed forest (Śaravana) by the Gaṅgā. There, six infants are born and nurtured by the six Kṛttikā star-maidens. When Pārvatī embraces them, they merge into one six-faced child, Kārttikeya, destined to lead the gods’ armies.

After his birth and training, Kartikeya is appointed commander of the gods’ armies. Armed with the Vel and supported by the devas, he confronts Tārakāsura, whose boon makes him invulnerable except to Śiva’s son. In a fierce battle, Kartikeya pierces Tārakāsura with the Vel, ending his tyranny and freeing the cosmos from his oppression.

Sūrapadman, a powerful demon, oppresses the worlds and defies the gods. Kartikeya leads the divine armies against him, engaging in a prolonged battle. Ultimately, Kartikeya defeats Sūrapadman, but instead of annihilating him completely, he transforms the demon into his peacock mount and rooster emblem, symbolically subjugating but not entirely destroying him.

In Tamil narratives, Murugan marries two consorts. Devasena (Deivayanai), daughter of Indra, represents divine authority and is often given to Murugan in gratitude for his victories. Valli, a tribal maiden or goddess, represents love, devotion, and the integration of folk traditions. Their marriages are celebrated in temple festivals and ritual reenactments, emphasizing Murugan’s role as both royal and folk deity.

At Swamimalai, a famous Murugan shrine, a local myth recounts that Murugan explains the meaning of the sacred syllable Om to Śiva. When Śiva asks about its significance, Murugan insists that the questioner must stand as a student, reversing the usual hierarchy. Śiva humbly listens, and Murugan expounds the esoteric meaning, symbolizing his mastery of spiritual knowledge.
