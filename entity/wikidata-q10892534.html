Who It Is

When Izanagi lit a single tooth-comb torch in the darkness of Yomi, he did not find his wife. He found what his wife had become: a swollen, rotting corpse, and crawling upon it — thunder itself. Eight thunders, coiled around her head, chest, belly and limbs, crackling and roaring as living gods. The Kojiki calls them Yakusa no Ikazuchi — the Eight Thunders — and they did not stay to be observed. They rose with the hags of the underworld to chase the living out of death.

Behavior is attested only in one narrative context: as pursuers. When Izanami, shamed and enraged that Izanagi has seen her polluted form, orders the Yomotsu-shikome (Hags of Yomi), then the Yakusanoikazuchi, then the Yomotsu-ikusa (150 warriors of Yomi) to chase Izanagi, the thunders obey and join the chase. They are hostile to the living, agents of Yomi's pollution and of Izanami's wrath. No dialogue, individual personality, or motive beyond this pursuit is recorded. They do not speak, bargain, or reappear after Izanagi escapes and blocks the pass of Yomotsu Hirasaka with a boul

Powers
Ō-ikazuchi (Great Thunder)

Embodies the overarching, overwhelming peal of thunder; associated with Izanami's head, suggesting thunder as a commanding, head-born roar. In ritual interpretation, the 'great' thunder is the loudest, most terrifying clap.

Limit: No independent feats beyond participation in the pursuit of Izanagi. Cannot leave Yomi after the boulder seals the pass in any attested text.

Hono-ikazuchi (Fire Thunder)

Thunder that is fiery, flashing, or that sets fires — the lightning-strike aspect. Born from Izanami's breasts/chest, linking fire/lightning to the breast as a source of nourishment turned destructive.

Limit: No attested ability to control fire independently of thunder; description is epithetic, not a demonstrated power.

Kuro-ikazuchi (Black Thunder)

Thunder of black clouds, darkness, or the dark thunder of the underworld. Epithet suggests storm-cloud blackness or the blackness of putrefaction.

Limit: Anatomical placement is the main textual variant; power description is inferential from the name.

Saku-ikazuchi (Cleaving / Rending Thunder)

Thunder that splits — the cleaving, cracking thunder that splits trees, rocks, and roofs. Name from saku (to split, to cleave).

Limit: No narrative feat of cleaving is described; name is the only evidence for the nature of the thunder.

Waka-ikazuchi (Young Thunder)

Young or fresh thunder — perhaps the sharp, youthful crack, or thunder of early summer (waka as young). Could also imply a weaker or newly born thunder.

Limit: No distinct mythic action; epithet may be age-based or seasonal.

Tsuchi-ikazuchi (Earth Thunder)

Thunder that strikes or rumbles in/through the earth — ground-shaking thunder, possibly earthquake-associated thunder or thunder that burrows.

Limit: Not to be confused with earth deities; no separate earth-shaking feat narrated.

Naru-ikazuchi (Rumbling / Roaring Thunder)

The prolonged rumbling, rolling thunder — from naru (to rumble, to sound). The sustained growl after the initial clap.

Limit: Descriptive epithet only.

Fusu-ikazuchi (Couchant / Prostrate / Creeping Thunder)

Thunder that crouches, crawls, or lies prostrate — perhaps low, creeping thunder that rolls along the ground or a thunder that lurks. From fusu (to crouch, lie prostrate).

Limit: Most obscure epithet; no narrative elaboration.

Collective Pursuit and Pollution Embodiment

As a group, the eight can pursue a fleeing kami across Yomi, embody kegare (death pollution), and act as extensions of Izanami's wrath. Their presence causes terror sufficient to make Izanagi flee and later require misogi (purification).

Limit: They fail to catch Izanagi; they are stopped by his use of magical obstacles (black headdress turned to grapes, comb turned to bamboo shoots) and finally by the boulder at Yomotsu Hirasaka. They do not appear outside Yomi.

Accounts I
The Death of Izanami and the Birth of KagutsuchiKojiki Sect. 7-8; Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki Book I

Nara period codification (712/720), reflecting earlier Yayoi-Kofun oral tradition

Izanagi and Izanami, the primordial creator couple, birth the islands and many kami. When Izanami gives birth to Kagutsuchi (Hinokagutsuchi), the fire kami, she is horribly burned and dies. Izanagi kills Kagutsuchi in grief, from whose blood and body further kami are born. Izanami is buried at Mt. Hiba on the border of Izumo and Hoki, and descends to Yomi-no-kuni. This death creates the first impurity and sets the stage for the thunders' emergence — they are born from the consequences of fire, death, and decay. Establishes Yomi as a polluted realm and Izanami as its ruler; creates the kegare that will later require Izanagi's purification and the birth of Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo. Without this death, the Yakusanoikazuchi would not exist.

Izanagi's Descent to Yomi and the Torch of the CombKojiki Sect. 9-10; Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki

Nara period

Grief-stricken, Izanagi follows Izanami to Yomi to bring her back. He finds her and begs her to return. Izanami says she has already eaten the food of Yomi and cannot easily leave, but will ask the gods of Yomi. She makes Izanagi promise not to look at her while she consults them. Izanagi waits, then breaks his promise: he breaks a tooth from his comb (tama-kushi) and lights it as a torch. In its light he sees Izanami's body swollen and rotting, maggots swarming, and the eight thunder kami coiled upon her corpse — Ō-ikazuchi on the head, Hono-ikazuchi on the breasts, Kuro-ikazuchi on the belly (or rectum), and the others on limbs. Terrified, he flees. Izanagi's violation of the taboo (seeing the polluted form) and his terror trigger Izanami's shame and rage, leading directly to the pursuit. The scene codifies the Shinto association of death, decay, and darkness with kegare and establishes the thunders' origin as corpse-born.

The Flight from Yomi: Hags, Thunders, and the 150 WarriorsKojiki Sect. 10; Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki

Nara period

Shamed that Izanagi has seen her polluted form, Izanami declares he has shamed her and sends the Yomotsu-shikome (the Hags/Shrews of Yomi) after him. Izanagi flees, and when the hags pursue, he throws down his black headdress (kuro-mikazura), which turns into grapes (or wild grapes, ebikazura), and the hags stop to eat them. He then throws his comb, which turns into bamboo shoots, again delaying them. Izanami then sends the Yakusanoikazuchi and the Yomotsu-ikusa (150 warriors of Yomi) in pursuit. Izanagi draws his sword (Totsuka no Tsurugi) and flees, using peaches (momo) from a peach tree at Yomotsu Hirasaka to drive off the pursuers — peaches having apotropaic power. He finally reaches the pass of Yomotsu Hirasaka and rolls a huge boulder (Chibiki no Iwa) to block the entrance to Yomi. The boulder seals Yomi from the living world, creating the boundary between life and death. Izanagi and Izanami speak through the boulder, pronouncing the first divorce and the establishment of mortality: Izanami will strangle 1,000 living each day, Izanagi will birth 1,500 each day. Accounts II
The Anatomical Variant: Kuro-ikazuchi's Contested OriginKojiki Sect. 10 vs. Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki variant notes (Aston Vol. I p. 21-22)

Nara period compilation; scholarly debate from Heian to modern

The Kojiki states Kuro-ikazuchi (Black Thunder) was 'on her belly' (hara ni imasu). A Nihon Shoki variant states Kuro-ikazuchi was 'in her rectum/anus' (尻, shiri/ketsu). This is not a trivial difference: it reflects differing levels of explicit pollution imagery and taboo. The Kojiki's belly is already polluted but less taboo than the rectum, which is the ultimate site of impurity. The variant shows that early transmitters were willing to make the horror more visceral, emphasizing kegare at its most extreme. Modern scholars use this variant to discuss how kegare was conceptualized and how taboo language was handled in court chronicles. It also fuels popular fascination and body-horror retellings of the Yomi scene.

Misogi: Purification After Yomi and the Birth of the Three Noble ChildrenKojiki Sect. 11-12; Nihon Shoki Jindai-ki

Nara period

After sealing Yomi, Izanagi feels polluted (kegare) from his contact with the dead and the thunders. He goes to Himuka (Hyuga), at the plain of Ahakihara by the Tachibana River, to perform misogi (purification by washing). As he discards his clothes and washes, many kami are born from his garments and from the washing itself, including the three noble children (Mihashira no Uzu no Miko): Amaterasu Ōmikami from his left eye, Tsukuyomi no Mikoto from his right eye, and Susanoo no Mikoto from his nose. The misogi is the direct ritual response to the pollution embodied by Izanami's corpse and the Yakusanoikazuchi. Establishes misogi and harae as central Shinto purification rites, still performed today. Establishes the heavenly lineage that will lead to the imperial house. The thunders' pollution is the theological reason purification is necessary.

Afterlife in Exegesis: Motoori Norinaga and the Kegare ReadingMotoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den (1798); modern scholarship: Philippi, Heldt, Aston commentaries; Kokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto

Edo period to present

Motoori Norinaga, the great kokugaku scholar, reads the Yakusanoikazuchi not as mere monsters but as kami who are simultaneously divine and polluted — proof that kami-ness is not moral purity but awesome power. He argues the maggots and thunders are the same phenomenon seen at different levels: the maggots are the physical decay, the thunders are the spiritual potency of that decay. Modern scholars (e.g., Nelly Naumann, Michael Ashkenazi) extend this to argue the eight thunders represent eight kinds of storm experienced by early Japanese agriculturalists, projected onto the corpse to explain why thunder was both feared and revered. Shapes how Shinto theology understands kegare, harae, and the ambivalence of kami. Influences modern encyclopedia entries that describe Yakusanoikazuchi as 'thunder deities of pollution' rather than simple storm gods.

Yakusanoikazuchi is a single thunder god named Yakusanoikazuchi. — It is a collective noun for eight distinct thunder kami. No single kami bears the name Yakusanoikazuchi alone; the name means 'the Eight Thunders.'

The eight thunders are the same as Raijin, the drummer thunder god. — Raijin is a later, single, sky-dwelling demon with drums, prominent from the Heian period onward. The Yakusanoikazuchi are eight Yomi-bound kami born on a corpse in the 8th-century chronicles, with no drums or sky palace. They occupy different mythic spaces.

Each of the eight thunders has a detailed individual myth, personality, and appearance. — The primary sources give only names (epithets for thunder types) and, for three, a body-part association. No individual stories, dialogues, or visual descriptions survive. Individual personalities are modern inventions for games and fiction.

The Kojiki says the thunders were maggots. — The Kojiki says maggots swarmed AND eight thunders were present. Whether the thunders emerged from the maggots or alongside them is ambiguous and varies by Nihon Shoki variant. The texts do not simply say 'the maggots were the thunders.'

Yakusanoikazuchi are worshipped at thunder shrines across Japan. — There is no major shrine dedicated to the eight as a collective in the Engi Shiki shrine list. Thunder worship in Japan is generally directed to Raijin, Tenjin (Sugawara no Michizane as thunder god), or local kaminari shrines, not to the Yomi eight. Their role is primarily mythic, not cultic.
