The Approach

She was one of fifty sisters, all bedded by the same hero in a single night—or was it seven? Pyrippe, the 'fiery mare' of Thespiae, is a footnote in the mighty legend of Heracles, yet her son would become one of the greatest warriors of the Trojan War. Her story is a strange blend of hospitality, deception, and heroic lineage.

Content advisory. Contains themes of non-consensual sexual encounter and exploitation of women, as depicted in ancient myth.
Identity

Pyrippe

Pronunciation pih-RIP-ee (English approximation); Ancient Greek: /py.ríp.pɛː/

Etymology

Ancient Greek Πυρίππη (Puríppē), literally 'fiery mare' (from πῦρ 'fire' and ἵππος 'horse'). The name may evoke swiftness, wildness, or a fiery nature.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology; specifically the mythological tradition of Thespiae in Boeotia. She is a minor figure, known only as one of the fifty daughters of King Thespius and as a lover of Heracles.

Disputed Identifications

No known disputes about her identity, but she is often conflated with her sisters in the various lists of the Thespiades. Some ancient sources give different names for the daughters, and Pyrippe may appear under variant names in different accounts.

Description

Appearance

No ancient source describes Pyrippe's physical appearance. As a princess of Thespiae, she would have been of noble birth, but no specific attributes are recorded.

Symbols & Iconography

No known iconography. She is not depicted in ancient art, and her name ('fiery mare') is not reflected in any known visual representation.

Behaviour & Personality

No individual personality traits are recorded. In the myth, she is a passive participant in Heracles' encounter, being one of many daughters who unknowingly slept with him. Her role is defined by her offspring, not by any actions or choices of her own.

Domains & Habitats

Thespiae, a city in Boeotia, Greece. The myth is set in the region of Mount Cithaeron, where Heracles hunted the lion.

Version Differences

The basic story is consistent across sources: Pyrippe is one of the fifty daughters, and she bears Patroclus to Heracles. However, the details of the encounter vary: some say Heracles slept with all fifty in one night (or over fifty nights), others over seven nights, and some accounts mention one daughter who refused and was punished. Pyrippe is not the one who refused in any known version.

Stories & Myths

The Daughters of Thespius

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.8; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.3; Pausanias 9.27.6-7; Tzetzes, Chiliades 2Culture GreekPeriod Mythic (Heroic Age)

Summary

When the Cithaeronian lion was ravaging the land of Thespius, the king asked Heracles to kill it. Heracles hunted the lion for fifty days and succeeded. During this time, Thespius entertained him lavishly, and each night sent one of his fifty daughters to his bed, hoping that they would all bear children by the great hero. Heracles, believing he was with the same woman each night, unknowingly slept with all fifty. Pyrippe was one of these daughters, and she conceived Patroclus.

Consequences

Pyrippe gave birth to Patroclus, who later became a famous hero in the Trojan War, the close companion of Achilles. The other daughters also bore sons, who became the ancestors of noble families in Thespiae and elsewhere.

Variants

Diodorus says the encounter took place over seven nights, with seven daughters each night. Another version (possibly from Herodorus, via Athenaeus) says one daughter refused to sleep with Heracles and was condemned to remain a virgin and serve as his priestess. In some lists, the names of the daughters differ, and it is possible that Pyrippe is sometimes called by another name.

Relationships

Heracles

Type lover (unwitting)Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.8

Description

Pyrippe was one of the fifty daughters who slept with Heracles while he was a guest of her father. She bore him a son, Patroclus.

Version Note

In all versions, the encounter is non-consensual on her part in the sense that she was sent by her father and Heracles was deceived into thinking he was with the same woman each night. However, the myth does not portray it as a violation; it is treated as a heroic feat and a blessing for Thespius.

Conflicting Accounts

None specifically about Pyrippe, but the circumstances vary: some say it happened over fifty nights, others over seven, and some say all in one night.

Non-consensual sexual encounter

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.8; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.3

Description

Pyrippe and her sisters were sent by their father to sleep with Heracles without his knowledge that he was with different women. They were likely not given a choice, and the encounter was orchestrated for the purpose of bearing heroic children.

Scholarly Context

Ancient Greek myths often depict sexual encounters with gods and heroes as consensual or even desirable, but modern readers may view this as exploitative. Scholars note that the myth reflects the importance of heroic lineage and the subordination of women in ancient Greek society.

Frazer's English translation (1921) lists Pyrippe as one of the daughters who bore a son to Heracles; she bore Patroclus.

Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca Historica 4.29.3

Type textDate 1st century BCELocator Book 4, chapter 29, section 3Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Diodorus gives a similar account of Heracles sleeping with the fifty daughters, but does not name them individually. He is a secondary source for the general myth.

Pausanias, Description of Greece 9.27.6-7

Type textDate 2nd century CELocator Book 9, chapter 27, sections 6-7Language Ancient Greek

Translation

Pausanias mentions the daughters of Thespius and their children, but does not name Pyrippe specifically. He provides context for the Thespian cult of Heracles.

Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 13.556

Type textDate late 2nd/early 3rd century CELocator Book 13, chapter 556 (Kaibel edition)Language Ancient Greek

Athenaeus cites earlier authors (e.g., Herodorus) on the story of Heracles and the Thespiades, but does not name Pyrippe.

John Tzetzes, Chiliades (Book of Histories) 2.221-230

Type textDate 12th century CELocator Chiliad 2, lines 221-230Language Medieval Greek

Tzetzes provides a list of the daughters and their offspring, including Pyrippe as mother of Patroclus. This is a late Byzantine source, but it preserves earlier traditions.
