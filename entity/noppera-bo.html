Who It Is

Imagine walking down a dark road, coming upon a young woman weeping — her shoulders shaking, her face hidden behind a sleeve. You approach to help. She lowers her arm. Where her face should be, there is nothing — not eyes, not a nose, not a mouth, not even a scar — just a smooth, pale expanse of skin, like an egg, like a whitewashed wall, like the face of a doll that was never painted. And when you turn and run, when you finally find a friendly face behind a counter and begin gasping out what you saw, that face too becomes blank — and a voice asks, 'Was it anything like this?'

The noppera-bō is primarily a trickster and frightener, motivated by mischief rather than malice. It is described in nearly all accounts as 'harmless' to humans in a physical sense — it does not attack, steal, kill, or curse. Its behavior follows a consistent pattern: (1) assumption of a mundane human identity in an everyday setting, (2) establishment of social context (a weeping woman needing aid, a food vendor, a fellow traveler), and (3) a sudden, shocking revelation of the blank face designed to maximize psychological terror. Powers
Perfect human disguise

The noppera-bō can appear as any ordinary human, indistinguishable from a real person until the moment of facial revelation. It can impersonate specific social roles: a weeping young woman, a soba noodle vendor, a Buddhist abbot, a teahouse proprietress, a fellow mountain traveler, a bob-haired girl. The disguise extends to clothing, posture, speech, and — crucially — the concealment of the blank face until the moment of dramatic reveal.

Limit: The disguise has at least one potential vulnerability: according to some folkloric accounts, the creature may slightly glow or luminesce like a kitsune or mujina in its transformed state, giving away its nature to a 'true hero' or perceptive observer. The Kaneyama-ki account suggests that the creature 'disappears' when physically overpowered, implying that its physical form may be unstable or illusory under duress.

Faceless revelation

The creature's primary power is the sudden transformation from an apparently normal human face to a completely blank, featureless expanse of skin. This is not a gradual change but a shocking instantaneous revelation — usually accomplished by slowly lowering a hand or sleeve from the face, building dramatic tension before the reveal. The psychological impact of seeing a human figure with no facial features — no eyes with which to make contact, no mouth with which to speak or scream, no nose with which to breathe — is consistently described as overwhelming and terrifying, causing victims to flee in panic.

Limit: No physical harm is reported from this ability. It is purely a tool of psychological terror.

Recurrent spooking (saido no kai / 再度の怪)

Perhaps the most distinctive narrative power: the noppera-bō often appears in pairs or sequences, terrorizing the victim, allowing them to flee to a 'safe' location (a house, a teashop, a temple), and then revealing itself AGAIN in the person the victim turns to for help. This double-scare structure is the hallmark of the most famous noppera-bō stories and has been identified by folklorists as a distinct narrative motif called saido no kai. The effect on the victim is devastating — the safe space is violated, trust in the ordinary world is shattered, and the boundary between reality and nightmare becomes permanently blurred.

Limit: The power appears to be duplicative rather than shapeshifting — it is unclear whether two separate noppera-bō coordinate, or the same creature teleports ahead of the fleeing victim.

Instantaneous disappearance

In the Kaneyama-ki account, when young Toki Akutarō wrestled the faceless yamabushi to the ground, the creature simply 'disappeared' — vanished without a trace. This ability to phase out of physical combat suggests that the noppera-bō's body may be illusory or insubstantial, comparable to other yōkai whose physical forms are not fixed in ordinary matter.

Limit: Unknown — only one account describes this ability.

Symbols — The noppera-bō is iconographically represented by the stark contrast between a fully dressed human body and a completely blank, featureless face — an image of radical absence that is simultaneously mundane and deeply unsettling. Traditional woodblock prints (ukiyo-e) depict the creature as a human figure in period dress (often a woman in a kimono holding a sleeve to her face) with a white, egg-like visage. Accounts I
The Mujina of the Akasaka RoadKwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things (1904), Lafcadio Hearn

Edo period setting; 1904 publication

An old merchant travelling along the Akasaka Road to Edo (Tokyo) at night encounters a young woman weeping near Kii-no-Kunizaka hill. She appears to be in distress, possibly contemplating suicide. When the merchant approaches to help her, she turns to face him, dropping the sleeve from her face — revealing a face completely smooth, with 'no eyes or nose or mouth'. Terrified, the merchant flees down the hill and reaches a soba noodle vendor, where he sits to catch his breath and calm his nerves. He begins telling the vendor about his horrific encounter. Midway through his story, the vendor turns around, strokes his own completely blank face, and asks: 'Was the lady's face anything like this?' The merchant flees again, with no further resolution to the story. The merchant's sense of reality is fundamentally shattered — the ordinary world has been revealed as potentially illusory. Hearn uses the story to illustrate the Japanese concept that reality is not always what it appears.

The 7-Shaku Noppera-bō of Oike-chōSorori monogatari (1663)

1663 (Kanbun 3)

In the Oike-chō district of Kyoto (in what is now Muromachi-dōri Oike-sagaru, Nakagyō-ku), a noppera-bō appeared that stood approximately 7 shaku — about 2.1 meters — tall. This is one of the earliest direct references to a noppera-bō by name or recognizable description. The account notes that nothing was written about what the creature's true identity was — it was simply a faceless giant that appeared and was recorded. The creature's appearance was recorded in the kaidan collection, but no further consequences are documented. The lack of a true-identity explanation distinguishes this account from those that attribute the phenomenon to mujina or tanuki trickery.

Accounts II
The Nupperibō at NijōgawaraShinsetsu Hyakumonogatari (1767)

1767 (Meiwa 4)

A creature called a nupperibō made a frightening appearance at Nijōgawara in Kyoto, near the Nijō-ōhashi bridge. Victims who encountered the creature came away with thick animal hairs attached to their clothing. Based on this evidence, local observers concluded that the apparition was not a ghost or independent spirit but the trick of a furry beast — likely a mujina (badger), tanuki (raccoon dog), or similar shapeshifting animal. This account is important because it explicitly attributes the nupperibō/noppera-bō phenomenon to shapeshifting animals rather than an independent yōkai species. The creature was rationalized as an animal trick, providing an early naturalistic/skeptical interpretation of the faceless-ghost phenomenon.

Toki Akutarō and the Yamabushi at Chōhō-jiKaneyama-ki, autobiography of Mori Nagayoshi (c. 1584)

c. 1584

When the future lord Kukuri Yorioki (then known by his childhood name Akutarō) was a youth, he went into the wilderness to hunt and encountered a monstrous yamabushi (mountain ascetic) standing about 1 jō (10 shaku, approximately 10 feet / 3 meters) tall. The figure had no face. Young Akutarō, displaying the martial courage expected of a samurai, wrestled the creature to the ground — but the ascetic immediately vanished. Akutarō descended from the mountain and came to Chōhō-ji temple, where he began telling the abbot about his supernatural encounter. The abbot suddenly clapped his hands and asked: 'Was that phantasm something like this?' At once, fiends with white, eyeless, noseless faces — 'like white gourds' — began spawning endlessly from the temple walls. Akutarō found himself unable to draw his sword, paralyzed by the supernatural infestation. He resigned himself to death when a wind suddenly blew, the temple disappeared, and he found himself standing alone in an open field. The Jūbakobaba of Hoke-zakaHigo Province (Kumamoto Prefecture) folklore, Shōwa-era anthology (c. 1944)

Recorded c. 1944, pre-modern setting

A slope called Hoke-zaka in Kumamoto was reputedly haunted by a Jūbakobaba ('lunchbox hag') that everyone feared — though no one knew exactly what it looked like. A traveler (seemingly a pilgrim of many temples) came to rest at a teahouse atop the slope and asked the woman running it for warm food. He inquired whether the rumored creature really appeared. 'Yes, it does come out,' she replied, and then — 'this is what a Jūbakobaba is like' — displayed her face without nose, eyes, or mouth. The traveler fled in terror down the slope and reached a second teahouse at the bottom. He confessed to the woman there what he had just seen, at which point THIS woman repeated the exact same line: 'This is what a Jūbakobaba is like' — and displayed her nopperapon face. This story was referenced by some scholars as possibly being Hearn's source material, since Hearn had spent time in the Kumamoto area as a teacher from 1891–1894.
