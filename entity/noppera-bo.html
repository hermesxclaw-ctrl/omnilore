Who It Is

A faceless ghost or yōkai appearing as an ordinary human, only to reveal a blank, featureless face — completely smooth, with no eyes, nose, or mouth. They delight in frightening humans but rarely cause physical harm. Often confused with the mujina.

Form

Indistinguishable from a normal human until they reveal a completely smooth, egg-like face with no features

Powers

Perfect human disguise

Revealing blank face at will

Harmless but terrifying shock

Perfect human disguise The noppera-bō can appear as any ordinary human, indistinguishable from a real person until the moment of facial revelation. It can impersonate specific social roles: a weeping young woman, a soba noodle vendor, a Buddhist abbot, a teahouse proprietress, a fellow mountain traveler, a bob-haired girl. The disguise extends to clothing, posture, speech, and — crucially — the concealment of the blank face until the moment of dramatic reveal. Universal across all accounts (1663–present) The disguise has at least one potential vulnerability: according to some folkloric accounts, the creature may slightly glow or luminesce like a kitsune or mujina in its transformed state, giving away its nature to a 'true hero' or perceptive observer. The Kaneyama-ki account suggests that the creature 'disappears' when physically overpowered, implying that its physical form may be unstable or illusory under duress. Some accounts treat the disguise as an inherent ability of the noppera-bō itself; others argue that the 'disguise' is actually the shapeshifting of a mujina or tanuki, and the faceless form is a secondary state, not a separate creature.

Faceless revelation The creature's primary power is the sudden transformation from an apparently normal human face to a completely blank, featureless expanse of skin. This is not a gradual change but a shocking instantaneous revelation — usually accomplished by slowly lowering a hand or sleeve from the face, building dramatic tension before the reveal. The psychological impact of seeing a human figure with no facial features — no eyes with which to make contact, no mouth with which to speak or scream, no nose with which to breathe — is consistently described as overwhelming and terrifying, causing victims to flee in panic. Defining trait across all accounts No physical harm is reported from this ability. It is purely a tool of psychological terror. The ōchobo variant of Hyōgo is described as a faceless girl who 'turns around' to reveal her blank face — suggesting the face is always absent and merely concealed, rather than transformed. This conflicts with the more common interpretation where the creature actively 'makes its face disappear'.

Recurrent spooking (saido no kai / 再度の怪) Perhaps the most distinctive narrative power: the noppera-bō often appears in pairs or sequences, terrorizing the victim, allowing them to flee to a 'safe' location (a house, a teashop, a temple), and then revealing itself AGAIN in the person the victim turns to for help. This double-scare structure is the hallmark of the most famous noppera-bō stories and has been identified by folklorists as a distinct narrative motif called saido no kai. The effect on the victim is devastating — the safe space is violated, trust in the ordinary world is shattered, and the boundary between reality and nightmare becomes permanently blurred. Prominent in Hearn's 1904 'Mujina', the Kaneyama-ki (1584), the Aomori zunberabō tale (Iwaya 1935), and the Kumamoto jūbakobaba tale (1944) The power appears to be duplicative rather than shapeshifting — it is unclear whether two separate noppera-bō coordinate, or the same creature teleports ahead of the fleeing victim. Scholar Masaru Tōda argues that the 'recurrent spooking' motif was originally used with oni and ogre-type yōkai, not noppera-bō, and may have been transferred to the noppera-bō by Hearn. Regional folktales with this motif collected after Hearn may reflect literary contamination rather than independent oral tradition.

Instantaneous disappearance In the Kaneyama-ki account, when young Toki Akutarō wrestled the faceless yamabushi to the ground, the creature simply 'disappeared' — vanished without a trace. This ability to phase out of physical combat suggests that the noppera-bō's body may be illusory or insubstantial, comparable to other yōkai whose physical forms are not fixed in ordinary matter. Kaneyama-ki (c. 1584) Unknown — only one account describes this ability. No other account describes physical combat with a noppera-bō, so it is unclear whether disappearance is a general ability or specific to this narrative.

Behavior & Habitat

Mischievous trickster; follows travelers and reveals its face for the shock reaction

Accounts

An old merchant travelling along the Akasaka Road to Edo (Tokyo) at night encounters a young woman weeping near Kii-no-Kunizaka hill. She appears to be in distress, possibly contemplating suicide. When the merchant approaches to help her, she turns to face him, dropping the sleeve from her face — revealing a face completely smooth, with 'no eyes or nose or mouth'. Terrified, the merchant flees down the hill and reaches a soba noodle vendor, where he sits to catch his breath and calm his nerves. He begins telling the vendor about his horrific encounter. Midway through his story, the vendor turns around, strokes his own completely blank face, and asks: 'Was the lady's face anything like this?' The merchant flees again, with no further resolution to the story.

In the Oike-chō district of Kyoto (in what is now Muromachi-dōri Oike-sagaru, Nakagyō-ku), a noppera-bō appeared that stood approximately 7 shaku — about 2.1 meters — tall. This is one of the earliest direct references to a noppera-bō by name or recognizable description. The account notes that nothing was written about what the creature's true identity was — it was simply a faceless giant that appeared and was recorded.

A creature called a nupperibō made a frightening appearance at Nijōgawara in Kyoto, near the Nijō-ōhashi bridge. Victims who encountered the creature came away with thick animal hairs attached to their clothing. Based on this evidence, local observers concluded that the apparition was not a ghost or independent spirit but the trick of a furry beast — likely a mujina (badger), tanuki (raccoon dog), or similar shapeshifting animal. This account is important because it explicitly attributes the nupperibō/noppera-bō phenomenon to shapeshifting animals rather than an independent yōkai species.

When the future lord Kukuri Yorioki (then known by his childhood name Akutarō) was a youth, he went into the wilderness to hunt and encountered a monstrous yamabushi (mountain ascetic) standing about 1 jō (10 shaku, approximately 10 feet / 3 meters) tall. The figure had no face. Young Akutarō, displaying the martial courage expected of a samurai, wrestled the creature to the ground — but the ascetic immediately vanished. Akutarō descended from the mountain and came to Chōhō-ji temple, where he began telling the abbot about his supernatural encounter. The abbot suddenly clapped his hands and asked: 'Was that phantasm something like this?' At once, fiends with white, eyeless, noseless faces — 'like white gourds' — began spawning endlessly from the temple walls. Akutarō found himself unable to draw his sword, paralyzed by the supernatural infestation. He resigned himself to death when a wind suddenly blew, the temple disappeared, and he found himself standing alone in an open field. This is the earliest clearly identifiable noppera-bō narrative in Japanese literature, and predates the Edo-period collections by nearly a century.

A slope called Hoke-zaka in Kumamoto was reputedly haunted by a Jūbakobaba ('lunchbox hag') that everyone feared — though no one knew exactly what it looked like. A traveler (seemingly a pilgrim of many temples) came to rest at a teahouse atop the slope and asked the woman running it for warm food. He inquired whether the rumored creature really appeared. 'Yes, it does come out,' she replied, and then — 'this is what a Jūbakobaba is like' — displayed her face without nose, eyes, or mouth. The traveler fled in terror down the slope and reached a second teahouse at the bottom. He confessed to the woman there what he had just seen, at which point THIS woman repeated the exact same line: 'This is what a Jūbakobaba is like' — and displayed her nopperapon face. This story was referenced by some scholars as possibly being Hearn's source material, since Hearn had spent time in the Kumamoto area as a teacher from 1891–1894. However, Masaru Tōda is skeptical about backdating this tale to pre-Hearn times, noting it was collected in 1944 — four decades after Hearn's story.

Content Notes

The noppera-bō's primary activity is inflicting psychological terror on humans. While classical accounts describe it as 'harmless' because it does not physically injure its victims, the deliberate infliction of paralyzing fear — particularly through the 'recurrent spooking' motif that destroys the victim's sense of safety — constitutes a form of non-physical violence. Some victims are described as being traumatized by the experience.

The line between 'harmless prank' and 'traumatic assault' in yōkai folklore reflects broader Japanese cultural attitudes about the acceptable boundaries of supernatural mischief. The noppera-bō's behavior exists at the limit of what 'mischief' can excuse.

Yosa Buson's Buson yōkai emaki (c. 1754–1757) depicts the nupperibōzu with the distinctive characteristic of having a single eyeball in its anus that 'glows like lightning'. Shigeru Mizuki later gave this variant the name shirime (尻目, 'butt-eye') and embellished it into a comic sketch where the creature strips naked and bends over to 'spook a samurai' with its glowing rear eye. This depiction is scatological and arguably obscene by modern standards.

The shirime is unusual even among yōkai for its explicit scatological humor. Mizuki's embellishment treats it as a comedic figure rather than a genuinely horrifying one.

Buson yōkai emaki (c. 1754–1757); Mizuki's yōkai encyclopedia

The noppera-bō's genuinely terrifying folkloric dimension — the existential horror of encountering a human-shaped thing with no face, and the devastating 'recurrent spooking' that destroys trust in reality — is often softened in modern popular culture, where the creature is treated as a cute or mildly spooky mascot character (e.g., in Yo-kai Watch, Mizuki's Kitarō, and children's yōkai encyclopedias). This sanitization risks erasing the creature's folkloric function as a genuine existential horror figure.

Folklorist Michael Foster has noted the general trend of yōkai 'domestication' in 20th-21st century pop culture, where Edo-era horror figures become cute mascots. The noppera-bō is a clear example of this phenomenon.

What We Don't Know

Research was conducted using English Wikipedia as the primary source, supplemented by Wikidata. The following gaps are acknowledged: (1) Original Japanese texts (Sorori monogatari, Shinsetsu Hyakumonogatari, Kaneyama-ki, Buson yōkai emaki) were not consulted in their original language — analysis relies on scholarly secondary sources and Wikipedia summaries. A Japanese-language specialist would be needed for primary source verification. (2) Modern Japanese-language scholarship on the saido no kai debate (Tōda's full arguments vs. Yanagita school counterarguments) could not be fully accessed through English Wikipedia. (3) Game appearances in Nioh 2, Persona, and Yo-kai Watch were identified via search but specific appearance details (attack patterns, character descriptions, game stats) could not be verified from Wikipedia summaries. (4) The Hawaii Waialae Mujina reports of 1959 and 1981 appear as external links on Wikipedia but their content could not be accessed. (5) The relationship between noppera-bō and Chinese faceless-spirit traditions (Ji Yun's Notes, Yetan suilu) merits deeper cross-cultural analysis that was not possible within a single research cycle. (6) Meoni (woman ogre) from Genji monogatari Book 53 is mentioned as a possible literary precursor but could not be independently verified. (7) The pedagogical use of the 'faceless ghost' figure in Japanese education (as an 'scary but harmless' introduction to yōkai for children) was not researched.

A way to hold it in your mind

Imagine walking down a dark road, coming upon a young woman weeping — her shoulders shaking, her face hidden behind a sleeve. You approach to help. She lowers her arm. Where her face should be, there is nothing — not eyes, not a nose, not a mouth, not even a scar — just a smooth, pale expanse of skin, like an egg, like a whitewashed wall, like the face of a doll that was never painted. And when you turn and run, when you finally find a friendly face behind a counter and begin gasping out what you saw, that face too becomes blank — and a voice asks, 'Was it anything like this?'
