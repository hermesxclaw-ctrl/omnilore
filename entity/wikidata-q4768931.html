Form

No surviving artistic depictions of Annea Clivana exist. As a minor 'indigitamenta' deity (a functional spirit of Roman religion), she likely had no anthropomorphic cult image. She was invoked in prayer and ritual, not depicted in temples. In modern imaginative reconstruction, she might be envisioned as a modest female figure in a simple stola, standing on a rocky slope, or as an abstract presence felt when climbing.

Powers

Safe Passage on Slopes Clivana ensures safe travel on hillsides and slopes, protecting travelers, animals, and goods from falls, slips, and accidents on inclines. Roman (all sources) Her domain is specifically slopes, not flat roads (which belong to other deities) or vertical cliffs (which are not 'clivi'). No contradictions recorded.

Blessing of Ascents She grants the physical and spiritual strength to ascend — a metaphor extended to life's 'uphill battles' in later interpretation, though this is modern extrapolation. Modern interpretation Not explicitly attested in ancient sources; this is a poetic extension of her domain. None.

Behavior & Habitat

As a functional deity, Clivana's 'behavior' is her domain: she presides over the act of ascending slopes and hillsides. She was invoked by Romans who needed to travel uphill — carrying goods, driving animals, or simply journeying. Her 'personality' in the sources is minimal; she is a numinous presence tied to a specific geographic feature.

Accounts

Clivana belongs to the 'indigitamenta' — the catalog of minor Roman deities who presided over specific, often mundane, functions. These gods were invoked in ritual formulas (the 'indigitamenta' lists) to ensure divine favor for every aspect of daily life. Clivana's function was so narrow — the slope, the hill — that she represents the extreme granularity of Roman polytheism, where even a hill had its own divine presence. Christian apologists like Augustine mocked this proliferation of gods, asking why the Romans needed a goddess for every hill and path.

The most famous Roman 'clivus' was the Clivus Capitolinus, the steep road leading to the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitoline Hill. Triumphal processions climbed this slope, and everyday Romans used it daily. While no specific ritual to Clivana on this road is attested, her domain would have encompassed it. The slope was both a literal ascent and a symbolic one — the climb to the greatest temple in Rome.

What We Don't Know

No surviving artistic depictions, no dedicated temples or cult sites, no festivals, no narrative myths. The exact nature of her worship (if any) is unknown. The meaning of 'Annea' is unresolved. No modern scholarly monograph exists; she is mentioned only in passing in studies of Roman religion. Roman religious life was organized around a dense local pantheon, and this deity's cult was tied to specific geographic and civic settings across the Italian peninsula. Inscriptions and temple dedications provide the primary evidence for such cults, and modern scholarship on Roman religion emphasizes the interplay between household, civic, and indigenous Italic worship traditions.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Annea Clivana as the patron saint of stairmasters and hill forts. She's the divine equivalent of a 'Caution: Steep Grade' sign — a quiet, functional presence that only becomes noticeable when you're out of breath. In a world full of gods who demanded temples and sacrifices, she was content with a passing acknowledgment from a tired traveler. She's the goddess for anyone who has ever looked at a hill and thought, 'This is going to be a workout.'
