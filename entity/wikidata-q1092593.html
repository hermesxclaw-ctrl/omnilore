Who It Is

In the pale limestone of the Dolomites, where underground rivers cut caves beneath alpine pastures, the Ladin people say you can still hear her — the Longana combing her impossibly long hair by a spring at dusk, half woman, half water, with feet that should not be seen. She will teach you to spin, to heal, to find the lost cow — and if you betray her taboo, she will take your child or pull you under.

Ambivalent, not simply good/evil. Attested behaviors: (1) Helpful teacher — teaches humans spinning, weaving, cheese-making, healing herbs, finding lost livestock, predicting weather — but demands secrecy, cleanliness, or observance of taboo (not looking at feet, not stealing thread, not entering her cave uninvited). (2) Guardian of waters — punishes pollution, damming, or boasting near springs with drought, flood or illness. Name & Tongue

Etymology — Generally derived from Latin aqua 'water' > *aquana 'water woman' > anguana/longana, with phonological palatalization and prosthetic l- in Ladin (l'Anguana > Longana). Proposed by Carlo Battisti and elaborated by Ulrike Kindl and Daniela Perco. Alternative folk-etymologies linking to Latin anguis 's

Pronunciation — Ladin: /lonˈɡaːna/ (lon-GAH-na), plural /lonˈɡaːne/; Italian Anguana /anˈɡwaːna/. Stress on penultimate syllable. In Gardenese Ladin sometimes [loŋˈɡana].

Aliases — Anguana, Aguana, Agana, Anguana d'acqua, Aivana, Vivana

Ownership — Living tradition of the Ladin people (Ladins) of the Dolomites — Val Gardena (Gherdëina), Val Badia, Val di Fassa, Livinallongo, Ampezzo, Cadore — with contiguous variants among ne

Powers
Hydrokinesis and water guardianship

Controls flow, purity and availability of springs, lakes and torrents; can cause drought, flood, or make water disappear if offended; can purify polluted water or reveal hidden springs.

Limit: Bound to her home water — cannot act far from it; power fails if water is blessed/consecrated in Christianized variants; requires ritual cleanliness and silence from humans

Supernatural weaving, spinning and pastoral craft

Spins thread that never breaks, weaves linen of extraordinary whiteness/fineness, churns butter that never spoils, makes cheese of exceptional quality; teaches these arts to favored humans.

Limit: Gift is conditional on taboo — looking at feet, stealing, or revealing secret breaks it; thread snaps or butter spoils if taboo violated

Shapeshifting and illusion

Can appear as beautiful woman, old washerwoman, frog, snake, or mist; can make cave appear as palace, or make herself invisible except for combing sound.

Limit: Feet remain animal despite transformation — the telltale that betrays her; cannot hide feet from those who know to look

Prophecy, fate knowledge and healing

Knows future births, deaths, weather; acts as midwife and herbalist; foretells avalanches or storms; knows location of lost persons/livestock.

Limit: Prophecy often ambiguous or conditional; healing requires payment (food, silence) and fails if Christian prayer is invoked to bind her

Enchantment, seduction and abduction

Enchants young men with song/beauty, lures them to water/cave; abducts children to raise or to serve; can cause lovesickness, drowning, or disappearance.

Limit: Cannot keep victim if rescue party maintains silence or uses iron/bell ringing; cannot enter consecrated ground; power wanes at noon church bells in Christianized versions

Vulnerabilities and countermeasures

Revealed by animal feet; repelled or bound by iron, church bells, holy water, making the sign of the cross, or by breaking taboo of hospitality; loses power if her comb is stolen.

Limit: Countermeasures are human-side folklore, not her 'powers'; efficacy varies by tale and reflects Christian folk religion overlay

Form & Signs

Appearance — Attested core: a female figure of striking beauty, young to middle-aged, with very long hair (often black or golden) that she combs obsessively by water. Clothing varies: white or green dress, sometimes nude or in animal skins in older tellings. The diagnostic, taboo feature is her feet/legs, which she hides: most consistently described as goat feet (caprine hooves), chicken/bird feet (hen's feet, zampe di gallina), or occasionally webbed/fish-li

Symbols — Water, weaving and spinning (distaff, spindle, loom), washing, comb/mirror, cave and spring, white linen, cheese and butter (pastoral knowledge), moon and liminal times. Iconography in folk art is sparse — no medieval church sculpture securely identified as Longana; modern depictions are 20th-century illustrations (e.g., Wolff illustrators, Museum Ladin educational panels, tourist signage). She is not winged and not horned; goat feet are not devi

Domains — Strictly liminal wet/wild ecotone: karst springs (sorc, fontana), waterfalls, alpine lakes (e.g., Lago di Carezza/Karersee, Lago d'Antermoia), cave systems (Bus de la Longana toponyms), torrents and mills, forest clearings near water, and high pastures where she appears at the malga (alpine dairy hut). She does not dwell in villages or churches; she enters human space only at night or when invited as midwife. Cosmology: not a goddess in a pa

Accounts I
The Longana of the Malga and the Shepherd Who Looked at Her FeetOral tradition Val Badia, recorded by Alton (1881) and retold by Wolff (1913) 'Die Anguane und der Senner'

Oral tradition 19th century recorded; story world undated pastoral past

A Longana comes nightly to a lonely alpine dairy hut (malga) to help a young shepherd churn butter and spin wool. She is beautiful, kind, and her work is flawless, but she keeps her long skirt covering her feet and forbids him to look. She warns: never speak of her, never try to see. For weeks prosperity follows — butter never fails. One night curiosity overcomes him; he scatters ashes/flour to see footprints or lifts her skirt. He sees goat hooves (in some versions chicken feet). She screams, declares the pact broken, and vanishes. The churn sours, the thread tangles, and the spring near the hut dries up. In some variants she curses him to never marry or to wander. Etiology for why a particular spring is dry or a pasture abandoned; moral about respect for taboo, curiosity, and the wild's conditional generosity. Establishes the foot-taboo as central diagnostic.

The Anguana Midwife of AntermoiaLadin oral cycle, summarized in Kindl (1983) and Museum Ladin archive; related to widespread Alpine 'fairy midwife' type

Oral 19th-20th century; narrative set in timeless village past

A human midwife is summoned at night by a strange woman to a cave by a lake (often Lago d'Antermoia). Inside the cave is a palace. The Anguana is in labour and needs human help because fairy women cannot give birth alone (or have lost knowledge). The human helps deliver the child. As reward she is given a handful of wood shavings or coal that turns to gold by morning — but only if she keeps silent. If she brags, the gold reverts to shavings and the child sickens. In darker variants the Anguana steals a human child to nurse her own. Explains taboo on boasting about otherworld gifts; reinforces reciprocity and silence; parallels pan-European fairy-midwife tales but with specifically Alpine water-cave setting.

The Kingdom of Fanes and the Anguanas' Prophecy (Fanes Epic)Karl Felix Wolff's constructed epic 'Das Reich der Fanes' assembled from fragments (1920s-1941), critically edited by Kindl & Bravi; not a single ancient text but Wolff's synthesis of Ladin fragments

Wolff's publication 1932-1941; claimed to reflect older mythic past but scholarly consensus: largely Wolff's romantic reconstruction

The Anguanas dwell by Lake Braies and in the realm of Queen Aurona. They are fate-women who give Princess Dolasilla a white armor that makes her invincible as long as she remains unmarried and does not remove it, and they foretell the rise and fall of the Fanes kingdom. When Dolasilla breaks the taboo (marries and removes armor for love), the Anguanas withdraw their protection, the Fanes are destroyed, and the survivors are turned to stone — the pale Dolomite peaks. The Anguanas themselves retreat underground, their lakes become inaccessible. Provides etiology for Dolomite landscape (pallid peaks as petrified Fanes) and central Ladin national myth; heavily touristic today. Scholarly debate: how much is authentic folklore vs Wolff invention — Kindl argues Wolff systematized disparate motifs into a false epic unity.

Accounts II
The Anguana of Lake Carezza (Karersee) and the RainbowSouth Tyrol oral legend, recorded by Wolff and in South Tyrol tourist literature; related to Ladin toponymic legends

Recorded late 19th century; story world mythic past

An Anguana lives in the crystal waters of Lake Carezza, famous for its rainbow colours. A sorcerer (or the wizard Masaré) wants to capture her and creates a rainbow to lure her out. She is enchanted by the colours and rises, but when she realizes the trick she flees and curses the lake to never again show its full rainbow to those who seek to exploit it — or she disappears forever, and the lake's colours fade when viewed greedily. Another telling: she falls in love with a human, is betrayed, and colours the lake with her tears. Etiology for lake's famous iridescence and for taboo against greed/exploitation of natural beauty; heavily commercialized in modern tourism, with sanitized mermaid statues.

The Washerwomen at Noon — The Anguane LavandaieVeneto oral tradition, documented by Daniela Perco and in Italo Calvino Fiabe italiane (1956) tale types; field recordings in Vicenza/Verona pre-Alps

Oral 19th-20th century; Calvino literary retelling 1956

At the hottest hour of noon, when no one should be near water, the Anguane appear at a ford or washing stone beating laundry with wooden battitoi. A passing farmer or child sees white sheets hung to dry that never get dirty. If greeted respectfully and left alone, they may leave a gift of bread or clean linen. If mocked, spied upon, or if someone steals a sheet, they drag the offender into the water, drown him, or beat him with the washing bat. In some versions they are souls of women who died in childbirth, condemned to wash eternally. Taboo on working at noon near water and on stealing; explains drownings at fords; links Anguana to pan-European 'midnight washerwoman' (lavandière) complex but with specifically Alpine noon timing.

The Stolen Child and the Wooden Log (Changeling Variant)Field collection in Cadore/Belluno, summarized in Perco (2014) and Kindl analyses; not universal for Longana but attested in Veneto-Ladin border

Oral 19th-20th century

An Anguana, unable to keep her own child alive (or desiring a human child to raise as servant), steals a beautiful human infant from its cradle while the mother is at the spring, leaving a wooden log (or a sickly, crying substitute) that looks like the child but withers. The parents notice the substitute eats enormously, does not grow, and has old features. A wise old woman advises to place the log on the hearth or threaten it with iron, causing the Anguana to return and exchange the children, complaining that humans are cruel. In other tellings the child is never returned and is heard singing by the water years later. Explains infant illness/death and parental guilt; reinforces apotropaic use of iron and fire; darker variant where child is lost permanently warns against leaving infants near water unattended.

Longana is just the Italian word for mermaid or a generic fairy. — Longana/Anguana is a specific Alpine water-woman with diagnostic animal feet, cave/spring habitat, and conditional teaching/punishing behavior. She is not fish-tailed in core Ladin tradition (that's a lowland/tourist overlay) and is not a winged fairy. Conflating her with Disneyfied mermaids erases her pastoral, ecological and taboo dimensions.

The Kingdom of Fanes is an ancient Ladin epic like the Nibelungenlied, and the Anguanas' prophecy is ancient scripture. — The unified Fanes epic is Karl Felix Wolff's 20th-century literary synthesis of disparate fragments. While individual motifs (Dolasilla, marmots, white armor) have oral roots, the coherent narrative with Anguanas as kingdom-level fate-givers is Wolff's construction, as demonstrated by Ulrike Kindl's critical work. Treating Wolff as verbatim ancient text is a major misconception.

Longana is the same as the Vanuatu village Longana, or the fruit longan, or a demon in global demonology lists. — Three distinct homonyms: (1) Ladin folklore figure, (2) village on Ambae Island, Vanuatu (Q1092593), (3) Dimocarpus longan fruit (often misspelled longana). No etymological or cultural connection. Database string-matching and AI summaries frequently conflate them.

Seeing a Longana always means good luck / she grants wishes. — In authentic tales luck is conditional and often dangerous. Gifts (gold, endless butter, fine thread) revert or become curses if taboo is broken (looking at feet, boasting, stealing). Many encounters end with loss, drowning or child abduction. Wish-granting is a modern softening.
