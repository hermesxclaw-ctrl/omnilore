The Approach

In the green world of Irish myth, where gods walk with mortal wounds, there was a healer whose skill was so perfect it became his death warrant. Miach, son of the great physician Dian Cecht, could mend flesh and bone with a word, but his greatest cure—a living arm for a wounded king—cost him his life. His grave bloomed with 365 herbs, a pharmacy born from murder, and his story still whispers questions about jealousy, healing, and the price of being better than your teacher.

Content advisory. None
Identity

Miach

Pronunciation Irish pronunciation: [ˈmʲiəx]

Etymology

The name Miach is often translated as 'bushel', but it has a meaning of an agreed upon amount of grain; Irish law texts also use miach in reference to an amount of malted grain, and in Munster there is reference to a fleith in méich or 'Feast of Miach'.

Cultural Ownership

Irish mythology, specifically the Tuatha Dé Danann. He is a figure of the mythical past, not part of living tradition.

Disputed Identifications

Some scholars hypothesize that Miach and his brother Oirmiach are vestiges of the Celtic Divine Twins, but this is a modern interpretation, not attested in medieval sources.

Description

Appearance

Miach is described as 'handsome, young, and of good stature' in the source material, when he and his brother arrive at Nuadu's court.

Symbols & Iconography

No specific symbols are associated with Miach in the available sources. As a physician, he may be associated with healing herbs, but this is not explicitly stated.

Behaviour & Personality

Miach is depicted as a skilled and confident healer, able to heal wounds that even his father cannot. He is also shown as dutiful, continuing to heal the Tuatha Dé alongside his father and sister after his own resurrection (if that is what the text implies).

Domains & Habitats

He belongs to the Tuatha Dé Danann, a supernatural race in Irish mythology. He resides in their realm, likely in the otherworld or in Ireland.

Version Differences

In the main account (from the source material, likely from the Cath Maige Tuired or similar), Dian Cecht kills Miach out of jealousy. In The Fate of the Children of Tuireann, Miach appears alive and well, with no mention of his death. This suggests either a different version of the story or that the death was not permanent.

Powers & Abilities

Healing

Source Version Main account (likely Cath Maige Tuired)

Description

Miach is a physician of the Tuatha Dé Danann. He can heal wounds, replace limbs, and even cure injuries that would be fatal. He heals the wounds inflicted by his father's sword, showing his ability to regenerate his own body.

Limitations

He can be killed if his brain is cut in half, as demonstrated by his father's fourth blow.

Contradictions

In The Fate of the Children of Tuireann, he is shown healing others, but no mention is made of his death.

Herbal knowledge

Source Version Main account

Description

After his death, 365 herbs grow from his grave, which his sister Airmid arranges according to their properties. This implies that Miach had knowledge of herbs, and his body even after death could produce healing plants.

Limitations

None specified.

Contradictions

None known.

Stories & Myths

The Death of Miach

Source Irish mythology, likely Cath Maige Tuired or similar textCulture IrishPeriod Mythological, medieval manuscripts

Summary

Miach replaces the silver arm that his father Dian Cecht made for Nuada with an arm of flesh and blood. Dian Cecht, jealous that his son could do what he could not, kills Miach. He strikes Miach's head four times with his sword. The first strike cuts the skin, which Miach heals. The second breaks his skull, which he also heals. The third grazes his brain, but he heals that too. The fourth cut splits his brain in half, killing him. From his grave grow 365 herbs, which his sister Airmid arranges, but Dian Cecht scatters them.

Consequences

Miach dies, but 365 herbs grow from his grave, which are scattered by Dian Cecht, losing their specific healing properties. Miach later appears in other stories, seemingly alive.

Variants

In some accounts, both Miach and Oirmiach are killed by Dian Cecht. The number of herbs and the details of the healing may vary.

The Healing of Nuada's Doorkeeper and Nuada's Arm

Source The Fate of the Children of TuireannCulture IrishPeriod Mythological, medieval manuscripts

Summary

Miach and his brother Oirmiach replace the missing eye of Nuada's doorkeeper with the eye of a cat. Then they draw a devil out of Nuada's blackened arm and kill it. Finally, they find a replacement arm for Nuada, and Oirmiach sets the arm while Miach retrieves herbs to complete the healing.

Patricide

Source Main account

Description

Dian Cecht kills his own son Miach out of jealousy. The killing is described in detail: four sword blows to the head, each more severe, with Miach healing each until the final blow splits his brain.

Scholarly Context

This story may reflect the theme of the older generation's jealousy of the younger, or the dangers of surpassing one's teacher. It is a common motif in mythology.

Miach is killed by Dian Cecht and does not return in the same story.

There is no consensus; it is likely that different stories have different versions, or that the death was not final in the mythological cycle.
