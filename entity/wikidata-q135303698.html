Who It Is

Imagine a lake so vast it takes a day to walk around, its waters so clear you can see the golden sand at the bottom, and so cool they can quench the fires of the mind itself. This is Anavatapta, the 'Not-Hot' lake, the mythical heart of the Buddhist cosmos, the source of the world's greatest rivers, and a place where gods, serpents, and Buddhas come to rest. It's the original 'sacred watering hole' of the spirit, a place so pure that even the concept of suffering seems to melt away in its presence.

As a location, Anavatapta is not a sentient being, but it is described as having a profound spiritual presence. It is a place of stillness and purity, where the water is always calm and cool, never too hot or too cold. It is a place where celestial beings, nagas, and great sages gather.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name is Sanskrit, meaning 'that which is not hot' or 'free from heat'. This refers to the lake's mythical property of being cool and temperate, unlike other great lakes. In Pali, the name appears as 'Anotatta'.

Pronunciation — uh-nuh-VAHP-tuh (Sanskrit: अनवतप्त)

Aliases — Anavatapta, Anotatta, Anawatapta

Source of Four Great Rivers

Anavatapta is the origin of four of the world's most important rivers, which flow through four distinct animal-shaped outlets. This makes it a hydrological nexus of the known world in Buddhist cosmology.

Limit: The specific identification of the rivers and their outlets varies between sources.

Coolness and Purity

The lake's defining quality is its coolness, which is not merely a physical property but a spiritual one. It is said to be free from the 'heat' of the passions and to have the power to cool the minds of those who contemplate it.

Limit: This is a spiritual quality, not a physical power that can be weaponized.

Residence of Sages and Celestial Beings

Anavatapta is a favored dwelling place for powerful sages (rishis), nagas (serpent beings), and devas (gods). The Buddha himself is said to have visited and taught there, and the region is associated with great spiritual power.

Limit: Access is generally restricted to beings of high spiritual attainment.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anavatapta is described as a vast, perfectly round lake, its waters crystal clear and cool, with a golden sandy bottom. It is surrounded by seven golden mountains, and its shores are adorned with jewels and precious trees. The lake is said to be 50 yojanas (approximately 400-500 miles) in circumference, a body of water so immense it defies earthly comparison.

Symbols — The lake symbolizes purity, coolness, and spiritual clarity. Its 'not hot' nature represents freedom from the 'heat' of passion, defilement, and suffering. The four rivers represent the dissemination of the Dharma (Buddhist teachings) to the four corners of the world. The lake itself is a symbol of the untroubled, clear mind of an enlightened being.

Domains — Anavatapta is the sovereign of all lakes and rivers. It is the source of four great rivers of the world: the Ganges, the Indus, the Oxus (Vakshu), and the Sita (often identified with the Tarim or a mythical river of the east). These rivers are said to flow from the lake through the mouths of animal-shaped rocks: an elephant, a bull, a horse, and a lion respectively.

Accounts I
The Buddha's Visit to AnavataptaVarious Sutras, including the Mahavastu and the Fo-su-hing-tsan-king (Buddhacarita)

The story of the Buddha's life (c. 5th century BCE) as recorded in later texts

The Buddha, during his travels, is said to have visited the shore of Anavatapta. He and his disciples would often go there to meditate and teach. On one such visit, the Buddha is said to have displayed his superhuman powers, such as walking through the air or causing the lake's waters to part, to convince a group of skeptical ascetics or nagas of his enlightenment. The visit solidified the lake's status as a sacred site and reinforced the Buddha's authority as a teacher of both humans and non-humans.

Accounts II
The Origin of the Four RiversAbhidharma-kosha-bhasya, Xuanzang's Records

As recorded from the 4th century CE onwards

According to the Abhidharma, the lake is the source of the Ganges, Indus, Oxus, and Sita rivers. The water flows out from the lake through the mouths of four animal-shaped rocks: an elephant (Ganges), a bull (Indus), a horse (Oxus), and a lion (Sita). These rivers then circle the lake seven times before flowing out to the four corners of the world, bringing life and prosperity. This story establishes Anavatapta as the hydrological and spiritual center of the known world, a microcosm of cosmic order.

The Nagas' AbodeJataka Tales, various Mahayana Sutras

c. 3rd century BCE onwards

Anavatapta is described as a favored residence of the nagas, the serpent-deities of Indian mythology. The lake is often depicted as their palace, a place of immense wealth and power, but also of wisdom. The nagas are said to guard the lake and its teachings, and they are often depicted as attending the Buddha's sermons there. The association with nagas links Anavatapta to the broader mythology of serpent-worship and to the concept of hidden wisdom that can be revealed to worthy beings.

Anavatapta is just another name for Lake Manasarovar. — While often identified with Manasarovar, Anavatapta is first and foremost a mythical lake in Buddhist cosmology. The identification with the physical lake is a later development, and many texts treat it as a purely celestial or otherworldly location.

The lake is 'hot' because it is in the Himalayas. — The name means 'not hot', and this is a spiritual quality, not a meteorological one. It represents freedom from the 'heat' of passion and suffering, not a literal temperature reading.

The four rivers are the only rivers in the world. — They are the four 'great' rivers that define the known world in ancient Buddhist geography. The myth is a way of explaining the world's hydrology, not a literal claim about all rivers.
