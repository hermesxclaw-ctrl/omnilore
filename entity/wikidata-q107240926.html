The Approach

In the shadowed mountains of Arcadia, a king and his fifty sons dared to test the king of the gods—and paid the ultimate price. Among them was Eumetes, a name whispered only in the lists of the damned.

Content advisory. This dossier contains references to cannibalism, child sacrifice, and divine retribution.
Identity

Eumetes

Pronunciation yoo-MEE-teez (English), eu-mē-tēs (Classical Greek)

Etymology

The name Eumetes (Εὐμήτης) is derived from Greek εὖ (eu, 'well') and μῆτις (mētis, 'counsel, wisdom'), thus meaning 'of good counsel' or 'wise in counsel'.

Cultural Ownership

Greek mythology, specifically the Arcadian tradition

Disputed Identifications

None recorded; Eumetes is only mentioned in lists of Lycaon's sons.

Description

Appearance

No specific appearance is described in ancient sources. As a son of Lycaon, he would be a mortal prince of Arcadia.

Symbols & Iconography

No symbols or iconography are associated with Eumetes.

Behaviour & Personality

Eumetes is described as one of the most nefarious and carefree of all people, along with his brothers. They committed an act of extreme impiety by serving the entrails of a child to Zeus.

Domains & Habitats

Arcadia, a region in the central Peloponnese of Greece.

Version Differences

The list of Lycaon's sons varies among sources; Eumetes appears only in Apollodorus. Other sources give different names and numbers.

Stories & Myths

The Testing of Lycaon and His Sons

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1; Pausanias, Description of Greece 8.2.4-6Culture GreekPeriod Mythic, set in the age before the flood of Deucalion

Summary

Lycaon, king of Arcadia, and his fifty sons were the most impious mortals. Zeus, to test them, visited them in the guise of a peasant. The brothers mixed the entrails of a child into the god's meal. Enraged, Zeus threw the meal over the table and killed Lycaon and all his sons with a lightning bolt. Eumetes was among those slain.

Participants

Zeus

Lycaon

Eumetes

the other sons of Lycaon

Consequences

The death of Lycaon and his sons; the flood of Deucalion was sent by Zeus to punish the impious race of humanity.

Variants

Pausanias recounts a similar story but does not name all the sons. Some versions say only Lycaon was punished, while others say the sons were spared or transformed.

Dark History

Cannibalism

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Eumetes and his brothers mixed the entrails of a child into Zeus's meal, an act of extreme impiety involving human sacrifice and cannibalism.

Scholarly Context

This act is often interpreted as a myth explaining the origin of human sacrifice or as a test of Zeus's omniscience.

Divine punishment

Source Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.8.1

Description

Zeus killed Eumetes and his brothers with a lightning bolt, a common mythological punishment for impiety.

Scholarly Context

The myth reflects the Greek concept of hubris and the consequences of challenging the gods.
