Who It Is

In the grand, glittering court of the High Plain of Heaven, when the sun goddess Amaterasu decided to send her grandson to rule the world below, she didn't just give him a blessing — she gave him a staff. A divine entourage of five kami, each a specialist in the sacred arts of governance, ritual, and dance. Among them, silent and unnamed, stood Amenomitori, the Heavenly Bird-Catcher. He is a footnote in the greatest story of Japanese statehood, a name on a list, a presence without a voice. And yet, for over a thousand years, shrines have honored him. Who was this quiet kami, this divine attendant who caught birds for reasons we can only guess? The answer, frustratingly and fascinatingly, is that we simply don't know. He is a ghost in the mythological machine, a placeholder for a story that was never written down.

Aside from being a member of the divine entourage that accompanied Ninigi, no specific behaviors, actions, or words are attributed to Amenomitori in any primary text. He is a purely functional figure: a companion of the Heavenly Grandchild.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Amenomitori (天鳥取 or 天禽取) is composed of 天 (ama, 'heaven'), 鳥/禽 (tori, 'bird'), and 取 (tori, 'to catch or take'). The name is thus typically rendered as 'Heavenly Bird-Catcher' or 'Bird-Catcher of Heaven,' though the exact referent of 'bird' in this context — a literal bird, a celestial omen

Pronunciation — ah-meh-noh-mee-toh-ree

Aliases — Ame no Mitori, Amatsu mitori, Heavenly Bird-Catcher

Ownership — Japanese (Shinto)

Divine Retinue Membership

Amenomitori's primary 'power' is his (or her) status as one of the chosen kami who descended from heaven to establish the imperial lineage. This grants him a permanent place in the mythological foundation of the Japanese state.

Limit: No specific powers are described. The role is purely honorific and positional.

Bird-Catching (Inferred)

The name 'Heavenly Bird-Catcher' implies a specific skill or domain over catching birds. This could be a literal hunting skill, a ritual role (catching birds for divination), or a metaphorical function (capturing a wandering spirit). No text confirms any of these.

Limit: Entirely speculative. The name is the only evidence.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No primary source describes Amenomitori's physical appearance. No iconographic tradition exists in classical or medieval art. The name's 'bird-catcher' etymology suggests a figure associated with avian life, but this is an inference from the name, not a description. In modern shrine contexts, he is typically represented generically as a male kami in courtly attire, but this is a modern convention, not an attested tradition.

Symbols — The only symbol associated with Amenomitori is the bird itself, derived from his name. No specific bird species is identified. In the broader context of the descent myth, the kami's presence in the entourage symbolizes the full panoply of divine support for the imperial line.

Domains — Given the lack of narrative, Amenomitori's domain is best inferred from his name and context. He is a kami of the High Plain of Heaven (Takamagahara), associated with the divine mandate to rule the Central Land. The 'bird-catcher' title suggests a potential domain over fowling, hunting, or the interpretation of bird omens, but this is speculative.

Accounts I
The Descent of the Heavenly Grandchild (Tenson Kōrin)Kojiki, Nihon Shoki, Kogo Shūi

Mythological (recorded 712-807 CE)

The sun goddess Amaterasu decides to send her grandson, Ninigi, to rule the Central Land of the Reed Plains. She assembles a divine entourage to accompany him, including Amenomitori. The group descends from Takamagahara to the peak of Takachiho in Kyushu. Amenomitori is present but is not given any lines or specific actions. The story establishes the divine mandate of the Japanese imperial family. The descent legitimizes the imperial line's rule over Japan. Amenomitori's presence, as part of the entourage, ties him to this foundational event, ensuring his place in the national pantheon.
