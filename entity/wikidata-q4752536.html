Who It Is

Imagine an entire goddess — her name, her altar, her single act of devotion — surviving for eighteen centuries, while everything else about her is dust. Ancasta is the ghost of that goddess: a name chiseled into stone, a vow made by a hand we'll never identify, a prayer we'll never hear. She is the smallest possible footprint of belief — a single stone in a Hampshire field — and yet she is proof that the Roman world was full of local, intimate, unnamed divinities who watched over springs, farms, and families. She is the patron saint of the forgotten faithful.

Unknown — no mythology, narrative, or cult practice is recorded. She received votive offerings, indicating she was believed to act in the world (likely healing, protection, or local providence), but her specific functions are absent from the record.

Votive acceptance

The only attested 'ability': she received a votive offering (likely a small statue, a coin, or a personal object) from a worshipper. This implies she was believed to have the power to grant favors, provide protection, or answer prayers.

Limit: The inscription is fragmentary — the dedicant's name and the reason for the offering are lost.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No image or description of Ancasta survives. The altar is plain — there is no carved figure, no attribute, no iconographic clue. We do not know if she was depicted in human form, animal form, or not at all. This is a genuinely unknown quantity.

Symbols — None recorded. No iconography, no associated animals, no sacred objects.

Domains — Inferred only: possibly a local, tutelary goddess of a spring, river, or topographical feature. The altar was found near the River Itchen, and some scholars have suggested a water connection, but this is speculation. She may have been a household or personal goddess of a single family or estate.

Accounts I
The Bitterne AltarRIB 97

2nd–3rd century CE

A single individual — whose name is lost to history — set up a small stone altar to the goddess Ancasta. The act was a standard Roman religious practice: a vow made, a favor sought, and a dedication given. That is the entire 'story' — a single, silent gesture of devotion that has survived for nearly two thousand years. The altar survived, was discovered in the 19th century, and is now the only proof that Ancasta ever existed.
