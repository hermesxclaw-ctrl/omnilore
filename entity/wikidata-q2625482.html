Who It Is

Imagine a father so terrified of drowning that he chops off his own daughter's fingers, one by one, as she clings to the side of his boat. Her severed fingers don't sink — they transform into whales, seals, and walruses, swimming away into the Arctic sea. This is the story of Anguta, the Inuit figure who may be a creator, a father, a ferryman of the dead, or all of these at once — depending on who you ask. He is a being of contradictions: a god who creates and destroys, a father who betrays, a guide who ferries souls across the final river. And he is a figure whose story was reshaped by missionaries, ethnographers, and the tides of history, leaving us with a puzzle: who was Anguta before the outsiders came?

Anguta's behavior varies radically by source: 1) In Boas's version, he is a stern father who chops off his daughter's fingers and casts her into the sea, then later becomes the ruler of the underworld, presiding over the souls of the dead. 2) In some Greenlandic versions, he is a benign creator who made the land and the sea. 3) In others, he is a psychopomp who ferries souls to the land of the dead. 4) In a few accounts, he is a trickster-adjacent figure who is outwitted by his daughter. Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Anguta is derived from the Inuktitut root 'angut-' meaning 'man' or 'male'. It carries the connotation of 'father' or 'the one who is male/paternal'. In some analyses, it is linked to the concept of 'the one who cuts' or 'the one who separates', though this etymology is less certain.

Pronunciation — AHN-goo-tah (IPA: /ˈaŋ.ɡu.ta/)

Aliases — Angut, Angutaq, Aguta, Angutaaq

Powers
Creation of the earth and sea

In some Greenlandic accounts, Anguta created the land and the waters. This is a very thin attestation — it appears in a few missionary accounts and may reflect Christian influence.

Limit: Not attested in most other Inuit regions; possibly a missionary-influenced invention.

Mutilation (in the Sedna myth)

In the most widely recorded version, Anguta chops off Sedna's fingers (and sometimes her hands) when she clings to the side of his boat. The severed fingers become sea mammals (whales, seals, walruses).

Limit: This is a narrative action, not a 'power' per se, but it is the defining act of his character.

Lordship over the dead (Adlivun)

In Boas's account, after the Sedna episode, Anguta retires to the underworld (Adlivun) where he rules over the souls of the dead. The dead must spend a year in his realm before being reincarnated or passing on.

Limit: This is a single ethnographer's account; other regions have different rulers of the dead (e.g., Sedna herself in some versions).

Psychopomp (ferryman of souls)

In some Netsilik and Iglulik accounts, Anguta (or a figure called Angut) ferries souls across a river or abyss to the land of the dead. He is a guide, not a judge.

Limit: Attested in only a few accounts; may be a regional specialization.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No consistent physical description of Anguta survives. He is not described as having a specific form in most accounts — he is a disembodied power, a voice, or a presence. In some Greenlandic accounts, he is an old man with a walrus-skin boat. In Boas's account, he is described merely as 'a powerful man' or 'a great being' who lives in the sky or in the underworld. The absence of a fixed iconography suggests Anguta was not a figure of visual worsh

Symbols — Anguta has no confirmed symbols or iconography. He is not depicted in Inuit art (carving, printmaking, or tattoo) in any pre-contact or early-contact context. His only 'symbol' is narrative: the boat (as ferryman), the knife (as mutilator), and the sky or underworld (as dwelling). The absence of symbols is itself significant — it suggests Anguta was a narrative figure, not an object of cultic worship.

Domains — Anguta's domains, where attested: 1) Creator of the earth and sky (some Greenlandic accounts). 2) Father of Sedna, the sea goddess (most widely recorded). 3) Ruler of the land of the dead (Adlivun) in Boas's account and some Baffin Island traditions. 4) Psychopomp, guiding souls to the afterlife (Netsilik accounts). 5) In some accounts, he is also the father of the winds or the controller of storms (though this is less well attested).

Accounts I
The Sedna Myth (Baffin Island version)Franz Boas, 'The Central Eskimo' (1888)

Recorded 1883-1884

Sedna, a young woman, refuses all suitors. A raven (or a dog, in some versions) seduces her with promises of a fine life, and she goes to live with him. She discovers he is a bird (or a dog, or a poor man) and cries out in distress. Her father Anguta hears her cries, comes to rescue her, and takes her back into his boat. The raven/husband summons a storm. In fear, Anguta throws Sedna overboard. She clings to the side of the boat. Anguta chops off her fingers one by one. Each finger becomes a sea mammal (seal, whale, walrus). Sedna sinks to the bottom of the sea and becomes the queen of the marine animals. In some versions, Anguta is also thrown overboard and drowned, becoming the lord of the dead. In others, he returns home and lives on, guilty and grieving. Sedna becomes the sea goddess who controls the animals. Anguta becomes (in some versions) the lord of the dead. Anguta's act of mutilation is the origin of all sea mammals. The myth explains the Inuit dependence on sea hunting and the need to appease Sedna (through shamans) when hunts fail.

Accounts II
Anguta as Creator (Greenlandic missionary accounts)Hinrich Rink, 'Tales and Traditions of the Eskimo' (1875); early missionary reports

18th-19th century

In these accounts, Anguta is described as the creator of the earth, the sea, and the sky. He is a distant, powerful being who does not intervene in human affairs. Some accounts say he created the first humans from clay or from rocks. This version is heavily influenced by Christian missionaries who sought to find an 'Inuit God' analogous to the Christian God. This version created a syncretic figure that later missionaries used to translate the Christian God. It may not reflect pre-Christian Inuit belief.

Anguta as Ferryman of the Dead (Netsilik version)Knud Rasmussen, 'Intellectual Culture of the Iglulik Eskimos' (1929)

Recorded 1921-1924

In this account, Anguta is a psychopomp who ferries the souls of the dead across a great river to the land of the dead. He is not a judge — he simply transports souls. The dead must wait for him on the shore, sometimes for days, before he comes to take them across. This version emphasizes Anguta's role as a guide, not a ruler. It suggests a more benign, bureaucratic view of the afterlife.

Anguta is the Inuit equivalent of the Christian God. — This is a missionary-influenced distortion. Early missionaries used 'Anguta' as a translation for God, but this was a pragmatic choice, not a reflection of Inuit belief. Anguta was not a single, all-powerful creator in most Inuit traditions; he was a figure within a complex pantheon of spirits, deities, and ancestors.

Anguta is a 'high god' or 'sky god' in Inuit religion. — This is an overstatement based on early ethnographic accounts (especially Boas) that sought to find a monotheistic 'high god' in Inuit religion. Anguta's role varies by region and is never that of a supreme deity.

Anguta is the devil or an evil figure. — This is a Christian-influenced misreading. Anguta's actions in the Sedna myth are violent, but the myth does not present him as 'evil' — it presents him as a flawed, desperate being. Inuit mythology does not have a single 'devil' figure.

Anguta and Sedna are the same being. — This is a confusion that arises from some accounts where Sedna's father is unnamed or where Sedna herself becomes a ruler of the dead. They are distinct figures in all recorded versions.
