Anguta's domains, where attested: 1) Creator of the earth and sky (some Greenlandic accounts). 2) Father of Sedna, the sea goddess (most widely recorded). 3) Ruler of the land of the dead (Adlivun) in Boas's account and some Baffin Island traditions. 4) Psychopomp, guiding souls to the afterlife (Netsilik accounts). 5) In some accounts, he is also the father of the winds or the controller of storms (though this is less well attested).

Form

No consistent physical description of Anguta survives. He is not described as having a specific form in most accounts — he is a disembodied power, a voice, or a presence. In some Greenlandic accounts, he is an old man with a walrus-skin boat. In Boas's account, he is described merely as 'a powerful man' or 'a great being' who lives in the sky or in the underworld. The absence of a fixed iconography suggests Anguta was not a figure of visual worship (Inuit tradition generally avoided anthropomorphic depictions of deities), but rather a being of narrative and ritual.

Powers

Creation of the earth and sea In some Greenlandic accounts, Anguta created the land and the waters. This is a very thin attestation — it appears in a few missionary accounts and may reflect Christian influence. Greenlandic, post-contact Not attested in most other Inuit regions; possibly a missionary-influenced invention. No clear contradiction — the issue is absence of evidence, not conflicting evidence.

Mutilation (in the Sedna myth) In the most widely recorded version, Anguta chops off Sedna's fingers (and sometimes her hands) when she clings to the side of his boat. The severed fingers become sea mammals (whales, seals, walruses). Baffin Island (Boas), Greenlandic (Rink), and many others This is a narrative action, not a 'power' per se, but it is the defining act of his character. In some versions, it is Sedna's husband (a raven or a dog) who mutilates her, not Anguta.

Lordship over the dead (Adlivun) In Boas's account, after the Sedna episode, Anguta retires to the underworld (Adlivun) where he rules over the souls of the dead. The dead must spend a year in his realm before being reincarnated or passing on. Baffin Island (Boas, 1888) This is a single ethnographer's account; other regions have different rulers of the dead (e.g., Sedna herself in some versions). Some accounts place Sedna as the ruler of the underworld, not Anguta.

Psychopomp (ferryman of souls) In some Netsilik and Iglulik accounts, Anguta (or a figure called Angut) ferries souls across a river or abyss to the land of the dead. He is a guide, not a judge. Netsilik/Iglulik (Rasmussen, 1929) Attested in only a few accounts; may be a regional specialization. No direct contradiction, but the role is not universal.

Behavior & Habitat

Anguta's behavior varies radically by source: 1) In Boas's version, he is a stern father who chops off his daughter's fingers and casts her into the sea, then later becomes the ruler of the underworld, presiding over the souls of the dead. 2) In some Greenlandic versions, he is a benign creator who made the land and the sea. 3) In others, he is a psychopomp who ferries souls to the land of the dead. 4) In a few accounts, he is a trickster-adjacent figure who is outwitted by his daughter. He is never described as a benevolent, loving father — his role is distant, stern, and often cruel.

Accounts

Sedna, a young woman, refuses all suitors. A raven (or a dog, in some versions) seduces her with promises of a fine life, and she goes to live with him. She discovers he is a bird (or a dog, or a poor man) and cries out in distress. Her father Anguta hears her cries, comes to rescue her, and takes her back into his boat. The raven/husband summons a storm. In fear, Anguta throws Sedna overboard. She clings to the side of the boat. Anguta chops off her fingers one by one. Each finger becomes a sea mammal (seal, whale, walrus). Sedna sinks to the bottom of the sea and becomes the queen of the marine animals. In some versions, Anguta is also thrown overboard and drowned, becoming the lord of the dead. In others, he returns home and lives on, guilty and grieving.

In these accounts, Anguta is described as the creator of the earth, the sea, and the sky. He is a distant, powerful being who does not intervene in human affairs. Some accounts say he created the first humans from clay or from rocks. This version is heavily influenced by Christian missionaries who sought to find an 'Inuit God' analogous to the Christian God.

In this account, Anguta is a psychopomp who ferries the souls of the dead across a great river to the land of the dead. He is not a judge — he simply transports souls. The dead must wait for him on the shore, sometimes for days, before he comes to take them across.

Content Notes

The core Sedna myth involves Anguta chopping off his daughter's fingers (and in some versions, her hands) to save himself from drowning. This is not a minor detail — it is the central act of the myth and the origin of sea mammals. It is a story of a father sacrificing his daughter's body for his own survival.

Anthropologists have interpreted this as a myth about the harsh realities of Arctic life, where survival often requires difficult choices. Some feminist scholars have critiqued the myth as encoding patterns of paternal control and violence against women. Others note that Sedna is not a passive victim — she becomes a powerful goddess who controls the sea and must be appeased by shamans.

Boas (1888), Rink (1875), numerous oral accounts

The use of 'Anguta' as a translation for the Christian God by missionaries is a form of cultural appropriation/erasure. It took a complex, ambiguous Inuit figure and flattened it into an analogy for a monotheistic deity, potentially distorting or erasing pre-Christian Inuit theology.

This is part of a broader pattern of Christian missionaries reshaping indigenous beliefs to fit their own framework. Some scholars argue this has caused lasting confusion about what Inuit actually believed about Anguta.

Greenlandic Bible translations (1893-1900)

Early ethnographers (Boas, Rink) may have exaggerated or distorted Anguta's importance to fit their own frameworks. Boas, in particular, was searching for a 'high god' in Inuit religion and may have over-emphasized Anguta's role as a creator or ruler of the dead.

This is a known issue in the anthropology of religion: early ethnographers often imposed their own categories (e.g., 'high god', 'creator') onto non-Western beliefs. The result may be a distorted picture of what Inuit actually believed.

Anguta's moral character
Anguta is a cruel, violent father who betrays his daughter. ⇌ Anguta is a tragic figure who acts out of fear and desperation.

Evidence for B: Some oral accounts that emphasize Anguta's grief after the event
What We Don't Know

1) PRIMARY SOURCE GAP: No pre-contact Inuit texts or artifacts depicting Anguta exist. All knowledge comes from post-contact ethnographic and missionary accounts. 2) REGIONAL GAP: Detailed accounts exist for Baffin Island, Greenland, and the Iglulik/Netsilik regions, but coverage of Alaskan Inuit (Iñupiat, Yup'ik) is thin. 3) LANGUAGE GAP: Most primary sources are in Inuktitut and have not been fully translated into English. 4) GENDER GAP: Most ethnographic accounts were recorded by male researchers from male informants; female perspectives on Anguta and Sedna may be underrepresented. 5) RIGHTS GAP: No known images of Anguta exist; any visual representation would be a modern artistic interpretation. 6) CULTURAL REVIEW NEEDED: This dossier would benefit from review by Inuit cultural experts to ensure accuracy and respectful presentation.

A way to hold it in your mind

Anguta is like the 'absent father' of Arctic mythology — a figure who is both essential and problematic. He's the parent who creates the world (or at least the sea mammals) but also the one who betrays his child. Think of him as a blend of Cronus (who devours his children) and Charon (the ferryman of the dead), but with no clear moral compass. He's not a villain and not a hero — he's a being who makes terrible choices under terrible pressure, and the consequences echo through the entire Inuit cosmos. In modern terms, he's the mythological equivalent of a complex, flawed character in a prestige drama — you can't root for him, but you can't look away.
