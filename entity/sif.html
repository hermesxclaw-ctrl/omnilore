Archive Dossier — Identity

Sif

Pronunciation SIFF (rhymes with 'cliff')

Etymology

The name Sif (Old Norse: Sif) is generally interpreted as meaning 'relation by marriage' or 'kinship', deriving from the Proto-Germanic root *sibjō, which signifies a clan or a bond of peace. This is related to the Old English 'sibb' (as in 'gossip', originally 'god-sibb', a spiritual kin), and the modern English 'sibling'. The name thus emphasizes her role as a connecting figure within the divine family, rather than a description of her own attributes.

Cultural Ownership

Norse (Old Norse-speaking peoples of Scandinavia and Iceland, attested primarily in 13th-century Icelandic texts recording earlier oral traditions)

Disputed Identifications

Some early scholars attempted to identify Sif with the earth goddess Jörð (also a wife/consort figure of Thor), but this is not supported by the primary sources. Her name's meaning 'kinship' has led to theories that she was more a titular figure (the 'kin-woman' of Thor) than a distinct personality, but the surviving myths treat her as a specific individual.

Archive Dossier — Approach

She is the wife of the thunder god, a woman whose hair was so famous it became a divine artifact, and yet she has no story of her own. In the surviving myths, Sif is a golden-haired figure standing in the shadow of Thor's hammer, defined by what others do to her. But her silence has made her a canvas: for Marvel, she is a fierce warrior; for modern pagans, a harvest queen; for the gods themselves, she is the 'kin-woman', the tie that binds the family of Asgard together. Her story is one of beauty, violation, and the quiet power of being the center of a family that holds the cosmos together.

Who It Is

Sif is the golden-haired goddess associated with the harvest and grain, wife of Thor, and mother of Ullr (by a previous marriage) and of Thrud (with Thor). Her most famous myth involves Loki sneaking into her bedroom and shaving off all her glorious golden hair while she slept. Thor, enraged, caught Loki and would have broken every bone in his body, but Loki swore to make amends. He went to the dwarves, who fashioned new hair from gold that would grow like real hair, along with the spear Gungnir for Odin and the foldable ship Skíðblaðnir for Freyr. In a creative contest between dwarves, Brokkr and Sindri also created Thor's hammer Mjölnir. Sif's golden hair thus indirectly led to the creation of many of the gods' greatest treasures. Sif represents the golden fields of grain at harvest time — her hair is the crop, golden and abundant. After a long winter, the return of golden Sif to Thor (who represents the summer sun and thunderstorms) symbolizes the return of fertility to the land.

Archive Dossier — Description

Appearance

Sif is described in the Prose Edda as 'the fairest of women' (Old Norse: 'hón er allra kvenna vænst'). Her defining feature is her hair, which is famously golden. In the myth, her original hair is cut off by Loki, and she is given a replacement made of gold by the dwarves, which 'grew' naturally. This has led to interpretations of her as a grain or harvest goddess, with her golden hair representing golden fields.

Version Differences

There are no significant contradictory versions of Sif's story in the primary sources. The main difference is between the Poetic Edda (where she is a silent, passive figure) and the Prose Edda (where she is the subject of the hair-cutting story). The later, modern interpretation of her as a harvest goddess is a scholarly reconstruction, not an attested tradition.

Behavior

Sif has very little agency in the surviving myths. She is primarily defined by her relationships: she is Thor's wife, the mother of Ullr, and the target of Loki's crude insult in Lokasenna. The Lokasenna passage suggests she was a peacemaker, as she is shown offering a drink to Loki and trying to calm the brewing conflict. She is not described as a warrior, nor does she have any known magical powers of her own.

Domains

Her domains are not clearly defined in the sources. Her name ('kinship') suggests a role as a domestic and familial figure. Her golden hair has inspired modern theories of a connection to grain/harvest, but this is a reconstruction, not an attested fact. She resides in Þrúðvangr (Thor's realm) with her husband.

Symbols

Her primary symbol is her golden hair. This is not just an aesthetic feature but a physical object of great value and craftsmanship, created by dwarves. It is one of the three great treasures Loki secures for the gods in the same story (along with Odin's spear Gungnir and Freyr's ship Skíðblaðnir), and is used as a bargaining chip in a wager with the dwarf Brokkr.

Origins

Sif is a goddess of the Aesir, daughter of unspecified parentage. The name 'Sif' means 'in-law' or 'relation by marriage,' making her the bridge between different families of gods. She had a son, Ullr, before marrying Thor, by whom she had Thrud.

Attested in Poetic Edda (Lokasenna, Hymiskviða) / Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál)Era Viking Age (c. 800-1100 CE), recorded 13th century

Archive Dossier — Powers

Golden Hair

Source Version Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35

Description

Sif's golden hair is a divine artifact in itself. After Loki cuts off her original hair, the dwarves forge a new head of hair from gold that is described as growing naturally. This is a unique object in Norse mythology, being a gift of the dwarves and a symbol of her husband's honor and her own beauty.

Limitations

The hair has no described powers beyond its natural growth and beauty. It is a symbol of status and a testament to the skill of its makers.

Contradictions

None.

Prophecy

Source Version Poetic Edda, Lokasenna (prose introduction)

Description

In Lokasenna, Loki accuses Sif of having invited him to her bed, but a prose note in the same text states that 'Sif is a prophetess, and she is called by the name Sif'. This is the only hint of any supernatural ability beyond her physical beauty.

Limitations

The nature and extent of this prophecy are entirely unexplored. It is a single, unelaborated mention.

Contradictions

This is a single, unelaborated mention. It is not referenced anywhere else.

Archive Dossier — Relationships

Thor

Type wifeSource Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 31; Skáldskaparmál 35; Lokasenna

Description

Sif is Thor's wife. She is the mother of his daughter Þrúðr (and possibly his sons Modi and Magni, though this is not explicitly stated). Thor is fiercely protective of her honor, as seen when he threatens Loki over the hair-cutting.

Version Note

Consistently presented as his wife.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Ullr

Type sonSource Prose Edda, Gylfaginning 31; Skáldskaparmál 35

Description

Sif is the mother of Ullr, a god associated with skiing, hunting, and archery. The Prose Edda does not name Ullr's father, stating only that he is Sif's son. This has led to speculation that Ullr was an older, more prominent god who was later displaced by Thor, and that his father was a different, possibly forgotten, figure.

Version Note

The father is unnamed.

Conflicting Accounts

Some modern sources speculate the father is a forgotten deity, but this is not attested.

Loki

Type antagonistSource Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35; Lokasenna

Description

Loki's relationship with Sif is one of malice and disrespect. He cuts off her hair and later publicly accuses her of infidelity. This is part of a larger pattern of Loki's disruptive behavior against the gods. The accusation in Lokasenna is particularly notable because it is a sexual insult that is silenced by Thor's brute force, not by a denial or a counter-argument.

Version Note

Consistently antagonistic.

Conflicting Accounts

Loki's accusation of infidelity is left unresolved.

Þrúðr

Type daughterSource Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál; Alvíssmál

Description

Þrúðr is the daughter of Thor and Sif. Her name means 'strength' or 'power'. She is referenced in a few poems and is the subject of a story where the dwarf Alvíss comes to claim her as his bride, a claim that is thwarted by Thor. She is a minor figure.

Version Note

Consistently presented as their daughter.

Conflicting Accounts

None.

Sources & Standing
Well-Attested

Multiple independent primary sources agree on the core narratives. Primary attestation: Poetic Edda (Lokasenna, Hymiskviða) / Prose Edda (Skáldskaparmál).
