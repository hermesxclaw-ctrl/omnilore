Who It Is

Of the fifty sons Heracles fathered in the house of King Thespius, Capylus was one — born not from a celebrated marriage or divine romance, but from a calculated night of hospitality, hunting, and royal ambition in Boeotia. He never slew a monster or won a throne in surviving myth; his destiny was stranger: to be counted, sorted, and shipped to colonize a distant island at his father's command.

No personality, speech, or moral traits are individually attested. Collective behavior: the sons are obedient to Heracles' and Iolaus' colonial command. Diodorus portrays them as colonists/founders under Iolaus' leadership. No myth shows Capylus speaking, choosing, or acting independently. Any personality ascribed is hypothetical.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — Ancient Greek Καπύλος. Etymology not explained in ancient sources. Powers
Heraclid lineage (no individual powers attested)

As son of Heracles, Capylus would be considered a Heraclid with heroic ancestry, but no superhuman strength, divine power, or specific feat is attributed to him individually in any surviving source. No transformation, weapon mastery, or resistance is described.

Limit: Mortality is assumed; no immortality or apotheosis. No evidence of Heracles' divine strength inherited.

Colonial founder status (collective, conditional)

If Capylus was among the forty sent to Sardinia, he would be counted as an oikist/colonist under Iolaus, credited in Diodorus with founding settlements and introducing Greek rites in Sardinia. This is a status, not a supernatural power.

Limit: Not attested individually for Capylus; depends on which disposition group he was assigned to, which is unknown.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient source describes Capylus individually — no stature, hair, clothing, armor, or facial features are attested. As a Thespian son of Heracles, he is generically understood as a Heraclid male of Boeotian royal descent, but this is reconstruction, not attestation. No vase painting, sculpture, or coin is identified as Capylus specifically. LIMC entries for Herakles and Thespiadai show collective youths but no labeled Capylus. Status: unknown

Symbols — No individual symbols, weapons, or iconographic attributes are attested for Capylus. Collectively, the Thespiadae are sometimes associated with Heraclean symbols (lion skin, club) and with colonial symbols of Sardinia (Iolaus as oikist). Thespiae's civic coinage featured Heracles and later Eros, but not individual sons. No verified visual record exists for Capylus.

Domains — Birthplace: Thespiae, Boeotia (city at foot of Mt. Helicon). Narrative habitats: Thespian palace during Heracles' stay; subsequently, according to Heracles' disposition, either Thespiae (if among the seven retained), Thebes (if among the three sent to Thebes), or Sardinia (if among the forty colonists). Accounts I
The Cithaeronian Lion Hunt at ThespiaeApollodorus Bibliotheca 2.4.10; Diodorus Siculus 4.29.2; Pausanias 9.27.6

Mythic time of Heracles' youth (before Labours); literary attestation Classical to Roman

Eighteen-year-old Heracles, living with King Amphitryon or at Thebes, is hosted by King Thespius of Thespiae while hunting the lion of Mt. Cithaeron that preyed on Thespius' and Amphitryon's herds. Thespius entertains Heracles for fifty days, sending a different daughter to his bed each night (or all in one night in variant). The lion is slain and flayed; Heracles wears its skin thereafter in some accounts. Capylus is conceived during this sojourn as the son of Hippo. Conception of fifty sons including Capylus; establishment of Heraclid lineage in Thespiae; Heracles' first lion-slaying feat prefiguring Nemean Lion; Thespius secures heroic grandchildren.

The Night(s) with the Fifty Daughters of ThespiusAthenaeus Deipnosophistae 13.566e citing Herodorus; Apollodorus 2.4.10; Tzetzes Chiliades 2.221-227

Archaic myth elaborated in Classical historiography

Thespius, having fifty daughters by his wife Megamede (daughter of Arneus) according to Apollodorus, wishes them all to bear children by Heracles. He plies Heracles with wine and sends his daughters to him, one after another, sometimes without Heracles realizing they are different women. Hippo, mother of Capylus, is one of them. All fifty conceive. Birth of fifty sons (Thespiadae), including Capylus; later division and colonization; Thespian royal house linked to Heracles.

The Division of the Sons: Seven, Three, and FortyApollodorus 2.7.8; Diodorus 4.29.3-4

Mythic generation after Heracles' youth; literary form Hellenistic/Roman

Years later, Heracles, before his death or before departing for other labours, sends instructions to Thespius regarding the now-grown sons. Thespius is to retain seven sons in Thespiae, send three to Thebes, and send the remaining forty, joined by his nephew Iolaus, to found a colony in Sardinia. Apollodorus presents this as a message; Diodorus as Heracles' direct arrangement. Creates three branches of Heraclids: Thespian, Theban, and Sardinian (Iolaans). Explains Boeotian and Sardinian Heracles cults. Capylus' fate depends on which group he was allotted — unattested which.

Accounts II
The Sardinian Expedition of Iolaus and the ThespiadaeDiodorus 4.29-30, 5.15; Pausanias 10.17.5; Apollodorus 2.7.8

Mythic colonization era; written 1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE

Iolaus, Heracles' nephew and charioteer, leads the forty sons (including possibly Capylus) to Sardinia. There they subdue barbarians, found cities, introduce Greek customs, and are later called Iolaans. Diodorus says they built works and were honored as heroes; Pausanias says the Iolaans in Sardinia still offered worship to Iolaus in his time. Aetiology for Sardinian place-names and cults of Iolaus/Heracles; links Sardinia to Greek mythic geography; provides heroic ancestry for Sardinian peoples in Greek eyes.

The Thespian Retention and Local Hero CultPausanias 9.27.6-7; Inscriptional evidence for Thespiae Heracleia

Classical to Roman era cult practice

Seven sons remain in Thespiae as local princes/heroes. Pausanias, visiting Thespiae in the 2nd c. CE, notes the Thespians honored Heracles and that the city had sanctuaries and traditions linked to the Thespiadae. While Capylus is not named in Pausanias' extant text, as son of Hippo he would be part of this collective memory. Thespian festivals (Heracleia, Erotidia) celebrated Heracles and his descendants. Anchors Thespian identity to Heracles; provides religious justification for elite genealogies claiming Heraclid descent.

Thespius' Stratagem: Hospitality as Procreative PoliticsAthenaeus 13.566 citing Herodorus; Apollodorus 2.4.10 scholia; Tzetzes 2.222

5th century BCE (Herodorus) onward

A meta-story about Thespius' intent: desiring that all his daughters bear Heracles' children to gain heroic lineage, he exploits xenia (guest-host obligation). Heracles, focused on the lion hunt and wine, complies unknowingly or willingly. The episode is presented as both hospitality and manipulation, with Hippo's union producing Capylus. Explains why fifty daughters were all impregnated; illustrates Greek themes of guest-friendship, lineage strategy, and Heracles' virility.

Capylus was a hero who performed labours or had powers like Heracles — No feat, power, or labour is attributed to Capylus individually. He is solely a genealogical figure in the Thespiadae list; any powers are invented by modern conflation with his father.

All fifty sons went to Sardinia — Only forty went to Sardinia with Iolaus; seven remained in Thespiae and three were settled in Thebes per Apollodorus 2.7.8 and Diodorus 4.29.

Heracles slept with Thespius' daughters with their enthusiastic consent as a reward — Ancient sources frame it as Thespius' stratagem, often involving wine and deception (Heracles thinking it was the same woman), with daughters' consent never discussed. Modern retellings sanitize the power imbalance.

Capylus is the same as Capys, ancestor of Aeneas — Capylus (Καπύλος), Thespian son of Heracles, is distinct from Capys (Κάπυς), son of Assaracus, king of Dardania and grandfather of Anchises. Different genealogy, era, and region.

There is ancient art depicting Capylus — No vase, sculpture, or coin has been identified as Capylus. LIMC finds no individual iconography for any Thespiadae except collectively as youths with Iolaus.
