Who It Is

On the northeastern coast of Papua New Guinea, an ancient story tells of two brothers whose quarrel shaped the world: one became the source of canoes, taro, and pigs, the other of ships, guns, and rice. This myth of Kilibob and Manup is not a single fixed tale but a living, regional complex that explains the origins of landmarks, trade, and the unequal distribution of wealth—and, in colonial times, the mystery of why Europeans held so many manufactured goods.

Kilibob/Kulbob is a culture hero and creator, often depicted as a hunter and carver. He is the younger brother who is wronged and later becomes the source of valuable objects and knowledge. He is clever and transformative, using fallen wood to create canoes and ships.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Not described in the available sources. In Karkar versions, Kulbob is associated with a lizard form when he escapes into a tree.

Symbols — Associated with the ngaul tree, canoes, ships, and European manufactured goods. On Karkar, linked to Mt. Kanagioi and Kulbob Bay.

Domains — Associated with the sea, islands, and specific landmarks on Karkar Island and the Madang coast. He is a figure of the coastal and maritime environment.

Accounts I
The Quarrel of the BrothersZ'graggen's survey; McSwain's Karkar account

Pre-colonial; recorded in the 20th century

The younger brother's arrow lands near the elder brother's wife. She asks him to carve its decorative pattern onto her body. The elder brother interprets this as adultery and attacks the younger brother. After repeated attacks, the younger brother escapes into a tree, which is felled and falls into the sea or bay. The younger brother reappears as a culture maker, creating canoes and ships from the fallen tree and distributing wealth and knowledge.

Accounts II
The Choice of GoodsZ'graggen's survey

Colonial era

The younger brother offers the older brother a choice between local goods (bows, arrows, canoes, taro, pigs) and European goods (firearms, dinghies, rice, canned food). The older brother chooses the local goods, explaining why Europeans possess manufactured wealth. Explains the unequal distribution of wealth between Europeans and New Guineans.

The Voyage and Landscape FormationKarkar traditions (McSwain)

Pre-colonial

After escaping, Kulbob constructs a great canoe and smaller craft from the ngaul tree. In one version, a large ship with a funnel and European goods rises from the water. He loads the vessel with valuable artifacts, animals, and food plants, releases beautiful young people from bamboo containers, and sails away. His voyages cause islands and channels to take their present form; the capsized canoe becomes Karkar Island and its outrigger Bagbag Island. Creates landmarks and distributes knowledge and resources across the region.
