Who It Is

When Telipinu vanished in a rage, the world stopped growing. Seeds refused to sprout, hearths smoked without fire, sheep abandoned their lambs and cattle their calves, and even the great Storm-god could not find his own son — until the gods sent a tiny bee on an impossible search through mountains and rivers to sting a sleeping god awake.

Myth presents Telipinu as mercurial, sensitive, and prone to sudden, overwhelming rage (Hittite kartimmiyatt-). His anger is not moral but elemental: when offended (cause never stated), he withdraws, and his absence causes cosmic sterility. He is not evil; he is essential and childlike — etymology as 'child' matches his behavior: he sulks, hides, falls into deep sleep in a meadow or moor, and must be coaxed, not coerced. The gods describe him as powerful yet vulnerable to small agents (bee). After his return, pacified by Kamrusepa's ritual, he is benevolent, restoring growth,

Aliases — Telepinu, Telibinu, Talipinu, Telipuna, D Telipinu (determinative DINGIR), Telepinuš (Hittite nominative)

Powers
Vegetative Fertility Control

Embodies and controls growth of grain, plants, and crops; his presence makes barley and wheat grow, his absence halts all germination and withers fields.

Limit: Power is passive and tied to his emotional state; he cannot be compelled to return by force, only soothed. Requires ritual appeasement after return to stay.

Pastoral and Human Fertility Influence

When angry, causes cattle, sheep, and humans to cease reproduction: cows reject calves, ewes reject lambs, mothers neglect children; on return, fertility resumes.

Limit: Effect is collateral of his withdrawal, not a targeted curse; reversed automatically upon his pacification.

Cosmic Withering / Famine Induction

His disappearance triggers universal sterility: hearths go out, rivers dry, gods and humans starve, even the Sun-god and Storm-god cannot feast.

Limit: Self-destructive; he also suffers hunger and isolation while hidden, requiring rescue.

Superhuman Vitality and Elusiveness

Can hide so effectively that major gods including the Storm-god and Hannahanna cannot find him; sleeps deeply in remote moors, impervious to divine search.

Limit: Vulnerable to the small bee of Hannahanna, which can traverse all landscapes and sting him awake; vulnerable to Kamrusepa's magic.

Pacified Beneficence and Kingship Legitimation

Once appeased with honey, wax, oil, and incantations by Kamrusepa, returns bringing abundance, restores kingship, and guarantees the land's prosperity; invoked in royal oaths.

Limit: Requires continuous cult offerings and correct ritual; anger can recur if neglected.

Royal Authority and Legal Reform (King Telipinu)

As king, exercised authority to codify succession, regulate palace bloodshed, conduct treaties (alliance with Kizzuwatna), and lead limited military recovery against Mitanni-Hurrians.

Limit: Controlled only core Hittite heartland; unable to prevent subsequent Middle Kingdom dark age; succession law ultimately failed to prevent later usurpations.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No canonical anthropomorphic description survives. As a Hattian-Hittite deity, Telipinu is implied to be anthropomorphic in myth (he sleeps, walks, wears shoes, is stung by a bee) but never described in physical detail. Hittite art does not securely identify him; unlike the Storm-god with pointed cap and mace or the Sun-goddess of Arinna with solar disk, Telipinu lacks a fixed iconographic attribute in surviving reliefs at Yazılıkaya or Alaca Höy

Symbols — Primary symbols: grain, sprout, furrow, plow, and fertile field; hearth and smoke (when absent, hearths are choked); the bee and honey/wax as instruments of his pacification. Ritual texts associate him with the oak and with honey, wax, and oil used by the healing goddess Kamrusepa to soothe his anger. In festival lists his offerings include bread, beer, sheep, and first fruits, marking him as a grain/vegetation god. His sacred animals are cattle

Domains — Divine Telipinu: god of agriculture, grain, vegetation, fertility of fields, and by extension pastoral fertility and human vitality. He inhabits the countryside, steppe, meadow, moor, and forest — liminal wild places outside the city — as well as the furrow and threshing floor. Accounts I
The Disappearance of Telipinu (Main Recension)CTH 324 - KUB 17.10 + KUB 17.9

Old Hittite composition, New Hittite copies 15th-13th c. BC

Telipinu flies into a rage for an unstated reason and vanishes. Immediately mist seizes windows, smoke chokes houses, hearths are stifled, altars are stifled, cattle and sheep are stifled, humans are stifled, and barley and wheat do not grow. The great gods search: the Sun-god goes, the Storm-god Tarhunna searches his son, but none find him. Hannahanna, the grandmother/mother goddess, dispatches a small bee, telling it to go sting Telipinu's hands and feet and bring him back. The bee searches high mountains, deep valleys, and rivers, finally finding Telipinu asleep in a meadow in the town of Lihzina. It stings his hands and feet, waxes his eyes and feet, and wakes him in greater fury. His anger now threatens to destroy rivers and houses. Finally the healing/magic goddess Kamrusepa appeases him with rites of honey, wax, and oil, soothing his heart like a reed bed, and escorts him home on an eagle's wings. He returns and fertility, feasting, and order resume. Establishes Telipinu as the archetypal 'disappearing god' whose anger explains famine and whose return explains spring/abundance

Kamrusepa's Healing Ritual for TelipinuCTH 325 - KUB 17.12, KUB 17.13

Middle Hittite 15th-14th c. BC

After the bee wakes the enraged Telipinu, the goddess Kamrusepa (goddess of healing and magic) performs a ritual to cool his anger. She takes cedar, honey, wax, oil, and incense, recites incantations to draw anger from his heart, liver, and limbs as one draws a splinter, and transfers it to the wilderness. She declares: 'As honey is sweet, as wax is pure, so may Telipinu's heart be sweet.' She washes him, anoints him, and leads him back to his temple and to his father Tarhunna. The ritual is both myth and performative magic to be recited when fields fail. Provides one of the most complete Hittite magical rituals; shows transfer of divine anger to wilderness and use of sympathetic magic; copied as a model for human healing rituals.

The Edict of Telipinu and the Purge of the PalaceCTH 19 - KBo 3.1 (Edict of Telipinu)

Reign of King Telipinu c.1525-1500 BC (middle chronology)

The Edict opens with a historical prologue recounting the bloodshed of the late Old Kingdom: King Ammuna murdered his family, Huzziya I murdered Ammuna's sons, Telipinu (son of Ammuna, son-in-law of Huzziya via Ištapariya) deposed Huzziya and his five brothers, exiling rather than executing them. Telipinu's rivals then murdered his son Ammuna and his wife Ištapariya. Telipinu seized the assassins but, declaring 'bloodshed must stop,' commuted death sentences to banishment. To prevent recurrence, he promulgated a succession law: only a prince of the first rank (son of a chief wife) may be king; failing that, a son of a second-rank wife; failing male issue, the husband (antiyant) of a first-rank princess shall be king. He also reorganized the palace staff (pankus assembly) and storehouses to curb corruption. First known Hittite constitutional law; stabilized succession temporarily but failed to prevent Middle Kingdom usurpations; primary source for Old Kingdom chronology and for Hittite concepts of justice and kingship.

Accounts II
Telipinu's Alliance with Kizzuwatna and Hurrian WarCTH 19 §§28-34; CTH 40 fragments

Late Old Kingdom c.1510 BC

After securing the throne, King Telipinu turned to foreign affairs. The Edict notes he 'went to Kizzuwatna' and made a treaty with its Hurrian ruler Isputahsu, forming an alliance against the greater Hurrian power of Mitanni (Hanigalbat). With Kizzuwatna as buffer and ally, he recovered some southeastern territory lost under Ammuna and reasserted control over the Cilician Gates. The campaign is described briefly, without the bombast of Hattusili I's annals, emphasizing restoration over conquest. The treaty is the earliest surviving Hittite-Kizzuwatna parity treaty, precursor to later Empire-period treaties. Temporarily secured southern frontier; model for later Hittite vassal treaties; but gains were lost again in the ensuing 70-year Middle Kingdom dark age.

Telipinu in the AN.TAH.ŠUM Spring FestivalCTH 591 - KUB 10.48, KUB 20.13 (AN.TAH.ŠUM festival)

Empire period 14th-13th c. BC

In the 35-day AN.TAH.ŠUM (crocus) spring festival, Telipinu is invoked on Day 22-23 alongside the Storm-god and Sun-goddess of Arinna. Priests offer him bread, beer, and a sheep in his temple at Hattusa, reciting his myth as a liturgy for agricultural renewal. The text lists his provision of 'life, health, and abundance of grain' and asks him to 'make the field thrive as before.' His statue is anointed with honey, recalling Kamrusepa's ritual. The festival shows his transformation from mythic protagonist to calendrical guarantor of spring. Demonstrates institutionalization of Telipinu's myth into state religion; links mythic narrative to seasonal agricultural cycle; provides evidence for temple economy.

The Vanishing of Other Gods: Telipinu as Archetype for Hannahanna and Inara CyclesCTH 334 (Disappearance of Hannahanna), CTH 336 (Myth of Inara)

Old to Middle Hittite 16th-14th c. BC

Telipinu's story belongs to a genre of Hattian 'disappearing god' myths. Parallel tales tell of Hannahanna herself disappearing, of the goddess Inara inviting the Storm-god to a feast, and of the Storm-god's son disappearing. In each, divine anger causes cosmic dysfunction, a search fails, and a small or marginal agent (bee, eagle) succeeds. Comparison shows Telipinu is the most complete and perhaps original of the cycle, with Hannahanna's own disappearance possibly modeled on his. These stories were recited together in winter rituals to explain and avert divine abandonment. Reveals Hattian narrative pattern and Hittite theology of divine presence/absence; helps scholars reconstruct Hattian religion before Hittite conquest.

Telipinu the god and King Telipinu were the same person, a deified king. — They are distinct: the god is a Bronze Age Hattian vegetation deity (attested centuries before the king), the king was a historical ruler c.1500 BC named after the god. No Hittite text deifies the king.

Telipinu is the Hittite god of the underworld or death. — He is a god of agriculture, grain, and fertility; his absence causes death by famine, but he does not rule the underworld (that is Lelwani).

The bee kills Telipinu with its sting. — The bee stings his hands and feet to wake him; it enrages him further, requiring Kamrusepa's soothing. He returns alive.
