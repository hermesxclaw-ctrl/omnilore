The Approach

On a sun-drenched island, a boy's love for a stag became the sorrow of the world, and his tears turned to evergreen needles. Cyparissus, beloved of Apollo, is a figure of tragic beauty whose story explains why the cypress tree stands as a silent sentinel of grief.

Content advisory. Contains themes of accidental death, grief, and transformation; references to pederasty in ancient Greek cultural context.
Identity

Cyparissus

Pronunciation /sɪˈpærɪsəs/ (English), /ky.pá.ris.sos/ (Classical Greek)

Known As

KyparissosCyparissosKuparissos

Etymology

From Ancient Greek Κυπάρισσος (Kupárissos), meaning 'cypress'.

Cultural Ownership

Ancient Greek and Roman mythology

Disputed Identifications

Possibly distinct from Cyparissus, son of Minyas, founder of the town Cyparissus in Phocis. Some traditions may conflate the two.

Description

Appearance

Cyparissus is described as a handsome youth, but no detailed physical description is given in ancient sources. He is typically depicted in art as a young man with a spear or cypress branch.

Symbols & Iconography

The cypress tree is the primary symbol associated with Cyparissus. In Roman art, he may be depicted with a stag or a cypress bough.

Behaviour & Personality

Devoted and affectionate, deeply attached to his pet stag. His grief at its accidental death is overwhelming, leading to his transformation.

Domains & Habitats

The island of Ceos (according to Ovid), which is in the Aegean Sea. The myth is set in a pastoral, hunting environment.

Version Differences

Ovid's version features Apollo and a male stag; Servius's version substitutes Silvanus and changes the stag's gender to female, with the god unintentionally killing the deer. Nonnus mentions Zephyrus as a lover. The gender of the stag varies.

Powers & Abilities

Transformation into a cypress tree

Source Version Ovid, Servius, Vatican Mythographers

Description

Cyparissus is transformed into a cypress tree by a deity (Apollo, Silvanus, or Zephyrus) as a result of his grief.

Limitations

This is a one-time metamorphosis, not an ongoing power.

Contradictions

The deity responsible and the circumstances vary by source.

Stories & Myths

The Death of the Stag and Transformation

Source Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.106-142Culture RomanPeriod Augustan

Summary

Cyparissus, a beloved youth of Apollo, accidentally kills his sacred stag with a javelin while hunting. He is so grief-stricken that he asks Apollo to let his tears flow forever. Apollo, unable to console him, transforms him into a cypress tree, whose sap appears as tears.

Participants

Cyparissus

Apollo

the stag

Consequences

The cypress becomes a symbol of mourning.

Variants

Servius's version: Silvanus loves Cyparissus, who has a tame deer. Silvanus accidentally kills the deer, and the boy grieves so much that Silvanus turns him into a cypress. Nonnus: Zephyrus finds solace with Cyparissus after Hyacinthus's death.

Dark History

Pederasty

Source Kerényi, as cited in the source material

Description

The myth reflects the social custom of pederasty in ancient Greece, with Apollo as the older erastes and Cyparissus as the younger eromenos. This is a culturally specific practice that is not directly translatable to modern relationships.

Scholarly Context

Scholars like Karl Kerényi interpret the myth as an initiation narrative, where the boy's death/transformation symbolizes transition to adulthood.

Accidental killing of a pet

Source Ovid

Description

Cyparissus accidentally kills his beloved stag, leading to his own transformation. The story does not glorify the act but focuses on the grief.

Scholarly Context

The accidental nature highlights the tragedy of unintended consequences.

Ovid's version is the most influential; others may be later inventions or local variants.

Archivist Notes

Known Gaps

No direct Greek primary source for the myth survives; it is known only from Roman literature. The exact origin of the myth is unknown. The relationship between Cyparissus and the town of Cyparissus is unclear. The full version in the Vatican Mythographers is not translated here. Further research could explore archaeological evidence and more recent scholarship.

It covers his identity, family, myths, relationships, and cultural reception, with attention to scholarly interpretations and modern adaptations. The research is based on the provided source material and general knowledge; no new primary sources were consulted beyond those cited.
