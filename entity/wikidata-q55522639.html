Who It Is

In the dense genealogical lists where the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki stitch the Japanese archipelago together from marriages between mountains, seas and winds, a name flickers and vanishes: Torinarumi no Kami — the Kami of the Bird-Crying Sea. No epic is told about him, no shrine claims him as its sole master, yet his placement in the lineage of the mountain and sea deities marks a hinge between the wild elemental world and the human clans who would later claim descent from the gods.

No narrative behavior, speech, or personality traits are attested. As a genealogical kami (a 'link' deity in the lineage), his function is structural: to connect the elemental deities (mountain/sea) to human clan ancestors. He does not act as trickster, warrior, or culture hero in surviving stories. Edo kokugaku commentators (Motoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den) treat such list-kami as 'ancestral pillars' rather than characterful agents. Status: unattested; any personality ascribed in modern fiction is adaptation-specific.

Powers
Genealogical/Ancestral Authority (Ujigami function)

As a kami in the divine lineage that leads to the Owari and related clans, his attested 'power' is ancestral legitimacy: descent from him confers divine ancestry on human kin groups. This is a sociopolitical/ritual power, not a supernatural feat. In Kiki ideology, being listed in the divine genealogy is itself the source of clan status.

Limit: No miracle, creation, or destruction is attributed; authority is passive and inherited. Not invoked in norito for practical benefits (healing, safe voyage) in surviving liturgy.

Inferred Marine-Avian Domain Affinity

By name, association with seabirds and sea. Reconstructed as having affinity over coastal birds and the sea surface where they cry. This is etymological inference, not textual attestation of ability (e.g., no story shows him calming waves or commanding birds).

Limit: No feat, spell, or ritual is described; cannot be cited as attested power. Must be labeled hypothetical.

Absence of Combat or Magical Feats

Explicit negative finding: no weapon (sword, bow, jewel), transformation, curse, blessing, or resistance is attested. Unlike Futsunushi, Takemikazuchi, or Watatsumi who have active episodes, Torinarumi no Kami has no recorded feat or failure.

Limit: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence in lost oral traditions, but within surviving canon, powers are unattested.

Accounts I
The Genealogical List of Ōyamatsumi's Descendants — Kojiki Birth RegisterKojiki, Book 1 (Kamitsumaki), Ō no Yasumaro 712

Nara period codification of earlier oral genealogy (mythic time: Age of the Gods - Kamiyo)

In the Kojiki's dense genealogy following the birth of the mountain deity Ōyamatsumi no Kami (son of Izanagi and Izanami) and his union with the sea deity's lineage, Torinarumi no Kami appears as one link in a chain of deities who bridge the elemental world and the human world. The text lists his name phonographically among several siblings/children without narrative elaboration: he is born, his name is given, and the genealogy proceeds to the next generation. No dialogue, deed, or location is provided. The function of the passage is to establish continuity from the primal kami to the clans of the central islands. Establishes divine ancestry for human kin groups; provides the textual anchor for all later references to Torinarumi no Kami. Without this list, the deity would be unknown. Later shrine genealogies and clan registers (Shinsen Shōjiroku) cite this passage to claim descent.

Nihon Shoki Variant Genealogy (Issho) — The Alternate LineNihon Shoki, Book 1, variant notes (issho / aru fumi) 720

Nara period, 720

The Nihon Shoki presents the main narrative of the Age of the Gods and then appends 'one writing says...' (issho ni iwaku) variants. In one such variant genealogical note, the position occupied by Torinarumi no Kami in the Kojiki is given with a slightly different sequence or phonetic spelling, or the lineage is traced through a different intermediate kami. The variant confirms that the genealogy was not fixed in the 8th century and that multiple oral lineages were being harmonized. Again, no action is described — only lineage. Demonstrates textual fluidity; modern scholars use the variants to argue that Torinarumi no Kami may be a local toponymic deity absorbed into the court genealogy rather than a pan-Yamato figure. Shows that even in 720 his name was already subject to scribal variation.

Accounts II
The Owari Clan Ancestor Charter — From Kami to MurajiShinsen Shōjiroku (815) and Atsuta Shrine tradition; Kojiki/Nihon Shoki genealogical extension via Owari no Muraji lineage

Early Heian (815) retrojecting mythic ancestry; shrine tradition continues to present

Later clan registers and shrine genealogies extend the Kiki lineage to human history: the descendants of the mountain/sea kami, including Torinarumi no Kami's generation, become ancestors of the Owari no Muraji, the hereditary custodians of Atsuta Shrine and maternal relatives of Emperor Keitai and Empress Jingu traditions. In this charter myth, Torinarumi no Kami is not an actor but a generational marker: 'X begat Torinarumi, who begat Y, who begat the Owari ancestors.' The story is thus a political genealogy, not a mythic adventure, used to legitimize the Owari clan's control of the Atsuta cult and the Kusanagi sword. Gives Torinarumi no Kami post-mythic relevance as a clan ujigami (ancestral deity). Explains why his name survives despite lack of myth: clan preservation. Atsuta Shrine's auxiliary shrines enshrine the broader lineage, though no independent Torinarumi shrine is attested.

Motoori Norinaga's Philological Resurrection — Kojiki-den (1798)Motoori Norinaga, Kojiki-den (Commentary on the Kojiki), 1798 (Edo period kokugaku)

Edo period, 1798

In his monumental 44-volume commentary, Motoori Norinaga pauses at the obscure name Torinarumi and attempts to reconstruct its meaning. He argues for the reading 'Tori-naru-mi' and proposes kanji 鳥鳴海, interpreting it as 'the sea where birds cry,' linking it to coastal toponyms and suggesting the deity personifies a specific inlet or bird-rich sea. Norinaga admits the name is 'without story' (koto nashi) and treats it as evidence of how ancient names encoded landscape. His discussion is the first extended 'story' about the deity — a scholarly detective story, not a myth. Norinaga's kanji choice became standard in modern editions and dictionaries, shaping all later reception. His interpretation that the name is toponymic rather than personal influenced 20th-century folklore studies. The episode illustrates how Edo scholarship created meaning for list-kami.

The Silent Kami in Modern Scholarship — Dictionary and Database EntryKokugakuin Encyclopedia of Shinto, Nihon Shinwa Jiten (Encyclopedia of Japanese Mythology), and digital Jinja databases (1990s-present)

Contemporary (1994-present)

In modern reference works, Torinarumi no Kami receives a 2-3 sentence entry: name, reading, position in genealogy, and note 'details unknown' (shōsai fumei). The 'story' here is the story of forgetting and cataloging: databases must decide whether to give him a separate page or merge him into a table. Kokugakuin lists him in the Ōyamatsumi genealogical table with a footnote on variant kanji; other encyclopedias omit him entirely. The entry itself becomes the primary way modern readers encounter him — as an example of the hundreds of kami who exist only as names. Defines contemporary knowledge boundary: confirms obscurity, prevents conflation, and flags the deity as 'attested but unnarrated.' Serves as a cautionary example against inventing lore for obscure kami.

Torinarumi no Kami is a major sea god who controls waves and commands birds, similar to Watatsumi. — No feat of sea-control or bird-command is attested. His name suggests sea/bird association, but Kiki texts give no story or power. Watatsumi is the active sea deity with palace and powers; Torinarumi is a silent genealogical link.

Torinarumi no Kami has a dedicated shrine and festival, and can be visited at a specific Jinja. — Engishiki Jinmyōchō (927) lists no shrine for Torinarumi no Kami; Jinja Honchō database shows no independent shrine. He is venerated only collectively as part of the Ōyamatsumi/Watatsumi/Owari lineage at shrines like Atsuta Jingū, not individually. No matsuri is dedicated to him alone.

The kanji 鳥鳴海神 is ancient and appears in the original Kojiki manuscript. — The oldest Kojiki manuscripts (Shinpukuji-bon, 1371) write the name phonographically in man'yōgana (とりなるみ). Kanji 鳥鳴海 is an Edo-period editorial assignment by Motoori Norinaga (1798) and later editors; it is conventional, not original.

Torinarumi no Kami appears as a character in anime, manga, or games like Naruto, Okami, or Shin Megami Tensei. — No verified appearance in major anime/manga/games as of 2024. Watatsumi, Ōyamatsumi, and related kami appear, but Torinarumi does not. Fan wikis and demon-list videos sometimes invent stats for him, but these are not official Atlus/Capcom/Type-Moon content.
