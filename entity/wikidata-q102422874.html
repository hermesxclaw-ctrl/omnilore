Who It Is

Imagine a monster so vast that his head brushed the stars, whose hundred serpent heads each spoke a different terrifying sound—bull, lion, dog, hiss—and whose very presence threatened to unmake the order of the cosmos. This was Typhon, the last and greatest challenge to Zeus's rule, a being born of Earth's fury and Tartarean depths, whose defeat secured the reign of the Olympian gods and whose legacy echoes in every monster that ever haunted Greek myth.

Typhon is portrayed as a force of chaotic destruction, 'terrible, outrageous and lawless' (Hesiod). He is motivated by a desire to overthrow Zeus and rule the cosmos. His behavior is characterized by extreme violence and a cacophony of terrifying sounds.

Powers
Superhuman Strength and Size

Typhon surpassed all offspring of Earth in size and strength; his head brushed the stars, and his hands reached from east to west.

Limit: None specified, but he was ultimately defeated by Zeus's thunderbolts.

Fire and Storm Generation

Typhon could emit fire from his eyes and heads, and was associated with destructive storms and volcanic activity.

Limit: His fires were quenched by Zeus's thunderbolts.

Multiple Heads and Voices

He had a hundred (or more) heads that produced various terrifying sounds—bull, lion, dog, hiss—which confused and terrified enemies.

Limit: None specified.

Wings and Flight

His body was winged, allowing him to fly.

Limit: None.

Serpentine Coils

From the thighs downward he had huge coils of vipers that hissed loudly.

Limit: None.

Regeneration or Resilience

Some sources imply that Typhon was nearly invincible, but he was eventually overcome.

Limit: Vulnerable to Zeus's thunderbolts.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Typhon's appearance varies significantly across sources. Hesiod describes him as a monstrous giant with a hundred snake heads on his shoulders, each emitting fire and diverse sounds. Apollodorus gives him a human form above the waist, with snake coils below, wings, and fire flashing from his eyes. Nonnus elaborates with multiple animal heads (leopards, lions, bulls, boars, bears, wolves, dogs) and two hundred hands. Earlier art, like the Chalcidi

Symbols — Typhon is often depicted with serpentine features, wings, and multiple heads. In art, he may be shown as a giant with snake legs or a hundred-headed monster. He is associated with volcanic activity, particularly Mount Etna, and with destructive storms.

Domains — Typhon's birth and dwelling are often placed in Cilicia, near the Corycian cave. After his defeat, he is imprisoned under Mount Etna or the island of Ischia, where his movements cause volcanic eruptions and earthquakes.

Accounts I
The Birth of TyphonHesiod, Theogony; Homeric Hymn to Apollo; Apollodorus; b scholia to Iliad

Archaic to Roman

Typhon's birth is narrated in several conflicting versions. Hesiod says he was the son of Gaia and Tartarus, born after the Titanomachy. The Homeric Hymn to Apollo has Hera bear him alone, in anger at Zeus. A scholion to the Iliad says Cronus gave Hera eggs that produced Typhon. In all cases, Typhon is born to challenge Zeus. Typhon's birth sets the stage for his rebellion against Zeus.

Accounts II
The Battle with ZeusHesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus; Nonnus; Pindar

Archaic to Late Antiquity

Typhon attacked Olympus, causing the gods to flee to Egypt (in some accounts). Zeus fought him with thunderbolts, and after a fierce struggle, Typhon was defeated and cast into Tartarus or buried under Mount Etna. Zeus's supremacy was secured; Typhon became a source of volcanic activity.

Typhon's OffspringHesiod, Theogony; Apollodorus; Hyginus; others

Archaic to Roman

With Echidna, Typhon fathered many famous monsters, including Cerberus, Orthrus, the Lernaean Hydra, the Chimera, the Sphinx, the Nemean Lion, and others. These creatures became antagonists in later heroic myths. The monsters terrorized Greece until heroes like Heracles and Theseus defeated them.

Typhon is a giant like the Giants who fought the Olympians. — Typhon is distinct from the Giants. He is a separate monstrous offspring of Gaia and Tartarus, not one of the Giants who were born from Uranus's blood.

Typhon is the same as the Titan Typhoeus. — Typhon and Typhoeus are the same entity; the names are interchangeable.

Typhon is a storm god in the modern sense. — Typhon is a monstrous being associated with storms and volcanic eruptions, but he is not a personification of storms like Zeus or Aeolus.

Typhon was defeated easily by Zeus. — The battle was cataclysmic and in some versions Typhon initially had the upper hand, cutting Zeus's sinews.
