Who It Is

Imagine a sky where the moon isn't a dead rock, but a man — a hunter, a brother, a fugitive — forever chasing the woman he wronged. That's the world of Alignak, the Inuit moon god, whose story isn't about gentle moonlight, but about the burning shame of a broken taboo, the cold ache of eternal loneliness, and the harsh justice of the Arctic night. He is the reason the moon waxes and wanes (he's hungry when it's thin, well-fed when it's full), the reason the stars are scattered (he spilled his torch in his frantic flight), and the reason the tides obey the night sky. He is a protector of orphans and a punisher of wrongdoers, a source of shamanic power and a terrifying figure of cosmic justice. His story is one of the most powerful and haunting in world mythology, a tale that explains the very order of the heavens while reminding us of the human cost of our deepest failures.

Alignak is a solitary, melancholic figure. He is eternally chasing his sister, the sun, but never catches her. This pursuit is not malicious in all tellings; in some, it is a longing for home and family that he can never reclaim. He is a hunter, and his behavior in the sky (the waxing and waning of the moon) is tied to his hunting exploits. When the moon is full, he is well-fed; when it is new, he is starving. He is a protector of orphans and the vulnerable, but also a figure of stern justice, punishing those who break taboos, especially regarding women's roles and the treatment of ani

Powers
Lunar Control

Alignak governs the phases of the moon. His actions in the sky (hunting, resting) directly cause the waxing and waning. A full moon means he is successful and powerful; a new moon means he is weak and hungry.

Limit: He is bound to his cycle; he cannot break free of the chase after his sister.

Tide Mastery

He controls the tides, pulling the ocean with his lunar power. This gives him influence over the sea, which is crucial for hunting and survival.

Limit: His control is tied to his lunar cycle.

Weather Manipulation

He can bring storms, especially winter blizzards, and is believed to control the cold. He is a bringer of both life-sustaining snow (for igloos) and deadly storms.

Limit: Unknown; he is a sky god, so his power over weather is significant.

Psychopomp / Guide of the Dead

He guides the souls of those who died by violence or drowning to the afterlife. He is a protector of the dead who did not receive a proper burial.

Limit: He only guides a specific subset of the dead; others go to Sedna's realm or other afterlives.

Shamanic Patron

He is a source of power for shamans. They can travel to him in their spirit journeys to gain knowledge, healing power, and protection.

Limit: Only trained shamans can access this power.

Divine Justice

He punishes those who break taboos, particularly those related to incest, murder, and mistreatment of animals. He is a stern enforcer of cosmic law.

Limit: His justice is often seen as harsh and unforgiving.

Hunter's Patron

As a celestial hunter, he blesses hunters on Earth with success. His dog-sled is a model for the ideal hunting team.

Limit: His blessing is conditional on the hunter respecting the animals and the taboos.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Alignak is typically described as a powerful young man, often a hunter, dressed in furs. In the sky, he appears as the moon itself. The dark spots on the moon are his house, or the remnants of his camp, or his dog-sled and team. In some accounts, he carries a torch (the moon's light) that he holds to light his way; in others, he is the torch. His face is sometimes said to be visible in the moon's craters, looking down on Earth.

Symbols — The moon (full, crescent, and new phases). The crescent moon is often interpreted as his hunting bow. The dog-sled and his team of dogs are his primary symbols, visible in the lunar maria. The torch or lamp is another symbol, representing his light and his role as a guide in the darkness. In shamanic traditions, the moon is a source of shamanic power and a gateway to the spirit world.

Domains — The moon, the night sky. He is the master of the tides and is believed to control the weather, particularly the winter storms. He also governs the realm of the dead in some traditions, acting as a psychopomp who guides souls of those who died a violent death or by drowning. His earthly home is the sky, but he is intimately connected to the sea and the ice.

Accounts I
The Chase of the Sun and MoonCollected by Franz Boas (1888) and Knud Rasmussen (early 20th century) in the Central Arctic.

Pre-contact; documented in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

In a communal snow house, a young woman (Malina) is secretly visited by a man at night. She suspects it is her brother, Alignak. To identify him, she smears soot on her hands and wipes it on his face. The next night, she sees the soot on her brother's face and realizes the truth. Horrified and ashamed, she flees the house. Alignak chases her. She grabs a torch (or a piece of moss) and runs into the sky, becoming the sun. Alignak grabs another torch, but he trips and spills the embers, which become the stars. He becomes the moon. He chases her across the sky, but she is always ahead of him. He grows hungry and weak as he chases, which is why the moon wanes. When he rests and eats, he grows full again, which is why the moon waxes. They are separated forever. The sun's warmth and light are a constant reminder of the sister's flight. The moon's cold light is a reminder of the brother's guilt and longing. The stars are the spilled embers of his torch. The myth explains the celestial order and the origin of the incest taboo.

Accounts II
Alignak, the Shaman, and the Moon LandingCollected by Knud Rasmussen in 'Intellectual Culture of the Copper Eskimos' (1932).

Pre-contact; documented in the early 20th century.

A powerful shaman is taken to the moon by a spirit. He meets Alignak, who is a great hunter. Alignak shows the shaman his house, which is made of the bones of a giant whale. He teaches the shaman songs and rituals that will help his people. The shaman returns to Earth with this knowledge, becoming an even more powerful angakkuq. This story establishes Alignak as a source of shamanic power and a teacher of sacred knowledge. The shaman gains immense power. The story reinforces the importance of the moon as a spiritual realm.

The Moon, the Sun, and the EclipseVarious oral traditions across the Arctic.

Pre-contact; documented in the 19th and 20th centuries.

During an eclipse, the people believe that Alignak has descended to Earth to visit his sister, or that he is being attacked by a monstrous being. To protect him, the people make noise, beating drums and pots, and shout to scare away the evil spirit. They also believe that during the eclipse, the moon's shadow is a sign that Alignak is angry or that a taboo has been broken. The eclipse is a time of fear and ritual action. The people's noise is believed to protect the moon and restore its light.

Alignak is the 'God of the Moon' in the same way Zeus is the 'God of the Sky'. — Inuit cosmology is not a pantheon of gods with clear domains. Alignak is a powerful being, but he is not all-powerful. He is subject to the same natural laws (hunger, fatigue) as humans. He is a 'spirit' or 'deity' (inua) of the moon, not a ruler of it.

The Inuit worship the moon as a god. — The Inuit do not 'worship' Alignak in a formal sense. They respect him, fear him, and interact with him through rituals and taboos. He is a force to be acknowledged, not a god to be praised. The relationship is more like a reciprocal agreement with a powerful spirit.

The sun and moon myth is a simple story of 'why the sun is bright and the moon is dim'. — The myth is a complex narrative about incest, shame, justice, and the origin of the cosmos. The brightness of the sun and the dimness of the moon are just one detail. The myth is a foundational social and moral story.

Alignak is a demon or evil spirit. — Alignak is not inherently evil. He is a complex figure who can be both a protector and a punisher. His role in the myth is tragic, not demonic.
