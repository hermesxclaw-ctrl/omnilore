Who It Is

In the thunderous heart of the Baltic skies, Perkūnas rides his chariot, wielding an axe that splits the clouds and the moon itself. He is the enforcer of cosmic order, the punisher of broken oaths, and the guardian of the sacred oak. To the ancient Balts, his roar was not mere weather—it was the voice of divine justice, a force that could shatter both stone and sin.

As god of thunder, he is powerful, wrathful, and just. He enforces cosmic order, punishes evil, and protects moral law. He is also associated with fertility and creative forces.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name's etymology is debated. Proposed origins include: (1) from PIE *perkʷ- 'oak', meaning 'Lord of Oaks'; (2) from PIE *per- 'to strike, beat', meaning 'Striker'; (3) a Slavic borrowing from Middle Belarusian perun 'lightning'. Pronunciation — Per-KOO-nahs (Lithuanian); PYAIR-kohns (Latvian)

Aliases — Perkūnas (Lithuanian), Pērkons (Latvian), Perkūns, Perkunos (Old Prussian), Parkuns (Sudovian), Pārkiuņs (Latgalian), Pjarkus (Sudovian, attested)

Ownership — Common Baltic god of thunder, second in importance to Dievas. Worshipped by ancient Baltic tribes: Lithuanians, Latvians, Old Prussians, Sudovians, Latgalians.

Powers
Thunder and Lightning

He controls thunder, lightning, and storms. He uses lightning as a weapon to strike enemies and punish wrongdoers.

Limit: None specified in sources.

Divine Justice

He punishes moral transgressions, such as the Moon's adultery, by splitting it in half with his sword.

Limit: He acts within the cosmic order.

Fertility and Creation

As a god of fertility and creative forces, he brings rain for crops and is associated with vegetative growth.

Limit: None.

He is a god of war, granting courage and success to warriors.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Pictured as middle-aged, armed with an axe and arrows, riding a two-wheeled chariot harnessed with goats. In other accounts, drives a fiery chariot with swift horses, or rides a fiery horse.

Symbols — Oak trees are sacred to him. His weapons are the axe and arrows (lightning bolts). The chariot drawn by goats is a key symbol, paralleling Thor and Taranis.

Domains — Sky, thunder, lightning, storms, rain, fire, war, law, order, fertility, mountains, and oak trees. He dwells in the sky.

Accounts I
The Heavenly Wedding and the Punishment of the MoonLithuanian folk songs

Traditional oral folklore, recorded 19th century

Saulė (the Sun) and Mėnulis (the Moon) are married. In one version, Saulė cheats on Perkūnas with Mėnulis, and Perkūnas splits Mėnulis in half with a sword. In a more popular version, Mėnulis cheats on Saulė with Aušrinė (the morning star) just after the wedding. Perkūnas punishes Mėnulis, but Mėnulis repeats the adultery and is punished again every month. This explains the phases of the moon. Though divorced, both Sun and Moon want to see their daughter Žemyna (the Earth). Explains the phases of the moon and why the Sun shines during the day and the Moon at night.

Accounts II
Perkūnas and the Goat SacrificeSudovian Book

16th century record

A ritual involving the sacrifice of a goat to Perkūnas (Parkuns) is described, indicating his role in fertility and agricultural rites. Ensures fertility and divine favor.

Perkūnas is the supreme god of the Baltic pantheon. — He is the second most important deity; Dievas is the supreme god.

Perkūnas and Perun are the same deity. — They are related but distinct deities; the linguistic connection is debated.

Perkūnas is only a god of thunder. — He is also associated with war, law, order, fertility, mountains, and oaks.
