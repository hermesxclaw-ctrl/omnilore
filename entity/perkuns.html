Perkūns

The Thunderer, The Striker, God of Lightning

also known asPerkūnas (Lithuanian)Pērkons (Latvian)Perkūns, Perkunos (Old Prussian)Parkuns (Sudovian)Pārkiuņs (Latgalian)Pjarkus (Sudovian, attested)
TypeThunder God / Warrior Deity
CultureBaltic (Latvian Perkūns, Lithuanian Perkūnas)
Who It Is
Form

Pictured as middle-aged, armed with an axe and arrows, riding a two-wheeled chariot harnessed with goats. In other accounts, drives a fiery chariot with swift horses, or rides a fiery horse.

Powers

Thunder and Lightning He controls thunder, lightning, and storms. He uses lightning as a weapon to strike enemies and punish wrongdoers. All Baltic traditions None specified in sources. None.

Divine Justice He punishes moral transgressions, such as the Moon's adultery, by splitting it in half with his sword. Lithuanian and Latvian mythology He acts within the cosmic order. In some versions, the Sun cheats on the Moon; in others, the Moon cheats on the Sun.

Fertility and Creation As a god of fertility and creative forces, he brings rain for crops and is associated with vegetative growth. General Baltic mythology None. None.

War and Victory He is a god of war, granting courage and success to warriors. General Baltic mythology None. None.

Accounts

Saulė (the Sun) and Mėnulis (the Moon) are married. In one version, Saulė cheats on Perkūnas with Mėnulis, and Perkūnas splits Mėnulis in half with a sword. In a more popular version, Mėnulis cheats on Saulė with Aušrinė (the morning star) just after the wedding. Perkūnas punishes Mėnulis, but Mėnulis repeats the adultery and is punished again every month. This explains the phases of the moon. Though divorced, both Sun and Moon want to see their daughter Žemyna (the Earth).

A ritual involving the sacrifice of a goat to Perkūnas (Parkuns) is described, indicating his role in fertility and agricultural rites.

Content Notes

Perkūnas splits the Moon in half with a sword as punishment for adultery, and this punishment is repeated monthly.

This is a mythological explanation for lunar phases, reflecting ancient views on crime and punishment.

Lithuanian folk songs

In Christian writings, Perkūnas is portrayed as a malicious spirit or demon.

This reflects the Christianization of the Baltic peoples and the demonization of pagan deities.

Chronicle of John Malalas, Jan Długosz

Scholarly Debates
Etymology of the name 'Perkūnas'
Derived from PIE *perkʷ- 'oak', meaning 'Lord of Oaks' ⇌ Derived from PIE *per- 'to strike', meaning 'Striker'
Evidence for A: Linguistic reconstruction of paradigm *pérku-Hō(n) 'having an oak tree' (Ostrowski).
Evidence for B: Fraenkel's etymology linking to verb per̃ti 'to beat, whip', with -k- as an extension.
Reconstruction of PIE god *Perkʷúh₃nos
A common PIE thunder god can be reconstructed from Perkūnas, Perun, Fjörgyn, Parjanya, etc. ⇌ No common PIE name for thunder god can be reconstructed; theonyms arose independently.
Evidence for A: Similarities between Baltic, Slavic, Germanic, and Indic forms (Mallory, Adams, Jackson).
Evidence for B: Linguistic differences in suffixes and roots; West and Łuczyński argue against reconstruction.
Perkūnas's marital relationship
Perkūnas is the husband of Saulė, and Mėnulis is the adulterer. ⇌ Saulė is married to Mėnulis, and Perkūnas is the punisher of Mėnulis's adultery.
Evidence for A: Some folk songs suggest Saulė cheats on Perkūnas with Mėnulis.
Evidence for B: More popular version: Mėnulis cheats on Saulė with Aušrinė.
What We Don't Know

Limited primary sources; most folklore collected in 19th century. Lack of archaeological evidence directly linking to Perkūnas. Many Latvian dainas untranslated. Detailed regional variations not fully documented. Modern reception is under-researched.

A way to hold it in your mind

In the thunderous heart of the Baltic skies, Perkūnas rides his chariot, wielding an axe that splits the clouds and the moon itself. He is the enforcer of cosmic order, the punisher of broken oaths, and the guardian of the sacred oak. To the ancient Balts, his roar was not mere weather—it was the voice of divine justice, a force that could shatter both stone and sin.
