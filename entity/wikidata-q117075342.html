This page is intentionally blank and ready for source-backed lore, versions, images, and connections. Atingkok is the lord of creation, the sky, and cosmic order. He presides over the beginning of time, the formation of the universe, and the establishment of natural and social laws. He is also associated with destiny and ultimate justice, as all things ultimately return to him. In the domestic sphere, he is honored in the Sanamahi cult, where he is invoked for household protection and prosperity.

Form

Atingkok is typically described as an anthropomorphic male deity, often depicted as an elderly or majestic figure with a white beard, symbolizing wisdom and eternity. In some iconographic representations, he is shown with multiple heads or with a radiant halo, reflecting his omniscience. He is often depicted seated in a meditative or benevolent posture, sometimes holding symbols of creation such as a conch shell (shankha), a discus (chakra), or a lotus. In the Lai Haraoba festival, his presence is invoked through ritual dance and music, and he is represented symbolically rather than through direct figural representation.

Powers

Creation of the Universe Atingkok is the sole creator of the universe. From the void, he brought forth the sky, the earth, the celestial bodies, and all living beings. He is often described as creating the world through his thought, word, or by using the primordial elements. Classical Meitei mythology as recorded in the Puya He does not create ex nihilo in all accounts; some versions suggest he shaped pre-existing primordial matter. Some texts attribute creation to a trinity or to the joint action of Atingkok and Leimarel Sidabi, while others emphasize his sole agency.

Omniscience and Omnipresence As the supreme deity, Atingkok is all-knowing and present everywhere. He perceives all events, thoughts, and actions across time. Throughout the Puya and ritual traditions He chooses not to intervene in every matter, allowing free will and the natural order to unfold. None significant; his omniscience is consistently affirmed.

Granting of Boons and Curses Atingkok can bestow blessings, powers, and prosperity upon mortals and deities alike. Conversely, he can impose curses or cosmic punishments for transgressions. Various myths and folktales His boons are typically conditional on moral behavior or ritual purity. The exact conditions for receiving boons vary between narratives.

Cosmic Order and Lawgiving Atingkok established the moral and natural laws of the universe, including the cycles of day and night, seasons, birth, and death. He is the ultimate judge who maintains cosmic balance. Puya texts and ritual tradition He delegates the daily enforcement of these laws to lesser deities and spirits. None significant.

Shape-shifting and Manifestation In some myths, Atingkok is able to assume various forms or manifest himself in different aspects to interact with the world, often taking the form of a wise man or an animal. Oral traditions and some Puya accounts Not a central aspect of his character; he usually acts from the celestial realm. The frequency and purpose of these manifestations vary across tellings.

Behavior & Habitat

Atingkok is portrayed as a wise, benevolent, and somewhat distant creator god. He is the prime mover who sets the cosmic order in motion and then withdraws, allowing his children and other deities to manage the world. He is characterized by patience, foresight, and a sense of cosmic justice. In the myths, he is not a capricious or wrathful deity but rather a thoughtful architect of existence. He tests his creations, particularly the first humans, and intervenes only when cosmic balance is threatened.

Accounts

In the beginning, there was only a vast, formless void. Atingkok, the self-existent lord, willed the universe into being. He first created the sky and the earth, separating them from the primordial waters. He then created the sun, the moon, and the stars to light the world. He fashioned the mountains, rivers, and forests, and filled them with animals and plants. Finally, he created the first human beings, often said to be a man and a woman, and breathed life into them. He established the cycles of nature and the laws of society, then withdrew to the celestial realm, leaving his son Sanamahi to guard the household and the welfare of humans.

Atingkok and Leimarel Sidabi had a son, Sanamahi, who became the most important household deity in Meitei religion. Sanamahi was born from the divine union of the sky father and the earth mother. He was given the duty of protecting the home and the family, and he is honored in every Meitei household with a dedicated shrine in the southwest corner of the main room. The birth of Sanamahi establishes the divine family structure and the link between the cosmic creator and the domestic sphere.

Atingkok created the first human beings, sometimes said to be a brother and sister or a couple, from clay or from wood. He breathed life into them and taught them the arts of civilization, including agriculture, weaving, and the use of fire. He also gave them the knowledge of the sacred rituals and the names of the deities, ensuring that they would remember their creator. In some versions, Atingkok created multiple pairs of humans in different regions, explaining the diversity of mankind.

Content Notes

From the 18th century onward, the Meitei kings gradually adopted Hinduism, and the traditional Sanamahist religion was marginalized. Temples were converted, and traditional practices were sometimes banned or absorbed into Hindu rituals. This led to a decline in the public worship of Atingkok and other traditional deities.

The process of Hinduization in Manipur is a complex topic, with some scholars viewing it as a peaceful synthesis and others as a form of cultural conquest. The revival of Sanamahism in the 20th century is a response to this historical marginalization.

Historical records, colonial accounts, academic studies

British colonial administrators and missionaries often described the Meitei religion as 'primitive animism' or 'devil worship', failing to recognize the sophisticated cosmology and ethical system centered on Atingkok. This misrepresentation contributed to the devaluation of the indigenous faith.

Postcolonial scholarship has critiqued these colonial narratives and sought to recover the complexity of indigenous traditions.

Colonial-era writings, missionary reports

Many Puya (ancient Meitei texts) were lost or destroyed during the periods of Hinduization and colonial rule. This has created significant gaps in our knowledge of Atingkok and the original Sanamahist theology.

The surviving Puya are a precious resource, but they are often fragmentary and have been subject to later editing and interpretation.

Scholarly Debates
Creator vs. Created
Atingkok is the sole, self-existent creator of the universe. ⇌ Atingkok himself was created or emanated from a higher principle (e.g., the primordial void or a supreme goddess).
Evidence for A: Many Puya texts and oral traditions describe Atingkok as the ultimate source.
Evidence for B: Some interpretations, particularly those influenced by later philosophical or Hindu thought, see Atingkok as a manifesta
Role of Leimarel Sidabi
Leimarel Sidabi is the consort and equal partner of Atingkok in creation. ⇌ Leimarel Sidabi is a secondary figure, created by Atingkok or as a later emanation.
Evidence for A: Some Puya and oral traditions emphasize the duality of sky and earth, with both being necessary for creation.
Evidence for B: Other texts focus on Atingkok's sole agency and describe Leimarel Sidabi as a later creation.
Identification with Sanamahi
Atingkok and Sanamahi are distinct deities, with Sanamahi as the son. ⇌ Sanamahi is a manifestation or aspect of Atingkok, and the two are essentially the same.
Evidence for A: The genealogical texts and the distinct ritual roles of the two deities support their separation.
Evidence for B: Some devotional and philosophical interpretations see them as one, emphasizing the unity of the divine.
What We Don't Know

The primary research gap is the limited availability of critical editions and English translations of the Puya texts. Most of the information on Atingkok comes from secondary sources and oral traditions, which are not always consistent. The exact dating of the Puya is uncertain, and the historical development of the Atingkok figure is not fully understood. Further research is needed on the relationship between Atingkok and other Meitei deities, particularly Leimarel Sidabi, and on the influence of Hinduism on the Sanamahist tradition. The visual iconography of Atingkok is also understudied, as there is no standardized depiction.

A way to hold it in your mind

Think of Atingkok as the ultimate 'world-builder' in a video game: he designs the entire universe, sets the rules, and then spawns in his children and the first humans to play it. He's the game master who trusts his players to make their own choices, only stepping in when the game is about to crash. He's the wise, somewhat distant grandfather of the Meitei pantheon, more interested in the big picture of cosmic order than in the day-to-day drama of mortal life.
