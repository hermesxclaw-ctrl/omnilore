Who It Is

Imagine a deity so specialized that her entire job description fits in four words: 'watches kids leave home.' Now imagine her coworker, whose job is 'watches kids come back.' No temples. No statues. No myths. Just two divine functionaries standing at the door, clocking in and out for eternity. The Romans had gods for everything — but Abeona and Adeona might be the most gloriously bureaucratic pair in any pantheon.

As indigitamenta, these goddesses were not worshipped with temples, festivals, or sacrifices. They were invoked ritually at specific moments — likely when a child first left the house and when they returned. Their 'behavior' is purely functional: they were believed to watch over the threshold, ensuring safe departure and safe return.

Powers
Safe Departure (Abeona)

Abeona's power was to ensure a child's safe passage when leaving the home. This was not a general travel blessing but specifically concerned the moment of egress — the act of stepping out of the domestic sphere.

Limit: Her power was apparently limited to the act of departure itself, not the entire journey. Other gods (like the Lares Viales, guardians of roads) covered travel beyond the threshold.

Safe Return (Adeona)

Adeona's power was to ensure a child's safe return to the home. She was the inverse of Abeona, guarding the moment of ingress.

Limit: Her power was limited to the moment of return, not the protection of the home itself (which was the domain of the Penates and Vesta).

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient artistic representations of Abeona or Adeona survive. No statues, frescoes, coins, or reliefs have been identified. Their appearance is entirely unknown — a rare case where a deity's function is documented but their form is a complete blank. They were likely never given individual anthropomorphic forms, existing instead as numina — abstract divine forces rather than personified gods.

Symbols — No known symbols. The closest association would be the Roman concept of the limen (threshold), which was itself considered sacred and guarded by the god Limus or the goddess Cardea. Abeona and Adeona may have been invoked alongside these threshold deities.

Domains — Abeona: protection of children leaving the home. Adeona: protection of children returning to the home. Together they formed a paired guardianship over the boundary between the domestic sphere and the outside world.

No myths, legends, or narratives involving Abeona or Adeona survive. They were purely functional deities — invoked in ritual, not celebrated in story. Unlike the great gods of the Roman pantheon, they had no genealogies, no love affairs, no epic deeds. Their obscurity is itself a data point: it tells us that Roman religion was not solely a matter of grand mythology but also of practical, everyday spiritual management.

Accounts II
Augustine's Polemic: The Indigitamenta as Pagan AbsurdityAugustine of Hippo, De Civitate Dei (City of God), Book 4

c. 413-426 CE

In his monumental work The City of God, written in the aftermath of the sack of Rome, Augustine of Hippo mounted a sustained philosophical attack on traditional Roman religion. Abeona and Adeona feature prominently in Book 4, Chapter 21, where Augustine ridicules the Roman practice of assigning a separate deity to every trivial human action. He sarcastically asks why Rome needs a goddess for departing (Abeona) and another for returning (Adeona) when a single god could handle both functions. For Augustine, the indigitamenta—the vast catalogue of minor Roman functional deities—represented the absurdity and theological bankruptcy of paganism: a religion so fragmented that it atomized divine power into thousands of specialized and often contradictory beings. This polemic, while hostile, is ironically the primary reason Abeona and Adeona are known to us today. Without Augustine's detailed and mocking cataloguing of the indigitamenta, these minor deities—along with dozens of others—would be completely lost to history.
