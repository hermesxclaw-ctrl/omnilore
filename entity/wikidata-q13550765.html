Form

No ancient artistic representations of Abeona or Adeona survive. No statues, frescoes, coins, or reliefs have been identified. Their appearance is entirely unknown — a rare case where a deity's function is documented but their form is a complete blank. They were likely never given individual anthropomorphic forms, existing instead as numina — abstract divine forces rather than personified gods.

Powers

Safe Departure (Abeona) Abeona's power was to ensure a child's safe passage when leaving the home. This was not a general travel blessing but specifically concerned the moment of egress — the act of stepping out of the domestic sphere. Roman indigitamenta, as reported by Varro and Augustine Her power was apparently limited to the act of departure itself, not the entire journey. Other gods (like the Lares Viales, guardians of roads) covered travel beyond the threshold. None recorded — the division of labor between departure and return gods seems to have been consistently reported.

Safe Return (Adeona) Adeona's power was to ensure a child's safe return to the home. She was the inverse of Abeona, guarding the moment of ingress. Roman indigitamenta, as reported by Varro and Augustine Her power was limited to the moment of return, not the protection of the home itself (which was the domain of the Penates and Vesta). None recorded.

Behavior & Habitat

As indigitamenta, these goddesses were not worshipped with temples, festivals, or sacrifices. They were invoked ritually at specific moments — likely when a child first left the house and when they returned. Their 'behavior' is purely functional: they were believed to watch over the threshold, ensuring safe departure and safe return.

Accounts

No myths, legends, or narratives involving Abeona or Adeona survive. They were purely functional deities — invoked in ritual, not celebrated in story. Unlike the great gods of the Roman pantheon, they had no genealogies, no love affairs, no epic deeds.

In his monumental work The City of God, written in the aftermath of the sack of Rome, Augustine of Hippo mounted a sustained philosophical attack on traditional Roman religion. Abeona and Adeona feature prominently in Book 4, Chapter 21, where Augustine ridicules the Roman practice of assigning a separate deity to every trivial human action. He sarcastically asks why Rome needs a goddess for departing (Abeona) and another for returning (Adeona) when a single god could handle both functions. For Augustine, the indigitamenta—the vast catalogue of minor Roman functional deities—represented the absurdity and theological bankruptcy of paganism: a religion so fragmented that it atomized divine power into thousands of specialized and often contradictory beings. This polemic, while hostile, is ironically the primary reason Abeona and Adeona are known to us today. Without Augustine's detailed and mocking cataloguing of the indigitamenta, these minor deities—along with dozens of others—would be completely lost to history. The passage thus serves a dual function: it preserves invaluable information about Roman religious practice while simultaneously working to discredit and dismantle that very tradition.

What We Don't Know

No archaeological evidence. No artistic depictions. No primary cultic inscriptions. The entire record rests on a handful of citations in Christian polemical works. The original Varro text is lost. The exact ritual formulas used to invoke these goddesses are unknown. Whether they were household deities or public cult figures is unclear. The full list of indigitamenta is fragmentary and may reflect Christian selection bias rather than Roman practice.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Roman religion were a modern tech company, the great gods (Jupiter, Mars, Venus) would be the executives with corner offices and billion-dollar budgets. The indigitamenta — deities like Abeona and Adeona — would be the mid-level managers with hyper-specific job titles: 'Director of Child Departure Safety' and 'Director of Child Return Safety.' They don't get invited to the board meetings, they don't have their names on the building, but they're in the org chart, and if you need them, you know exactly which extension to dial.
