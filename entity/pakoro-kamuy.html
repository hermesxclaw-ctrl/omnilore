Pakoro-kamuy is the fearsome Ainu god of smallpox and epidemic disease, one of the most dreaded deities in the pantheon. He is believed to walk invisibly among humans, carrying disease in his bag, and can only be appeased through specific rituals, offerings, and the avoidance of certain taboos.

The Approach

In the snow-bound world of the Ainu, where every mountain, river, and creature hums with divine presence, Pakoro-kamuy slinks through the shadows—a small, quick-witted deity of poverty and hunger, feared not for his power but for his insatiable appetite. He is the god who makes rice vanish from the pot and leaves families with empty bellies, a reminder that even the gods must sometimes struggle to survive.

Content advisory. No explicit dark material beyond themes of poverty, hunger, and deprivation. Some traditional tales may involve trickery or theft, but no graphic violence or sexual content is attested in available sources.
Identity

Pakoro-kamuy

Pronunciation pah-KOH-roh KAH-moo-ee (approximate; Ainu pronunciation may vary)

Known As

Pakoro-kamuiHakoro-kamuyHakoro-kamuiPakorokamuy

Etymology

The name is composed of 'pakoro' (possibly meaning 'to be poor' or 'poverty') and 'kamuy' (deity or spiritual being). Thus, Pakoro-kamuy is often interpreted as 'the deity of poverty' or 'the god of want.'

Cultural Ownership

Ainu people of Hokkaido, Japan, and the Russian Far East (Sakhalin, Kuril Islands)

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate Pakoro-kamuy with other minor deities of misfortune or with the more prominent trickster figure, though such identifications are not universally accepted. There is no consensus on whether Pakoro-kamuy is a distinct deity or a manifestation of a broader concept of poverty.

Description

Appearance

Pakoro-kamuy is often described as a small, unkempt deity, possibly with shabby clothing and a hungry look. Some tales depict him as a beggar or a starving child, reflecting his association with poverty. He may appear as a human, but also as a small animal, such as a mouse or a bird, to sneak into homes and steal food.

Symbols & Iconography

No known iconography; Pakoro-kamuy is not depicted in Ainu art or crafts. His symbols are those of poverty and hunger: empty pots, tattered clothes, and the act of eating.

Behaviour & Personality

Pakoro-kamuy is characterized by his insatiable hunger and his desperate, often cunning, attempts to obtain food. He is not malicious but is driven by need, and he may resort to trickery or theft. He is also associated with the spread of poverty and misfortune, though not out of spite but as a natural consequence of his presence. In some tales, he can be appeased or driven away by offerings or by the generosity of humans.

Domains & Habitats

Pakoro-kamuy dwells in the human world, particularly in and around homes, where food is prepared and stored. He may also inhabit places associated with want, such as barren fields or empty storehouses. As a kamuy, he is part of the spiritual landscape of the Ainu world, which includes both the human realm and the realm of the gods.

Version Differences

Regional variations may exist in the name and specific tales, but due to limited sources, these are not well-documented. Some versions may emphasize his role as a bringer of poverty, while others focus on his personal hunger and the humorous or pitiable ways he tries to satisfy it.

Induce poverty

Pakoro-kamuy is believed to bring poverty and want to households he visits. His presence is said to cause food to spoil, crops to fail, and wealth to disappear.

His influence may be countered by proper rituals, offerings, or the generosity of the household.

Some tales suggest that he does not actively cause poverty but merely appears where poverty already exists.

Stories & Myths

The Hungry God

Source Ainu oral tradition (uwepeker)Culture AinuPeriod Unknown (pre-modern)

Summary

A poor family repeatedly finds that their food disappears, and they suspect a thief. They set a trap and catch a small, ragged deity, who identifies himself as Pakoro-kamuy. He explains that he is always hungry and cannot help but steal food. The family, taking pity on him, offers him a meal, and he leaves, blessing them with good fortune.

Participants

Pakoro-kamuy

A poor Ainu family

Consequences

The family's generosity is rewarded, and Pakoro-kamuy ceases to trouble them.

Variants

In some versions, the family drives him away with curses or by burning something, and he never returns.

The God of Poverty Visits a Rich House

Source Ainu oral traditionCulture AinuPeriod Unknown

Summary

Pakoro-kamuy visits a wealthy household, hoping to share in their abundance. The rich family, however, is stingy and refuses him food. In anger, he curses the household, causing their wealth to dwindle and their storehouses to empty. The family eventually becomes poor, and only then do they learn to be generous.

Participants

Pakoro-kamuy

A wealthy Ainu family

Consequences

The rich family loses their wealth, teaching them the value of generosity.

Variants

Some versions have the family repenting and making offerings, after which their fortune is restored.

Poverty and hunger

Source Ainu oral tradition

Description

Pakoro-kamuy represents the harsh realities of poverty and hunger that were common in traditional Ainu society, especially during harsh winters or times of scarcity. His tales reflect the anxiety of not having enough to eat.

Scholarly Context

Ethnographic studies of Ainu life indicate that food scarcity was a real concern, and deities like Pakoro-kamuy likely served to explain and personify these fears.

Theft

Source Ainu tales

Description

In some tales, Pakoro-kamuy steals food from humans, which could be seen as a violation of social norms. However, his actions are driven by necessity, not malice.

Scholarly Context

Theft in Ainu society was generally condemned, but tales of deities stealing may have served as moral lessons about generosity and the consequences of greed.

Cultural Significance

Pakoro-kamuy reflects the Ainu understanding of disease as a spiritual phenomenon with moral causes. The devastating smallpox epidemics brought by Japanese settlers in the 18th and 19th centuries were interpreted by many Ainu as Pakoro-kamuy's punishment for the breakdown of traditional ways. This contributed to a complex and often tragic relationship with colonial authorities. Traditional Ainu medicine combined herbal remedies with elaborate rituals designed to appease Pakoro-kamuy.
