Who It Is

In the vast, crowded pantheon of ancient Rome, there was a goddess with a single, silent job: she fed you before you were born. Her name was Alemona, and she was the divine equivalent of a prenatal nutritionist — a specialized functionary in a religious system that believed in dividing every task of life, no matter how small, among a vast army of hyper-specific deities. While the Greeks told grand stories of Zeus and Athena, the Romans quietly worshipped dozens of gods whose entire existence was a single, narrow function: gods of the door hinge, gods of the first cry of a newborn, gods of the middle of a field. Alemona is one of the most extreme examples of this Roman obsession with divine specialization. She had no myths, no temples that we know of, no statues, and no personality. Alemona's 'behavior' is entirely functional: she is a divine force that nourishes the fetus in the womb. She does not act in myths, does not intervene in the lives of heroes, and does not have a personality. Her 'behavior' is her function. She is one of a series of deities who collectively oversaw the entire process of pregnancy and infancy, each with a specific, narrow task. Powers
Fetal Nourishment

Alemona's sole power is to nourish the fetus in the womb. This is not a metaphorical or poetic function; it is a literal, physical one. The Romans believed that the fetus needed divine assistance to receive nourishment, and Alemona was the deity who provided it. This power is attested by Tertullian and Augustine, and implied by her name's etymology.

Limit: Alemona's power is strictly limited to the prenatal period. She does not protect the mother, does not ensure a safe birth, and does not nourish the infant after birth. Those functions belong to other deities. Her power is also entirely passive — she does not act, but rather is invoked to provide a constant, ongoing function. She cannot be 'summoned' for a specific crisis; she is a background process, not a miracle-worker.

Form & Signs

Appearance — No ancient description of Alemona's appearance survives. She is never depicted in any known surviving work of art, coin, or relief. This is typical of the Roman 'Sondergötter' — they were invoked by name in prayer and ritual, but they did not have developed iconographies or myths. They were, in essence, names attached to functions. Any artistic depiction of Alemona would be a modern invention, and no such depiction is known to exist.

Symbols — No symbols are attested for Alemona. She has no known animal, plant, or object associated with her. In the absence of any iconographic tradition, the only 'symbol' that can be associated with her is the concept of nourishment itself — the womb, the umbilical cord, the fetus. N/A

Alemona has no myths. She is not mentioned in any surviving work of poetry, drama, or fiction. She appears only in antiquarian lists of deities and in Christian polemics against paganism. This is not a gap in our sources — it is a reflection of her nature. The Roman 'Sondergötter' were not mythic figures; they were functional ones. They were invoked in prayer, but they did not have stories. The absence of myths is itself a significant piece of evidence. It shows that Roman religion had a stratum of deities who were purely functional, with no narrative identity. This is a key difference between Roman and Greek religion: the Greeks mythologized everything, while the Romans were content to have nameless, storyless functions personified as deities.
