The Approach

In the shadowed courts of the underworld, where souls are weighed and judged, there stands a creature unlike any other—a chimera with the head of a tiger, the body of a dragon, and ears that can hear the truth of every being across the universe. This is Diting, the loyal steed of the bodhisattva Kṣitigarbha, a being whose single horn pierces the veil of deception and whose very presence guards the gates of hell.

Identity

Diting

Pronunciation Dìtīng (dee-ting), with the third tone on 'di' and the first tone on 'ting'.

Known As

谛听DìtīngĐế ThínhTi听Earth ListeningEarth Listener

Etymology

The name 'Diting' is composed of two Chinese characters: 谛 (dì) meaning 'truth' or 'to examine closely' and 听 (tīng) meaning 'to listen'. Thus, the name can be interpreted as 'one who listens to the truth' or 'truth-hearer'. In Vietnamese, it is rendered as Đế Thính.

Cultural Ownership

Diting originates from Chinese Buddhism, specifically as the steed of Kṣitigarbha (Dizang Wang Pusa). It is also venerated in Vietnamese Buddhism as Đế Thính. The creature has become a part of broader East Asian folklore and appears in Chinese classical literature such as 'Journey to the West'.

Disputed Identifications

Some sources conflate Diting with other mythical creatures, such as the 'hou' (monkey) or 'xiezhi' (a unicorn-like beast that judges truth). However, Diting's unique combination of features and its specific association with Kṣitigarbha distinguish it. There is no major scholarly dispute over its identity, but its exact form varies in different artistic depictions.

Description

Appearance

Diting is described as a composite creature with the head of a tiger, the body of a dragon, the tail of a lion, a single horn, dog-like ears, and feet like a qilin. Its body is often depicted with scales and a flowing mane. In some modern depictions, such as in the manhua 'Fabulous Beasts', Diting is shown as a wolf-like creature with black fur, except for white ears and a white spinal ridge in quadrupedal form.

Symbols & Iconography

Diting is often depicted in Buddhist art as a small, lion-like beast with a single horn, sometimes shown alongside Kṣitigarbha. It symbolizes the ability to hear and discern the truth, and its horn is sometimes interpreted as a tool to receive information from across the universe.

Behaviour & Personality

Diting is portrayed as loyal, protective, and wise. It acts as a guardian and companion to Kṣitigarbha, and in the Journey to the West, it shows prudence by not revealing the truth to avoid conflict. In modern media, it is depicted as a responsible and somewhat bureaucratic figure, as the CEO of the Underworld.

Domains & Habitats

Diting resides in the underworld (Diyu) with Kṣitigarbha, where it guards the souls of the dead. It can also travel between realms, as seen in its ability to discern beings across the universe.

Version Differences

The traditional version describes Diting as a composite beast, while the legend of the white dog suggests it was originally a dog. In modern adaptations, Diting may have a more anthropomorphic or wolf-like appearance. The horn is sometimes omitted in later depictions.

Stories & Myths

The Legend of the White Dog

Source Popular Buddhist legendCulture Chinese, KoreanPeriod Tang dynasty (7th-9th century)

Summary

Before Kṣitigarbha left heaven, he learned that his mother from a past life would be reborn as a dog. He was reincarnated as Kim Gyo-gak, a prince of Silla, who later traced the dog and adopted it. The dog became his companion and followed him to Mount Jiuhua, where he became a monk. The dog acted as his guard and, upon Kṣitigarbha's enlightenment, became Diting, the guardian of hell.

Participants

Kṣitigarbha

Kim Gyo-gak

The white dog

Consequences

The dog became Diting, a divine creature, and Kṣitigarbha fulfilled his vow to save all beings from hell.

Variants

Some versions say the dog was originally a divine beast, not a reincarnated mother.

The True and False Monkey King

Source Journey to the West, Chapter 58Culture ChinesePeriod Ming dynasty (16th century)

Summary

When the real and fake Sun Wukong argued, they sought help from Kṣitigarbha, who referred them to Diting. Diting identified the true monkey but, fearing the fake monkey would cause havoc in his abode, advised sending them to Buddha.
