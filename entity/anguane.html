Who It Is

In the misty valleys of the Veneto, where the rivers run cold and the forests crowd the slopes, the Anguane are said to lurk—beautiful, elusive, and dangerous. These water spirits of northern Italy are neither wholly benign nor wholly malevolent; they are tricksters of the streams, guardians of hidden springs, and sometimes, the abductors of unwary travelers.

Anguane are capricious and unpredictable. They are known to be playful, sometimes helping humans by teaching them songs or weaving, but they can also be vindictive, leading travelers astray, drowning people, or stealing children. They are often associated with the protection of natural springs and may punish those who pollute or disrespect these waters. Their behavior is not consistently malevolent; they may reward those who show them kindness, but they are always dangerous to cross.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name 'Anguana' is likely derived from the Latin 'anguis' (snake), reflecting a possible serpentine origin or association with water snakes. Some scholars also link it to the Celtic 'an-ghuana' or a pre-Roman substrate, but these are speculative.

Pronunciation — /ænˈɡwɑːneɪ/ (Italian: [aŋˈɡwaːne])

Aliases — Anguana, Anguanes, Anguane (plural), Anguane (Venetian), Anguane (Friulian), Anguane (Lombard)

Ownership — Italian (specifically the regions of Veneto, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, and Lombardy)

Powers
Shape-shifting

Anguane are said to be able to transform into animals, particularly snakes, or to appear as beautiful women to lure humans. They may also change their appearance to appear old or young.

Limit: They may be bound to their watery domain and cannot stay on land for extended periods.

Enchantment and seduction

They possess a mesmerizing voice and beauty that can bewitch humans, leading them into the water or into the forest. Their songs are said to be irresistible.

Limit: Those who are aware of their nature may resist, and certain charms or religious symbols may protect against them.

Control over water

They can command rivers and springs, causing floods or droughts. They may also create whirlpools or mist to disorient travelers.

Limit: Their power is limited to their own body of water; they lose power if separated from it.

Cursing and blessing

They can bestow good fortune or misfortune upon humans, depending on how they are treated. They may curse a family for generations or bless a village with abundant water.

Limit: Their curses can be lifted by certain rituals or by making amends.

Abduction of children

In some tales, they steal human children, replacing them with changelings or taking them to their underwater realm. They may also lure children into the water to drown.

Limit: Children who are baptized or protected by charms are safe.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Anguane are typically described as beautiful young women with long, flowing hair, often dark or golden. They are sometimes depicted with webbed feet or a fish-like tail, though this varies by region. In some accounts, they appear as fully human, while in others they have subtle animal features, such as a hint of scales or a tendency to vanish into water. They are often dressed in white or green, blending with their aquatic surroundings.

Symbols — Water, rivers, springs, and caves are the primary symbols associated with Anguane. They are also linked to snakes, due to the etymology of their name, and to the moon, as they are often active at night. In art and literature, they are depicted as seductive figures, sometimes with a comb or mirror, reminiscent of classical water nymphs.

Domains — They inhabit freshwater environments: rivers, streams, lakes, ponds, and springs, particularly in mountainous or forested areas. They are also said to dwell in caves near water, and some legends place them in underground chambers or grottos. They are most active at dawn, dusk, and night, and are especially powerful during certain lunar phases.

Accounts I
The Anguana and the ShepherdOral tradition from the Veneto

Unknown (collected in the 19th-20th century)

A young shepherd encounters an Anguana by a stream. She asks him for a comb, and he gives her his own. In gratitude, she teaches him a magical song that makes his sheep produce more milk. However, she warns him never to tell anyone how he learned the song. When he breaks his promise, the Anguana disappears, and his good fortune ends. The shepherd loses his fortune and the Anguana never returns.

Accounts II
The Anguane of the Friulian LakesOral tradition from Friuli

Unknown (collected in the 19th-20th century)

In a lake in Friuli, a group of Anguane is said to dwell. They are known to lure fishermen with their singing, causing them to fall into the water and drown. A local hero, however, manages to capture one Anguana and forces her to reveal the location of a hidden treasure. She complies but curses him, saying that his family will never prosper. The hero becomes rich, but his descendants suffer misfortune for generations. The hero gains wealth but his family is cursed.

The Anguana's CaveOral tradition from Lombardy

Unknown (collected in the 19th-20th century)

A cave near a river is said to be the home of an Anguana. A woodcutter, lost in the forest, stumbles upon the cave and sees the Anguana weaving a garland of flowers. She invites him in and offers him food. He eats, and she tells him he may stay as long as he wishes, but he must never leave without her permission. He stays for what seems like a year, but when he finally leaves, he finds that decades have passed. He returns to his village an old man, and the Anguana is never seen again. The woodcutter loses his youth and his former life.
