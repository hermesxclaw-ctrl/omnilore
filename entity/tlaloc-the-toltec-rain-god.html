Who They Are

Tlaloc was the rain god of the Toltec civilization, inherited from earlier Teotihuacan and later passed to the Aztecs. The Toltec version of Tlaloc was the god of the Tula mountains — providing the rain that made the fertile valley productive. The famous 'Chac Mool' figures at Tula are associated with Tlaloc's cult, representing intermediaries who received offerings for the rain god.

The Approach

In the rarefied air of Cerro Tláloc, where clouds gather and thunder rolls, the ancient Mexica believed the rain god himself dwelt. Tláloc, whose fanged visage and goggle eyes stared from temple walls, was no distant myth but a living presence—feared for his storms, cherished for his life-giving rain, and honored with offerings from jaguar skulls to human hearts. His legacy persists today, not as a relic of a vanished civilization, but as a vibrant thread woven into the fabric of Mexican identity.

Content advisory. This dossier discusses human sacrifice, including the ritual killing of children, as practiced by the Aztec and other Mesoamerican cultures in the worship of Tláloc. It also addresses colonial violence and the forced conversion of indigenous peoples. Readers sensitive to these topics should proceed with caution.

Etymology

The name Tláloc derives from Classical Nahuatl, likely a compound of 'tlālli' (earth) and 'octli' (a sacred fermented beverage), possibly meaning 'he who makes things sprout' or 'earth nectar.' Alternative etymologies suggest a link to 'tlālli' and 'ōcēlōtl' (jaguar), but this is debated.

Cultural Ownership

Tláloc is primarily an Aztec (Mexica) deity, but his worship was widespread in Mesoamerica, with antecedents at Teotihuacan and possible roots in Maya and Olmec cultures. He is a pan-Mesoamerican rain god, venerated by many Nahua peoples and others.

Disputed Identifications

There is considerable confusion in the identification of Tláloc in iconography. Many abstract or 'scary' representations have been mislabeled as Tláloc. His two main identifiers are fangs and ringed eyes, but even these can be ambiguous. The colossal statue at Coatlinchan may not be Tláloc but a female deity, possibly his sister. This confusion highlights the difficulty in distinguishing Tláloc from other deities and from his consorts.

Description

Appearance

Tláloc is depicted with distinctive goggle-like ringed eyes, a fanged mouth (often with two or three to four fangs), and a bifurcated tongue. His lips are often shaped like a mustache. He frequently carries a vessel with water, a lightning bolt wand, or a cornstalk. He may be shown with jaguar, serpent, owl, butterfly, or shell attributes. In some representations, he is a boulder or mountain.

Symbols & Iconography

Butterflies, jaguars, serpents, owls, water lilies, shells, spiders, the eye-of-the-reptile symbol, the Cross Venus symbol, lightning, maize, and water. The Mexican marigold (Tagetes lucida), known as cempohualxochitl, was burned as ritual incense.

Behaviour & Personality

Tláloc was a giver of life and sustenance, but also feared for his power over hail, thunder, lightning, and rain. He was not considered malicious but could be wrathful, sending storms and drought. He was associated with caves, springs, and mountains, and was believed to reside on sacred mountains, especially Cerro Tláloc.

Domains & Habitats

Rain, water, earthly fertility, lightning, thunder, hail, mountains, caves, springs. His primary shrine was on Cerro Tláloc; he was also worshipped at Teotihuacan and in the Templo Mayor at Tenochtitlan.

Version Differences

At Teotihuacan, Tláloc often has jaguar features, reflecting the association of thunder with jaguars. The Maya version (Chaac) has no specific jaguar relation. In the Aztec period, Tláloc is more anthropomorphic with clear fangs and goggles. Some representations may be of his consorts or other deities, leading to confusion.

Stories & Myths

The Rain God's Mountain

Source Archaeological evidence, colonial accountsCulture AztecPeriod Pre-Columbian

Summary

Tláloc resided on Cerro Tláloc, where his primary shrine was located. Pilgrimages were made to this mountain, and sacrifices, including human sacrifices, were offered there to ensure rain.

Participants

Tláloc

Aztec priests

Pilgrims

Consequences

The mountain became a sacred site and a focal point for rain rituals.

Variants

Similar mountaintop shrines to rain gods existed across Mesoamerica.

The Tlálocan Paradise

Source Florentine Codex, other codicesCulture AztecPeriod Post-conquest (recorded)

Summary

Tláloc ruled Tlálocan, a paradise for those who died from water-related causes, such as drowning, lightning, or water-borne diseases. This was a place of lush vegetation and eternal spring.

Participants

Tláloc

The dead

Consequences

Tláloc's realm provided an afterlife for many.

Variants

Some sources say Tlálocan was also for those who died in battle or childbirth, but this is debated.

Sacrifices at the Templo Mayor

Source Archaeological evidence, colonial accountsCulture AztecPeriod Late Postclassic

Summary

At the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, the Tláloc half of the pyramid received offerings, including jaguar skulls, a complete jaguar skeleton, and human sacrifices. Chacmools depicting Tláloc were used as receptacles for sacrificial blood and hearts.

Participants

Tláloc

Aztec priests

Sacrificial victims

Consequences

These rituals were believed to ensure rain and agricultural fertility.

Variants

Similar practices occurred at other sites.

The Coatlinchan Statue

Source Modern scholarshipCulture MexicanPeriod 1964 (relocation)

Summary

A colossal 168-ton statue found at Coatlinchan was thought to represent Tláloc and was moved to the National Museum of Anthropology in 1964. Some scholars now believe it may depict a female deity, perhaps Tláloc's sister.

Participants

Tláloc

Scholars

Coatlinchan residents

Consequences

The statue became a symbol of Mexican national identity, and a reproduction was erected in Coatlinchan, where residents continue to venerate it.

Variants

The identification is disputed.

Human sacrifice

Source Florentine Codex, Durán, archaeology

Description

At Cerro Tláloc and the Templo Mayor, human sacrifices were offered to Tláloc. Victims included children, whose tears were believed to bring rain, and warriors.

Scholarly Context

Human sacrifice was a central part of Aztec religion, and Tláloc was one of the deities who demanded such offerings.

Colonial erasure

Source Colonial chronicles, church records

Description

The Spanish sought to eradicate indigenous religions, destroying temples and sacred objects, and forcibly converting people to Christianity. Despite this, Tláloc worship survived in some forms.

Scholarly Context

This is part of the broader history of colonial violence and cultural suppression.

Mislabeling of artifacts

Source Modern scholarship

Description

The confusion over Tláloc's identity has led to mislabeling of artifacts, potentially erasing the identities of other deities.

Scholarly Context

This is a scholarly issue, not a dark historical practice, but it has implications for cultural heritage.

Research Scope

This dossier focuses on the Aztec deity Tláloc, with emphasis on his worship in the Valley of Mexico, particularly at Tenochtitlan and Cerro Tláloc. It draws on archaeological evidence, early colonial accounts, and modern scholarship. It does not exhaustively cover all Mesoamerican rain deities, but contextualizes Tláloc within broader Mesoamerican traditions.

Cultural Significance

The Toltec Tlaloc cult represented a continuation and expansion of the rain god tradition that began with the Olmec and flourished at Teotihuacan. The Chac Mool — first appearing at Tula — became one of the most widespread ritual objects in Mesoamerica, found from the Toltec heartland to Chichen Itza and the Aztec capital. The Toltec foundations of Tlaloc worship were directly inherited by the Aztecs, who regarded the Toltecs as the inventors of all proper religious practice. The temple of Tlaloc at Tula stood as a precedent for the Templo Mayor's Tlaloc shrine in Tenochtitlan.
