Who It Is

In the rarefied air of Cerro Tláloc, where clouds gather and thunder rolls, the ancient Mexica believed the rain god himself dwelt. Tláloc, whose fanged visage and goggle eyes stared from temple walls, was no distant myth but a living presence—feared for his storms, cherished for his life-giving rain, and honored with offerings from jaguar skulls to human hearts. His legacy persists today, not as a relic of a vanished civilization, but as a vibrant thread woven into the fabric of Mexican identity.

Tláloc was a giver of life and sustenance, but also feared for his power over hail, thunder, lightning, and rain. He was not considered malicious but could be wrathful, sending storms and drought. He was associated with caves, springs, and mountains, and was believed to reside on sacred mountains, especially Cerro Tláloc.

Name & Tongue

Etymology — The name Tláloc derives from Classical Nahuatl, likely a compound of 'tlālli' (earth) and 'octli' (a sacred fermented beverage), possibly meaning 'he who makes things sprout' or 'earth nectar.' Alternative etymologies suggest a link to 'tlālli' and 'ōcēlōtl' (jaguar), but this is debated.

Pronunciation — Classical Nahuatl: [ˈtɬaːlok]

Aliases — The Toltec Rain God, The Provider, God of Rain, Lord of the Rain

Powers
Rain Control

Tláloc controlled rain, deciding when and where it fell. He could send gentle rains for crops or destructive storms.

Limit: Could be supplicated through rituals and sacrifices.

Thunder and Lightning

He wielded lightning and thunder, which were seen as his tools or manifestations.

Limit: Not explicitly limited.

Fertility

He gave earthly fertility, ensuring abundant harvests and the growth of plants.

Limit: Dependent on proper worship.

Control over Hail

He could send hail, which could destroy crops, thus he was both beneficial and destructive.

Limit: Not specified.

He resided in mountains and could manifest as boulders or mountains themselves.

Limit: Not a power per se, but a trait.

Form & Signs

Appearance — Tláloc is depicted with distinctive goggle-like ringed eyes, a fanged mouth (often with two or three to four fangs), and a bifurcated tongue. His lips are often shaped like a mustache. He frequently carries a vessel with water, a lightning bolt wand, or a cornstalk. He may be shown with jaguar, serpent, owl, butterfly, or shell attributes. In some representations, he is a boulder or mountain.

Symbols — Butterflies, jaguars, serpents, owls, water lilies, shells, spiders, the eye-of-the-reptile symbol, the Cross Venus symbol, lightning, maize, and water. The Mexican marigold (Tagetes lucida), known as cempohualxochitl, was burned as ritual incense.

Domains — Rain, water, earthly fertility, lightning, thunder, hail, mountains, caves, springs. His primary shrine was on Cerro Tláloc; he was also worshipped at Teotihuacan and in the Templo Mayor at Tenochtitlan.

Accounts I
The Rain God's MountainArchaeological evidence, colonial accounts

Pre-Columbian

Tláloc resided on Cerro Tláloc, where his primary shrine was located. Pilgrimages were made to this mountain, and sacrifices, including human sacrifices, were offered there to ensure rain. The mountain became a sacred site and a focal point for rain rituals.

The Tlálocan ParadiseFlorentine Codex, other codices

Post-conquest (recorded)

Tláloc ruled Tlálocan, a paradise for those who died from water-related causes, such as drowning, lightning, or water-borne diseases. This was a place of lush vegetation and eternal spring. Tláloc's realm provided an afterlife for many.

Accounts II
Sacrifices at the Templo MayorArchaeological evidence, colonial accounts

Late Postclassic

At the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, the Tláloc half of the pyramid received offerings, including jaguar skulls, a complete jaguar skeleton, and human sacrifices. Chacmools depicting Tláloc were used as receptacles for sacrificial blood and hearts. These rituals were believed to ensure rain and agricultural fertility.

The Coatlinchan StatueModern scholarship

1964 (relocation)

A colossal 168-ton statue found at Coatlinchan was thought to represent Tláloc and was moved to the National Museum of Anthropology in 1964. Some scholars now believe it may depict a female deity, perhaps Tláloc's sister. The statue became a symbol of Mexican national identity, and a reproduction was erected in Coatlinchan, where residents continue to venerate it.

All pre-Hispanic cultures worshipped the same Tláloc. — While a rain god was widespread, each culture had its own name and variations.

Tláloc was only worshipped through human sacrifice. — Offerings included incense, food, flowers, and animal sacrifices, with human sacrifice reserved for major rituals.
