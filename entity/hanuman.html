Who It Is

Imagine a superhero who can leap an ocean in a single bound, rip open his chest to reveal his god inside his heart, and still insists he’s just a servant—that’s Hanuman, the divine monkey whose story has powered Indian imagination for over two millennia.

Across textual and folk traditions, Hanuman’s defining behavioral trait is unwavering bhakti (devotion) to Rāma, expressed through humility, service, and readiness for self-sacrifice. In the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa he is a shrewd diplomat and scout, capable of careful speech and strategic thinking; in Tulsīdās’s Rāmacaritamānasa he becomes the paradigmatic devotee whose every action is an offering. Folk narratives and modern retellings highlight his playful, childlike curiosity (such as mistaking the sun for a fruit) alongside fierce martial prowess in battle. Powers
Immense physical strength

Hanuman is repeatedly described as possessing extraordinary strength, capable of uprooting trees, defeating powerful demons, and carrying an entire mountain of medicinal herbs when he cannot identify the specific plant needed to heal Lakṣmaṇa. This strength is both martial and protective, invoked by devotees seeking resilience.

Limit: His strength is framed as subordinate to dharma and Rāma’s will; he does not act independently for personal gain. In some narratives, he initially forgets his own power until reminded by Jāmbavān, suggesting psychological rather than metaphysical limitation.

Flight and prodigious leaping

Hanuman’s leap across the ocean to Laṅkā is one of his most iconic feats, often interpreted as flight or superhuman jumping. As a child, he flies toward the sun, mistaking it for a fruit, demonstrating innate aerial mobility.

Limit: His leap is preceded by encouragement from allies, implying that confidence and remembrance of his boons are necessary. Ocean-crossing is presented as exceptional, not routine travel.

Shape-shifting and size alteration

Hanuman can change his size at will—expanding to gigantic proportions to intimidate enemies or shrinking to a tiny form to infiltrate Laṅkā and approach Sītā discreetly. This ability is central to his role as scout and warrior.

Limit: Size change is used strategically, not continuously; texts do not describe fixed limits, but his transformations are always in service of Rāma’s mission.

Devotional immunity and protection

Hanuman is often believed to grant protection from fear, evil spirits, and planetary afflictions to those who recite his hymns, especially the Hanumān Cālīsā. His own body is described as resistant to weapons and fire, surviving the burning of Laṅkā.

Limit: Protection is conditional on sincere devotion and disciplined practice (e.g., regular recitation, ritual purity). Texts do not present him as overriding all karmic consequences.

Knowledge, eloquence, and diplomacy

Hanuman is praised for his wise speech and ability to assess situations, particularly in his respectful yet firm dialogue with Sītā and his confrontations with Rāvaṇa. He serves as messenger and negotiator as much as warrior.

Limit: His counsel is always framed within loyalty to Rāma; he does not seek independent political power.

Burning of Laṅkā

After being captured and having his tail set on fire, Hanuman enlarges it and uses the flames to burn much of Laṅkā, demonstrating both resilience and destructive capacity.

Limit: The act is framed as punitive and strategic, not wanton destruction; he spares certain areas according to some versions, though details vary.

Immortality (cirañjīvī status)

Purāṇic traditions and popular belief regard Hanuman as one of the seven or eight immortals who continue to live in the world, available to devotees.

Limit: Immortality is spiritual and mythic; texts do not describe a continuous visible presence. It functions primarily as theological assurance of ongoing protection.

Accounts I
Hanuman’s miraculous birthPurāṇic narratives and later retellings; summarized in EBSCO Research Starter.

Classical to medieval, with ongoing vernacular elaboration

Hanuman is born to Añjanā, a celestial being cursed to take monkey form, and either the monkey warrior Keśarī or the wind god Vāyu. In some accounts, Vāyu carries divine essence to Añjanā, making Hanuman his spiritual son. His birth is accompanied by boons of strength, agility, and devotion, setting the stage for his role in Rāma’s story. Establishes Hanuman’s liminal identity as both vanara and divine being, and his connection to Vāyu, which later grounds his relationship to Bhīma in the Mahābhārata.

The child who tried to eat the sunPurāṇic and later narrative traditions; summarized in EBSCO Research Starter.

Classical to medieval, with enduring popular retellings

As a child, Hanuman, insatiably hungry, mistakes the rising sun for a giant fruit and leaps into the sky to seize it. The gods panic; Indra strikes him with the thunderbolt, injuring his jaw. Vāyu, enraged, withdraws the wind from the world, causing cosmic distress. The gods then grant Hanuman boons of strength and protection, restoring balance. Explains his name (linked to the jaw), his boons, and his special relationship to Vāyu. It also frames his power as divinely sanctioned yet needing guidance.

First meeting with Rāma and LakṣmaṇaVālmīki Rāmāyaṇa; Ludvik’s comparative analysis; Rāmacaritamānasa.

Classical Sanskrit epic; early modern vernacular epic

Hanuman, serving Sugrīva, encounters Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa in the forest. Disguised as a mendicant, he tests their intentions, then recognizes their divine nature. His eloquent speech wins their trust, and he becomes Rāma’s devoted servant and ally. Marks the beginning of Hanuman’s central role in the search for Sītā and the war against Rāvaṇa, and establishes his identity as ideal messenger and devotee.

Accounts II
The leap to Laṅkā and meeting with SītāVālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sundarakāṇḍa); Rāmacaritamānasa; Ludvik’s analysis.

Classical and early modern epic

Encouraged by Jāmbavān, Hanuman remembers his latent powers and leaps across the ocean to Laṅkā. Shrinking his form, he infiltrates the city, finds Sītā in the Aśoka grove, and delivers Rāma’s message and ring. He reassures her of rescue and offers to carry her away, but she refuses, insisting that Rāma must personally defeat Rāvaṇa. Demonstrates Hanuman’s courage, intelligence, and respect for Sītā’s agency. The episode becomes a central devotional focus, especially in Sundarakāṇḍa recitations.

Burning of LaṅkāVālmīki Rāmāyaṇa; Rāmacaritamānasa; Ludvik’s analysis.

Classical and early modern epic

Captured by Rāvaṇa’s forces, Hanuman’s tail is set ablaze as punishment. He enlarges it, escapes, and uses the fire to burn much of Laṅkā’s buildings, sowing chaos before returning to Rāma with news of Sītā. Weakens Laṅkā and showcases Hanuman’s destructive potential in service of justice. It becomes a vivid image in art and performance.

Bringing the mountain of medicinal herbsVālmīki Rāmāyaṇa; Rāmacaritamānasa; Ludvik’s analysis.

Classical and early modern epic

When Lakṣmaṇa is gravely wounded in battle, Hanuman is sent to fetch specific medicinal herbs from a distant mountain. Unable to identify them, he uproots and carries the entire mountain back, allowing the healers to find the needed plants and save Lakṣmaṇa. Symbolizes Hanuman’s practical ingenuity and refusal to be defeated by uncertainty. The image of him flying with the mountain is iconic in visual culture.

Bhīma’s encounter with HanumanMahābhārata (Vana Parva).

Classical Sanskrit epic

Bhīma, seeking a special flower for Draupadī, encounters an old monkey blocking his path. When he tries to move the monkey’s tail, he cannot, realizing the being’s immense strength. The monkey reveals himself as Hanuman, Bhīma’s half-brother through Vāyu, and blesses him. Connects Hanuman to the Mahābhārata world and reinforces his status as elder, wise, and powerful. It also underscores humility as a virtue even for strong warriors.
