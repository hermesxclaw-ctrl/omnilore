Who It Is

Hanuman is one of the most beloved, widely worshipped, and instantly recognizable deities across the Hindu world. He is a vanara (a divine monkey-like being), the son of the wind god Vayu and the celestial nymph Anjana, and the greatest devotee (bhakta) of Rama, the avatar of Vishnu. Hanuman's life story is detailed primarily in the Ramayana. As a child, he mistook the rising sun for a ripe fruit and leaped toward it to eat it, provoking Indra to strike him with a thunderbolt. In retaliation, Vayu withdrew the wind from the world, forcing the gods to placate both father and son with multiple divine boons that made Hanuman nearly invincible. His central role in the Ramayana begins when Rama seeks to rescue his abducted wife, Sita, from the demon king Ravana. Hanuman, as minister to the monkey king Sugriva, becomes Rama's most trusted ally. His greatest feats include: leaping across the ocean from India to Lanka (a distance of 100 yojanas); locating Sita in the Ashoka grove and delivering Rama's ring as a token of hope; allowing himself to be captured and his tail set ablaze, only to use it to burn down much of Ravana's golden city; and most famously, flying to the Himalayas to retrieve the entire Sanjeevani mountain when Lakshmana lay dying on the battlefield, unable to identify the specific healing herb. Hanuman represents the ideal of selfless devotion (bhakti), humility, immense physical and spiritual strength, and unwavering loyalty. He is a brahmachari (celibate) whose power derives from his purity and his complete absorption in Rama's name. In the Hanuman Chalisa (40 verses to Hanuman), composed by Tulsidas, he is extolled as the remover of obstacles, the destroyer of evil spirits, and the granter of courage and wisdom. Hanuman's iconography—often depicted tearing open his chest to reveal Rama and Sita enshrined in his heart—is one of the most potent images of devotional Hinduism.

Attested in Valmiki's Ramayana (c. 500–100 BCE) / Tulsidas' Ramcharitmanas (16th century CE)Era Treta Yuga (mythological time); Epic composition: 1st millennium BCE

Form

Attested depictions in temple sculpture, paintings, and performance describe Hanuman as a powerful vanara (monkey-being) with a humanoid body and simian face, often muscular, with broad shoulders and a long tail. He is frequently shown carrying a gadā (mace) and sometimes the entire mountain of medicinal herbs (Dronagiri) in one hand, symbolizing his strength and resourcefulness. Iconographically, he may be kneeling in reverence before Rāma and Sītā, tearing open his chest to reveal their images in his heart, or standing as a heroic guardian at temple gates. Regional styles vary—from more animal-like forms in some South Indian bronzes to highly anthropomorphic, almost superhero-like posters in modern North Indian devotional art—yet all emphasize vigor, agility, and devotion.

Powers

Immense physical strength Hanuman is repeatedly described as possessing extraordinary strength, capable of uprooting trees, defeating powerful demons, and carrying an entire mountain of medicinal herbs when he cannot identify the specific plant needed to heal Lakṣmaṇa. This strength is both martial and protective, invoked by devotees seeking resilience. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sundarakāṇḍa, Yuddhakāṇḍa); Tulsīdās’s Rāmacaritamānasa; later folk narratives. His strength is framed as subordinate to dharma and Rāma’s will; he does not act independently for personal gain. In some narratives, he initially forgets his own power until reminded by Jāmbavān, suggesting psychological rather than metaphysical limitation. No major contradictions on strength itself, though different texts emphasize either his martial prowess or his self-effacing humility, creating tension between heroism and servitude.

Flight and prodigious leaping Hanuman’s leap across the ocean to Laṅkā is one of his most iconic feats, often interpreted as flight or superhuman jumping. As a child, he flies toward the sun, mistaking it for a fruit, demonstrating innate aerial mobility. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sundarakāṇḍa); Rāmacaritamānasa; EBSCO Research Starter summary. His leap is preceded by encouragement from allies, implying that confidence and remembrance of his boons are necessary. Ocean-crossing is presented as exceptional, not routine travel. Some retellings treat his movement as literal flight; others emphasize a single, miraculous jump. These are interpretive differences rather than explicit textual contradictions.

Shape-shifting and size alteration Hanuman can change his size at will—expanding to gigantic proportions to intimidate enemies or shrinking to a tiny form to infiltrate Laṅkā and approach Sītā discreetly. This ability is central to his role as scout and warrior. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sundarakāṇḍa episodes of entering Laṅkā and confronting demons); later vernacular Ramayana traditions. Size change is used strategically, not continuously; texts do not describe fixed limits, but his transformations are always in service of Rāma’s mission. No major contradictions, though some regional performances exaggerate his gigantism or miniaturization beyond textual descriptions.

Devotional immunity and protection Hanuman is often believed to grant protection from fear, evil spirits, and planetary afflictions to those who recite his hymns, especially the Hanumān Cālīsā. His own body is described as resistant to weapons and fire, surviving the burning of Laṅkā. Rāmacaritamānasa; later Hindi devotional literature; Wolcott’s ethnographic account of Bhojpuri Hanuman worship. Protection is conditional on sincere devotion and disciplined practice (e.g., regular recitation, ritual purity). Texts do not present him as overriding all karmic consequences. Scholarly accounts note variation in the specific benefits attributed to his worship (e.g., protection from ghosts vs. success in exams), reflecting evolving folk belief rather than textual dispute.

Knowledge, eloquence, and diplomacy Hanuman is praised for his wise speech and ability to assess situations, particularly in his respectful yet firm dialogue with Sītā and his confrontations with Rāvaṇa. He serves as messenger and negotiator as much as warrior. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa; Ludvik’s comparative analysis; Rāmacaritamānasa. His counsel is always framed within loyalty to Rāma; he does not seek independent political power. Some modern popular retellings simplify him into a purely brawny figure, downplaying his intellectual and diplomatic dimensions, creating a tension between scholarly and popular images.

Burning of Laṅkā After being captured and having his tail set on fire, Hanuman enlarges it and uses the flames to burn much of Laṅkā, demonstrating both resilience and destructive capacity. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sundarakāṇḍa); Rāmacaritamānasa; Ludvik’s analysis. The act is framed as punitive and strategic, not wanton destruction; he spares certain areas according to some versions, though details vary. Later devotional retellings sometimes amplify or minimize the scale of destruction; exact extent of the burning differs across versions.

Immortality (cirañjīvī status) Purāṇic traditions and popular belief regard Hanuman as one of the seven or eight immortals who continue to live in the world, available to devotees. Purāṇic references (exact loci vary); EBSCO Research Starter summary; Lutgendorf’s discussion of contemporary belief. Immortality is spiritual and mythic; texts do not describe a continuous visible presence. It functions primarily as theological assurance of ongoing protection. Lists of cirañjīvīs differ among sources; Hanuman’s inclusion is widely accepted but not uniform across all textual traditions.

Behavior & Habitat

Across textual and folk traditions, Hanuman’s defining behavioral trait is unwavering bhakti (devotion) to Rāma, expressed through humility, service, and readiness for self-sacrifice. In the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa he is a shrewd diplomat and scout, capable of careful speech and strategic thinking; in Tulsīdās’s Rāmacaritamānasa he becomes the paradigmatic devotee whose every action is an offering. Folk narratives and modern retellings highlight his playful, childlike curiosity (such as mistaking the sun for a fruit) alongside fierce martial prowess in battle. He is often invoked as a protector against fear, illness, and malevolent forces, and his worship emphasizes discipline, courage, and moral steadfastness.

Accounts

Hanuman is born to Añjanā, a celestial being cursed to take monkey form, and either the monkey warrior Keśarī or the wind god Vāyu. In some accounts, Vāyu carries divine essence to Añjanā, making Hanuman his spiritual son. His birth is accompanied by boons of strength, agility, and devotion, setting the stage for his role in Rāma’s story.

As a child, Hanuman, insatiably hungry, mistakes the rising sun for a giant fruit and leaps into the sky to seize it. The gods panic; Indra strikes him with the thunderbolt, injuring his jaw. Vāyu, enraged, withdraws the wind from the world, causing cosmic distress. The gods then grant Hanuman boons of strength and protection, restoring balance.

Hanuman, serving Sugrīva, encounters Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa in the forest. Disguised as a mendicant, he tests their intentions, then recognizes their divine nature. His eloquent speech wins their trust, and he becomes Rāma’s devoted servant and ally.

Encouraged by Jāmbavān, Hanuman remembers his latent powers and leaps across the ocean to Laṅkā. Shrinking his form, he infiltrates the city, finds Sītā in the Aśoka grove, and delivers Rāma’s message and ring. He reassures her of rescue and offers to carry her away, but she refuses, insisting that Rāma must personally defeat Rāvaṇa.

Captured by Rāvaṇa’s forces, Hanuman’s tail is set ablaze as punishment. He enlarges it, escapes, and uses the fire to burn much of Laṅkā’s buildings, sowing chaos before returning to Rāma with news of Sītā.

When Lakṣmaṇa is gravely wounded in battle, Hanuman is sent to fetch specific medicinal herbs from a distant mountain. Unable to identify them, he uproots and carries the entire mountain back, allowing the healers to find the needed plants and save Lakṣmaṇa.

Bhīma, seeking a special flower for Draupadī, encounters an old monkey blocking his path. When he tries to move the monkey’s tail, he cannot, realizing the being’s immense strength. The monkey reveals himself as Hanuman, Bhīma’s half-brother through Vāyu, and blesses him.

A way to hold it in your mind

If Rāma is the ideal king and Sītā the ideal queen, Hanuman is the ideal fan—except his fandom is so intense it becomes theology. He’s the bridge between gods and humans, between epic and everyday life: a liminal figure who shows that the greatest power isn’t just muscle or magic, but the willingness to throw all of it behind something—or someone—you love without holding anything back.
